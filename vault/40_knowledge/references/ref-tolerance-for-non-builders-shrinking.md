In a recent interview with Lenny Rachitsky, Nikhyl Singhal (former VP at Meta and Google) predicted that half of product managers are about to be replaced, not because of performance but because their role was never about building in the first place. Singhal projects 30,000 legacy administrative PMs giving way to 8,000 AI-fluent builders. The role isn’t shrinking. The tolerance for non-builders is.

The PMs Singhal is talking about are mid- to senior-level in their careers. They built credibility on bureaucracy, layers of planning meetings, roadmap theater, and slide decks nobody acts on. Some of that work is gone. Some have shifted: stakeholder management has become stakeholder engagement, downstream of building rather than upstream; resource allocation has become data-driven prioritization; alignment meetings still happen, but they follow a build instead of gating it. What’s emerging in its place is a model in which everyone in the org is a potential builder, supported by agent portfolio governance and the kind of judgment work that the coordination roles never required. The higher you sat in the old org, the more of your day was the first list and the less of it was the second.

I started using ChatGPT to draft performance reviews in late 2022, when most enterprises had told staff not to use generative AI for anything work-related. At this point in my career, I was a senior director with multiple managers and PMs reporting to me. At this level, performance reviews tended to be complex and often took me ten to fourteen days to complete. After building an AI process, I was able to reduce that effort to roughly 45 minutes, and it became a model example at the firm. By 2024, I was stitching NotebookLM, ChatGPT, and Grok together to draft six-figure proposals in minutes. I have been using LLMs and agents fully in my business since 2024 and writing publicly about coding agents in Claude Code for Non-Coders since the summer of 2025, now with 29,000+ subscribers. None of that required permission. All of it required a willingness to act before the playbook caught up.

This isn’t a recent disposition. In the early 2000s at the Federal Reserve Board, I prototyped a secure platform for banks to submit compliance filings online, replacing the fax-based process everyone had accepted. Nobody assigned it to me. When my managers found out the thing existed and worked, they reassigned me to it as my full-time job. Years later, at Booz Allen, I built a social network analysis tool on my own time to visualize connections across an enterprise through email, phone records, and organizational data. The executives learned what it could do, and we turned it into a client service offering. The mindset hasn’t changed. The cost of acting on it just collapsed.

## The Cost of Acting Without Permission

The “permission” most people think they need at work is rarely about authority. It’s about resources. Building something took scarce inputs: your own time, someone else’s bandwidth, compute, the organization’s staffing decisions, and weeks or months of calendar. The gatekeepers weren’t really protecting authority. They were allocating scarce inputs. Forgiveness over permission worked when it did because a few people were willing to put in their own input on bets the organization wouldn’t have funded upfront. Those inputs were expensive, and the mindset was rare, which is why it was also an edge.

AI collapsed the inputs. A prototype that used to take three sprints takes a Saturday afternoon. Research that used to take a week takes twenty minutes. The first draft of something complex takes one prompt and a little taste. The disposition that used to pose a real career risk for people like me to act on now costs almost nothing to try. The reasonable assumption was that once the cost came off, the mindset would spread fast. It hasn’t. Pew Research’s tracking shows that most US adults have still never used ChatGPT regularly. Anthropic’s Economic Index shows sustained use of frontier models concentrated in a narrow slice of the workforce. Paid-tier subscribers across all frontier providers are a fraction of the professionals who could benefit. Agent harness users are a fraction of that fraction.

## What’s Gone, What Survived, What Emerged

Singhal’s framing extends beyond product management because the same shift is operating across most senior knowledge work. The upstream work of bureaucracy, planning theater, and gatekeeping existed to allocate scarce inputs before a build began. AI made those inputs cheap, which made the gatekeeping work redundant. What’s left is the downstream work of shipping something that creates value, plus the portfolio-level judgment of which builds are worth running. The model that’s emerging treats everyone in the org as a potential builder, with agent portfolio governance and data-driven prioritization as the supporting disciplines. None of these were on the org chart five years ago. They’re the work mid-to-senior people are increasingly hired against now.

I have watched this play out at large enterprises for years. Most people carrying the PM title are glorified project managers, running alignment meetings, presenting roadmaps, and translating between teams that should be talking directly. The shipping happens in spite of them, not because of them. Organizations have long incentivized exactly this: the PM who presents well and aligns the most stakeholders gets promoted; the builder who ships quietly gets overlooked. AI won't fix that culture. It is going to expose it.

Many organizations don’t actually want builder PMs. They want presenters. They want alignment theater. The builder PM gets penalized for being too technical or insufficiently strategic. Changing individual behavior without changing what gets rewarded at the org level is a half-solution, and it’s the harder problem most leaders haven’t fully reckoned with yet.

## What Executives Are Missing

MIT NANDA’s [State of AI in Business 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/08/18/mit-report-95-percent-generative-ai-pilots-at-companies-failing-cfo/) found that roughly 95% of enterprise GenAI pilots delivered no measurable P&L impact after $30-40 billion in spend. The report attributes most of the shortfall to a learning gap in the systems themselves. The diagnosis is real and incomplete. The organizations running these pilots were designed for upfront planning and alignment, and AI moved that coordination downstream into the same builder-versus-coordinator gap Singhal is describing. Non-technical people gained execution capability they didn’t have before. Technical people gained the ability to skip the coordination they once needed. Both shifts pushed bureaucracy from before you build to a thin layer of coordination after you build, and most organizations were designed around the old model. There’s no downstream coordination machinery to catch five incompatible tools shipped by five non-technical teams, or five orphan prototypes shipped by five engineers who each figured they’d demo their way into buy-in.

The leaders who do understand this aren’t trying to put the mindset back in the bottle. They’re building the downstream coordination structure around the new way of working, and they’re discovering that the adoption gap within their own workforce is a bigger constraint than the technology itself.

## Domain Judgment Is the Second Layer

Inside the small group that does adopt frontier models with depth, the next scarce skill is domain judgment. Now that Claude, GPT, Gemini, and a few large open-weight competitors are genuinely good, content and style are commodities. What separates useful output from noise is human judgment in the context of the domain. Regulated industries have rails that exist because a federal agency, a medical board, or an external auditor will eventually show up. Fiduciary roles have rails because you’re managing someone else’s money or risk. When an AI-generated solution lands in those environments, the work isn’t deciding whether the output is clever. It’s knowing which rails it crosses, and which of those will matter when the audit arrives. That judgment doesn’t come from the agent’s corpus. It comes from having sat in the room when a regulator asked the question, or from having carried the liability for the answer.

This is part of why software engineers are starting to get hired back. Forrester’s [Predictions 2026: The Future of Work](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/future-of-work-predictions-2026-whats-coming-for-work-and-the-workforce/) found that 55% of employers regret laying off workers for AI-related reasons, and projected that roughly half of AI-attributed layoffs will be reversed, often at offshore rates or reduced pay. The companies that cut too deep are discovering they no longer have the in-house judgment to recognize when an AI-generated solution is about to cross a line that could lead to an audit finding, a data breach, or a class action. During a recent client engagement, the agent I was working with issued a strong recommendation based on all available data. On its face, it was good. I reframed it before we took it into the room, because I knew something about the client that wasn’t in the data: a history I’d carried forward from earlier work, and a read on what the decision-makers were actually responding to that no retrieval system was going to surface. We won the contract. Good judgment requires domain experience, and experience is the one input AI has not made cheaper.

## The Question on the Table

The frontier tools are cheap. The agent harnesses are documented. Free resources for learning are abundant. There are no more barriers, especially if you’re mid-level or senior. There are also no more excuses. The tolerance for non-builders is shrinking, and the higher your title, the more visibly you sit on the wrong side of that line if you’re not building.

The question is not whether to act. The question is whether your organization is structured to reward the people who build, or the ones who talk about building. If it’s the latter, your individual reinvention will outpace the org’s, and you will find yourself either pulling the org along or finding a place that already values the work. The work that carried you into mid-career is not the work that will carry you forward. The replacement is the willingness to be a beginner again, in public, at the cost of frontier tools. To spend your own time on something that may not work, with tools that didn’t exist a year ago, in pursuit of an output you can ship without permission.
