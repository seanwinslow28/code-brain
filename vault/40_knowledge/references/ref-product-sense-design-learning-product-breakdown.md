**Question:** Design a product that changes how people learn.

**Section 0: Thought Process (What You’re Doing Before You Speak)**

This question is a gift and a trap at the same time. The gift is that it’s wide open, which means you control the narrative entirely. The trap is that width makes candidates waffle into vagueness:

“learning is a big space,” “there are many users,” “AI could help in lots of ways.”

The move is to pick a frame immediately and own it. Before you say a word, you need to have locked in four things privately:

**1\. The company.** Open-ended design questions require you to pick a company. You’re not designing in the abstract; you’re designing as a PM at a specific company with specific assets, a specific

mission, and a specific business model. Pick a company whose platform capabilities give your AI solution built-in credibility. Google is ideal here because it has Gemini, YouTube (the world’s largest

informal learning platform), Google Classroom, Google Search, and a mission that directly maps to knowledge access.

**2\. The ecosystem player.** Learning products serve multiple stakeholders: learners, educators, institutions, employers, content creators. You need to pick one and defend why in one crisp sentence.

Learners are the obvious choice. Within learners, self-directed adult professionals are the right cut because they have a high-stakes outcome, clear motivation, and are deeply underserved by existing

tools.

**3\. The problem worth solving.** Most existing learning products solved access (Coursera) and pacing (Duolingo) but not one thing: the gap between what someone studies and whether it actually prepares

them for a specific real-world outcome. That is the problem. It is high frequency and high severity for the segment you’re going to pick.

**4\. The AI angle.** Every solution in an AI product sense question needs a genuine AI mechanism, not “use AI to personalize.” The specific AI bet here is Socratic dialogue as the primary teaching

modality. Instead of content consumption, the product teaches through questioning and adapts to the learner’s actual reasoning gaps in real time. This is differentiated, non-obvious, and technically

achievable today with LLMs.

Lock those four before you speak. Then open with assumptions and your plan.

\---

**Section 1: Coaching Notes**

Before reading the spoken answer, here is what makes a 10/10 response to this question specifically, and where most candidates fall short.

**What separates a 10/10 from a 6/10:**

The 6/10 answer describes a product with features. It has decent structure: segments, problems, solutions. It is forgettable because it could apply to any PM at any company on any day.

The 10/10 answer has a through line. Every section connects to every other section. The mission statement you write in Product Motivation becomes the filter you use to prioritize segments, problems,

and solutions. The problem you identify has exactly one root cause. The solution you pick addresses that root cause with specificity. The v1 is de-scoped to the minimum that tests the riskiest

assumption.

**The five things interviewers are actually scoring:**

1\. Do you understand why the current category exists and what gap it leaves?

2\. Can you identify a non-obvious segment with a real, specific pain?

3\. Do your solutions feel like they came from someone who has built AI products, not someone who has read about them?

4\. Can you commit to one recommendation and defend it with trade-off reasoning?

5\. Does your answer ladder back to a business outcome, not just a user outcome?

**The specific failure modes for this question:**

\- Picking “students” as the segment (too broad, too contested by incumbents)

\- Proposing “adaptive learning paths” as the primary innovation (Duolingo and Khan Academy already do this)

\- Using “AI will personalize the content” without specifying what the AI actually does technically

\- Forgetting to connect the product to Google’s business model (ad revenue, cloud, Workspace)

\- Not proposing a North Star Metric before going into solutions

**Timing:** Assumptions + Plan (1 min), Product Motivation (4 min), Ecosystem + Segmentation (8 min), Problem Identification (7 min), Solution Development (8 min), v1 (4 min), close (2 min). Total: 34 min.

**Section 2: Spoken Version**

Below is what you would actually say, section by section. Read these as spoken language, not essay prose. Short sentences. Practice natural pauses built in.

\---

**Opening: Assumptions and Plan**

*What to say:*

“Before I dive in, I want to state a few assumptions upfront, and then share how I plan to structure my time. Does that work for you?

First, I’m going to assume I’m a PM at Google, specifically working on a new product in the learning and education space. Google has strong assets here: Gemini for AI, YouTube as the world’s largest informal learning platform, Google Classroom for institutional reach, and a mission around making information universally useful. I’ll design within that context.

Second, I’ll focus on English-speaking markets to start, with the US as primary.

Third, I’ll assume we are designing a net-new product, not improving an existing one.

Here is how I plan to use our time together. I’ll start with why this product space matters and define a placeholder mission. Then I’ll identify the key players in the learning ecosystem and pick one segment to go deep on. From there, I’ll walk through the user journey for that segment, identify the most important problem, and brainstorm solutions. If we have time, I’d love to describe a concrete v1. Does this plan work for you?”

\---

*Coaching note for this section:* The 30 to 45 seconds you spend here sets the entire tone. You are showing the interviewer that you think before you act. The company choice matters: Google gives you

AI credibility, distribution, and a mission that directly connects to learning. State your assumptions assertively, not as questions. “I am going to assume” is stronger than “Can I assume?”

\---

**Product Motivation**

*What* *to* *say:*

“Let me take a minute to describe the product space and why it matters.

Learning is one of the most fundamental human activities, and it is also deeply broken. We have more content than at any point in history: YouTube has 800 million videos, Coursera has 7,000 courses, Khan Academy covers every school subject for free. Access to knowledge is essentially solved.

What is not solved is conversion. The gap between consuming information and actually being able to use it is enormous. Studies consistently show that traditional passive learning, watching a lecture, reading a textbook, has a retention rate below 10 percent after 30 days. People are learning more than ever and retaining less than ever.

The reason is that most learning products optimize for content delivery, not for understanding. They treat the learner as a container to fill, not a mind to develop.

Google is uniquely positioned to change this. It already has the world’s knowledge indexed. It has Gemini for dialogue. It has YouTube for media-rich content. And with Google Classroom, it has a foot in institutional distribution. No other company has all of these assets simultaneously.

So the product I want to design is an AI tutor that teaches through dialogue rather than content. Not a course platform. Not a quiz app. A thinking partner that helps you understand, not just know.

My placeholder mission statement: Help anyone master anything faster by making every learning moment feel like it was designed just for them.

I’ll carry this mission into every decision I make from here.”

\---

*Coaching note for this section:* The strongest product motivation answers do three things. They name the category’s existing solution and its fundamental flaw. They explain why the company in question is uniquely positioned to solve it. And they close with a mission statement specific enough to guide decisions but broad enough to allow creativity. Notice the phrase “not a course platform, not a quiz app.” Saying what you are NOT is as important as saying what you ARE. It signals that you understand the competitive landscape and are making a deliberate choice.

\---

**Ecosystem** **Players** **and** **Segment** **Selection**

*What* *to* *say:*

“Let me now identify the key players in the learning ecosystem before I narrow down.

The major stakeholders are: learners themselves, educators and content creators, employers who have an interest in workforce skills, academic institutions, and parents for younger learners.

I want to focus on learners, and specifically on a sub-group within learners that I think is the highest-leverage opportunity.

Before I do, let me propose a North Star metric that will guide my solution thinking. I want to come back to this, but I’ll plant the flag now: my North Star is weekly sessions where the AI meaningfully changed what the user studied next. Not time spent. Not lessons completed. Moments where the system genuinely adapted to close a real gap in understanding.

Now, within learners, I want to segment by primary motivation. There are three meaningful groups:

First, credentialed students: people studying for formal degrees or certifications. High reach, but heavily served by existing platforms like Coursera and Udemy. Medium underserved degree.

Second, curiosity-driven lifelong learners: people exploring topics out of personal interest. Medium reach, and reasonably served by YouTube and podcast content. Medium underserved degree.

Third, career transitioners: people actively trying to learn new skills to change roles or industries. Think the marketing manager trying to become a product manager, or the software engineer trying to move into machine learning. High reach globally, tens of millions of people. And critically, highly underserved.

I want to focus on career transitioners. Here is why. They have a specific, measurable outcome: a job. They are highly motivated and willing to invest time and money. But existing platforms fail them in a precise way: the content is not mapped to their specific goal. They don’t know if what they’re studying will actually prepare them for the role they want. They feel like they’re working hard but can’t tell if they’re moving.

Let me bring this segment to life with a persona.

Meet Sara. She is 32, a marketing manager at a mid-size company, and she has decided she wants to move into product management. She studies for 45 minutes most evenings after putting her kids to bed. She has enrolled in three Udemy courses and read two PM books. But every morning she wakes up and wonders the same thing: am I learning the right things? And am I actually getting better, or just accumulating information?

Sara is who I am designing for.”

\---

*Coaching note for this section:* Two things to notice here. First, I proposed the North Star Metric early, right after product motivation, not at the end. Interviewers at companies like Meta and DoorDash want to see you anchor your thinking around a metric before you start generating solutions. Second, the persona work is doing real job here. “Sara” is not decoration. She becomes the lens through which I evaluate every problem and every solution. The question I will ask about every decision from here is: does this help Sara?

\---

**Problem** **Identification**

*What* *to* *say:*

“Now let me walk through Sara’s journey and identify where it breaks down.

She starts by deciding she wants to become a PM. She searches YouTube and Google for ‘how to become a product manager.’ She gets flooded with results: courses, bootcamps, blogs, Reddit threads. No signal about what’s actually right for her situation. She picks a course based on reviews. She enrolls, starts watching lectures. The lectures are well-produced. She feels like she is learning. She takes notes.

She tries to apply what she learned to a mock case study. She realizes she does not really understand the concept she just watched a 20-minute video about. She re-watches it. It still does not quite click.

Weeks pass. She finishes a course. She starts another one. She has no way to tell how close she is to being ready. She applies to a PM role, gets rejected after a case interview, and is not sure what

she needs to improve.

Along the journey I see three problems.

Problem one: she cannot tell what to learn. The space is overwhelming and there is no connection between her specific situation, her background, the roles she is targeting, and the content she should

prioritize. This is high frequency and high severity.

Problem two: passive consumption does not stick. Watching creates the feeling of learning without the reality of it. She can recognize concepts after watching a lecture but cannot generate them under

pressure. High frequency, medium severity.

Problem three: no feedback on reasoning gaps. When she gets something wrong in an exercise, she sees a right answer but not why her thinking went wrong. Medium frequency, high severity.

I want to prioritize problem one. Here is why. If she is studying the wrong things, no amount of content quality or retention improvement matters. The root cause is that the product has no model of her. It treats her as a generic learner rather than a specific person with a specific goal. Every other problem downstream depends on solving this one first.

And this is the problem that most directly connects to my mission statement: making every learning moment feel like it was designed just for her. Right now, none of it feels designed for her at all.”

\---

*Coaching note for this section:* The user journey should be specific, not generic. “She starts, then she learns, then she finishes” is not a journey. It is a stage diagram. Walk through what someone actually does, moment by moment, including the friction they feel emotionally. Notice I named the emotional state: “she wakes up every morning wondering if she is learning the right things.” That is cognitive empathy, not just behavioral description. The problem prioritization logic is also explicit: I named the root cause and connected it back to the mission. A common failure here is picking the most emotionally compelling problem rather than the highest-leverage one.

\---

**Solution** **Development**

*What* *to* *say:*

“Now let me brainstorm three solutions to this problem.

Solution one: AI Job-Outcome Mapper. This is the obvious solution. We analyze thousands of PM job postings, extract the skill requirements, and map Sara’s current learning to a readiness score for her target roles. She sees something like: ‘You are 61 percent ready for a PM role at a Series B startup. Your biggest gaps are SQL and stakeholder management.’ Impact is high. Effort is medium. The risk is that job postings are noisy signals, and this could create anxiety rather than clarity.

Solution two: Socratic AI Tutor. This is my preferred solution, and I will explain why in a moment. Instead of delivering content for Sara to consume, the AI teaches by asking. It starts by asking Sara what she thinks, surfaces the gaps in her reasoning, and then introduces the concept in response to her own confusion. This is how expert human tutors actually work. The AI does not lecture. It interrogates, in the best sense of the word. Impact is very high. Effort is high, but Google’s Gemini infrastructure makes this feasible.

Solution three: AI-Facilitated Peer Learning Cohorts. Match Sara with five people at the same stage, with similar backgrounds and similar targets. The AI facilitates a weekly case discussion, assigns collaborative exercises, and provides group feedback. Impact is medium. Effort is low. But it depends on network effects and is harder to personalize at the individual level.

I want to recommend Solution two, the Socratic AI Tutor, for three reasons.

First, it addresses the root cause directly. Sara does not know what she does not know. The Socratic method surfaces her actual reasoning gaps, not the gaps she self-reports. The AI builds a model of her mind through dialogue, which is the only reliable way to understand what she has actually internalized.

Second, it is differentiated. Every competitor offers content plus quizzes. Some offer adaptive pacing. None offer teaching through questioning as the primary modality. This is the kind of solution that would not ship from a content company because it requires re-architecting the product from a consumption model to a conversation model.

Third, it connects to Google’s existing infrastructure. Gemini already handles multi-turn reasoning conversations. YouTube’s content library becomes the reference layer the AI draws from. This is not a greenfield build. It is Gemini plus Google’s knowledge assets, directed at a specific learning context.

The trade-off I am making: this is harder to build than Solution one, and it requires significant investment in conversation quality. If the AI reasons poorly, it teaches poorly, and the damage to trust is hard to reverse. I would mitigate this by using RAG over verified curriculum content so the AI’s claims are grounded and citable.”

\---

*Coaching note for this section:* Notice the three solutions follow a pattern: one obvious/expected option (the job mapper), one recommended option (the Socratic tutor), and one that is plausible but de-prioritized with clear reasoning (peer cohorts). The recommendation is defended with three specific reasons, not just “it’s the best.” And the trade-off is named explicitly.

Interviewers are not looking for certainty; they are looking for judgment. Naming the downside of your recommendation and how you would mitigate it signals maturity. The AI mechanism is also specific: “Gemini handles multi-turn reasoning, YouTube is the reference layer, RAG grounds the claims.” That specificity is what separates a candidate who has built AI products from one who has just read about them.

\---

**v1 Definition**

*What to say:*

“If we had time to ship a v1, here is how I would de-scope it.

What is it? A Socratic onboarding session. When Priya signs up, instead of asking her to browse a course catalog, the product opens a conversation. The AI says: ‘Tell me what you are trying to learn and why. I am going to ask you a few questions to understand where you are starting from.’ Over ten minutes, it asks her targeted questions, surfaces what she does and does not know, and generates a personalized learning path based on her actual gaps, not her self-assessment.

Who sees it? New users in the first session only. We are not rebuilding the full product; we are replacing the onboarding experience.

What does it do? It creates a model of the learner before any content is delivered. Instead of the generic ‘welcome, here are your recommended courses,’ Priya sees: ‘Based on our conversation, you have strong instincts around user empathy but gaps in defining success metrics. Here is where we start.’

How does it solve the problem? It immediately connects what she is about to learn to why she is there. The feeling of ‘am I studying the right things’ is addressed in the first ten minutes, before she has consumed a single piece of content.

Two risks I would monitor. First, users may feel uncomfortable with a ten-minute dialogue before seeing any content. Mitigate by being transparent about what the AI is doing and why, and offering a ‘skip and browse’ option for users who prefer the traditional experience. Second, LLM reasoning errors in a teaching context erode trust permanently. Mitigate with citations for every factual claim and a human-review layer for any content the AI generates during the first 90 days.”

*Coaching* *note* *for* *this* *section:* The v1 is not a roadmap. It is not a PRD. It is four sentences that answer four specific questions: what is it, who sees it, what does it do, and how does it solve the problem. The key discipline here is de-scoping ruthlessly. The full Socratic tutor is a massive build. The v1 is one conversation at onboarding. That is testable, shippable, and directly de-risks the most uncertain assumption: that users are willing to teach the AI about themselves before consuming content.

\---

**Close**

*What* *to* *say:*

“To summarize: I designed an AI-powered Socratic tutor built on Google’s infrastructure, targeting career transitioners like Priya who are motivated but lack a system that understands them as individuals. The core problem I chose to solve is not content quality or access. It is the absence of a model of the learner. The v1 tests that thesis in ten minutes at onboarding, before we commit to rebuilding the full product experience.

My North Star: weekly sessions where the AI meaningfully adapted what the user studied next. I would track that alongside time-to-first-aha-moment and 30-day retention, with a guardrail on conversation quality scores to catch model errors early.

Happy to go deeper on any section, or discuss how this connects to Google’s broader business model.”

\---

*Coaching* *note* *for* *this* *section:* The close serves two purposes. It reminds the interviewer of the through line: segment, problem, solution, metric, all connected. And it signals that you have more to give if they want to probe. The

phrase “happy to go deeper” is an invitation, not a filler. If you have done the work, say it with confidence. If your through line is tight, the close almost writes itself.
