# Innovation Strategy — Quick Reference

Read this file once for the strategic mindset, the disruption-vs-incrementalism distinction, and the move-on criteria for each phase.

---

## The Mindset

> **Markets reward genuine new value — not dressed-up incrementalism.**
>
> **Innovation without business-model thinking is theater.**
>
> **Incremental thinking is how category leaders become footnotes.**

Strategy is about deliberately choosing what *not* to do. Brutal truth about market realities precedes innovation exploration. Comfortable consensus produces footnotes; clarity produces moves.

The eight phases progress through three movements:

```
See the landscape (1–2) → Find the opening (3–5) → Commit to the move (6–8)
```

---

## Phase 1 — Strategic Question

**Goal:** Establish the strategic framing — what business, what challenge, what success looks like.

**Why it matters:** Most "strategy work" fails because it's tactical work in disguise. "How do we beat competitor X?" is tactical. "Where are the disruption opportunities in our market?" is strategic.

**What you produce:** Company / context, strategic focus, current situation, the actual strategic challenge as a single question.

**Fail modes:**
- Strategic question is actually a feature question. ("What features should we ship?" — not strategy.)
- Strategic question is actually a competitive question. ("How do we beat X?" — not strategy, that's a tactical sidebar.)
- No definition of breakthrough success.

**Move on when:** The strategic question would survive being read aloud at a board meeting without anyone asking "wait, can you re-state that?"

---

## Phase 2 — Market Analysis & Forces

**Goal:** Unflinching clarity about market realities. See it before you bet.

**Why it matters:** Strategy built on a misread of the market is strategy that produces confident failure.

**What you produce:** Market segments, competitive landscape, substitutes (including non-obvious ones), changing forces, where customers are underserved or overserved.

**Choose frameworks** from the **Market Analysis** category in `innovation-frameworks.md`:
- Industry structure questions → Five Forces
- Market sizing → TAM/SAM/SOM
- Macro forces → PESTLE
- Innovation timing → Market Timing Assessment
- Differentiation analysis → Competitive Positioning Map

**Fail modes:**
- Treating the obvious competitors as *the* competitors. Substitutes and non-consumption are usually the bigger threats.
- Assuming current market structure will hold. The interesting strategy lives in what's *changing*.
- Confusing market presence with market opportunity. Big markets aren't automatically the right markets.

**Move on when:** You can name the three forces most likely to redefine the market in 24 months and where customers are systematically underserved.

---

## Phase 3 — Jobs to Be Done

**Goal:** Discover what customers are *actually* hiring solutions to do — functional, emotional, social — separate from the features they currently buy.

**Why it matters:** JTBD reveals unmet needs the entire feature-driven competitive set is missing. It's the cheapest path to a category-redefining insight.

**What you produce:** Jobs (functional, emotional, social), pains, gains, alternatives currently used (including non-purchase), conditions that would cause customers to fire the existing solution.

**Fail modes:**
- Confusing jobs with features. "They want a faster checkout" is a feature; "they want to feel competent and not waste time" is a job.
- Forgetting non-consumption. The customer who isn't buying anything tells you more than the customer already buying yours.
- Stopping at the functional job. Emotional and social jobs usually carry the higher willingness to pay.

**Move on when:** You can describe the customer's job in language they would recognize and you can name the alternatives — including the "do nothing" alternative.

---

## Phase 4 — Blue Ocean / Uncontested Space

**Goal:** Find or design a market position where competition is structurally less relevant.

**Why it matters:** Sustained competitive advantage is rare in red oceans. Blue oceans aren't free, but they reward the moves better.

**What you produce:** What to **eliminate** (industry takes for granted, customer doesn't actually value), **reduce** (below industry standard), **raise** (above standard), **create** (industry has never offered). Plus uncontested-market vectors.

**Fail modes:**
- Naming a "blue ocean" that's actually a small red ocean. If competitors will follow you in within 12 months, it's not blue.
- Eliminating things customers actually do value. The Eliminate move is the riskiest — pressure-test it.
- Creating new factors that no one wants. Just because a factor is new doesn't make it valuable.

**Move on when:** You can articulate the value innovation in a single sentence that names what's reduced/eliminated *and* what's raised/created — and a customer would recognize the trade.

---

## Phase 5 — Business Model Innovation

**Goal:** Design how value is created, delivered, and captured — not just what gets built.

**Why it matters:** Most "innovation" stops at product. The biggest strategic moves are model moves: subscription instead of license, two-sided market instead of linear, free + paid instead of paid-only.

**What you produce:** Business Model Canvas (or partial), value proposition, revenue and cost structure, model weaknesses, candidate model innovations.

**Choose frameworks** from the **Business Model** category:
- Comprehensive mapping → Business Model Canvas
- Product-market fit at unit level → Value Proposition Canvas
- Borrow proven patterns → Business Model Patterns
- Monetization redesign → Revenue Model Innovation
- Margin redesign → Cost Structure Innovation

**Fail modes:**
- Designing a clever model that requires capabilities the company doesn't have and can't build.
- Underestimating channel economics. The model that works for the founder's first 10 customers usually breaks at 100.
- Ignoring how incumbents would respond. A model that works *if competitors stay still* is a fragile model.

**Move on when:** You can describe how value is created, delivered, and captured in one paragraph — and you can name what would have to be true for the model to scale.

---

## Phase 6 — Competitive Advantage / Moats

**Goal:** Identify the structural moats that defend the strategy from copycat erosion.

**Why it matters:** Innovation without defensibility is a gift to competitors who scale faster than you do. Moats convert tactical wins into sustained value capture.

**What you produce:** Identified moats (network effects, scale economies, switching costs, brand/trust, IP, data feedback loops, regulatory advantage, embedded distribution), the strength of each, what would erode each.

**Fail modes:**
- Calling things moats that aren't. "Best UX" is rarely a moat. "Better team" is rarely a moat. Network effects, scale, lock-in, regulation — those are moats.
- Counting on a single moat. Most durable strategies have layered moats.
- Ignoring how moats erode under technological change. AI is reshaping what counts as defensible right now; pressure-test legacy moat assumptions.

**Move on when:** You can name at least two structural advantages and articulate what would have to change in the world for them to fail.

---

## Phase 7 — Strategic Roadmap (Three Phases)

**Goal:** Sequence the strategy as a roadmap with phase-appropriate ambition.

**Why it matters:** Strategy that doesn't sequence is strategy that doesn't ship. The right sequence is also a hedge — early phases validate the assumptions later phases bet on.

**What you produce:**
- **Phase 1 — Immediate Impact:** quick wins, hypothesis validation, initial momentum
- **Phase 2 — Foundation Building:** capability development, market entry, systematic growth
- **Phase 3 — Scale & Optimization:** market expansion, efficiency, competitive positioning

For each: key initiatives, deliverables, resource needs, success metrics, decision gates.

**Fail modes:**
- Phase 1 that's actually three years of work pretending to be quick wins.
- Phase 3 that assumes Phase 1 worked exactly as planned. The roadmap is a hypothesis, not a script.
- No decision gates. Without gates, the team will execute Phase 2 even when Phase 1 invalidated the strategy.

**Move on when:** Each phase has a clear decision gate that could send you back to a previous phase or pivot the strategy outright.

---

## Phase 8 — Assumption Validation Plan

**Goal:** Make the strategy's hidden bets explicit and define how we'd know they're wrong.

**Why it matters:** Strategy is a stack of assumptions. Naming them is the difference between confident execution and confident failure.

**What you produce:**
- **Leading indicators** — early signals of strategy working (engagement, adoption, efficiency)
- **Lagging indicators** — business outcomes (revenue, market share, profitability)
- **Key hypotheses** — the bets the strategy depends on
- **Validation plan** — how each hypothesis gets tested, in what sequence
- **Risk mitigation** — top risks with detection and response
- **Decision gates / triggers** — explicit "if X then pivot to Y" rules

**Fail modes:**
- Naming only outcome metrics. By the time the lagging indicator moves, it's usually too late to course-correct.
- "Validation" that's actually confirmation-seeking. Real validation is *trying to prove the strategy wrong* and seeing whether it survives.
- No abandon trigger. Some strategies need a kill switch the team commits to in advance.

**Move on when:** A different team could read the validation plan and know what to do at each gate without further input.

## When This Workflow Fits — and When It Doesn't

**Fits:**
- New market opportunities or major pivots
- Competitive threat assessment and disruption potential
- New business model design
- Sustainable competitive advantage hunting
- Pressure-testing whether an idea has strategic merit (vs. tactical merit)

**Doesn't fit:**
- Incremental feature improvements (use `sw-creative-toolkit:brainstorm` instead)
- Pure technical optimization (use `sw-creative-toolkit:problem-solving`)
- Well-established markets with clear, stable rules
- Time-critical tactical decisions

If you're not sure whether to use this approach, ask: *"Is the question about how we win at the same game, or about whether we should be playing a different game?"* If different game, use this. If same game, you probably want brainstorm or problem-solving.
