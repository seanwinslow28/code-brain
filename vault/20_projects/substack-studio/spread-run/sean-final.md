# The Skill That Gave Me Sisters

*I spent three months teaching Claude to write like four authors I love. It came back with jokes about a family I don't have.*

I was on the ferry home, re-reading Dharma Bums, admiring Kerouac keep a single sentence going long enough to punch me in the face four or five separate times to get the point across. Around that same stretch I'd found Nate B. Jones on YouTube, who has a pile of episodes about teaching an LLM your personal taste. Those two things met somewhere in the middle of the harbor and turned into a thought.

What if I could make Claude write like this?

So I went down the rabbit hole. I picked four of my favorite authors. Kerouac, Hunter S. Thompson, David Sedaris, Kurt Vonnegut. I researched each one, synthesized how they build a sentence, and crammed all four into a single writing skill. From then on, every essay, every document, every social post would come out the other end sounding like literary gold rather than typical AI slop.

The skill worked. That was the problem.

It leaned Sedaris for the essays, and honestly, that tracked. Comedic deflation is how I tend to write anyway, so of course that's the door it walked through. What didn't track was where the deflation landed. The punch lines kept coming back aimed at my wacky sisters. 

I'm an only child. 

It would get the whole family involved. I had it write a piece about agentic workflows and it ended with a callback to “spending chilly New York evenings making hot cocoa in the kitchen with Mother”. I had to re-read the sentence a few times to see if I could figure out the connection, but I had a tough time finding the correlation between AI evals and my youth. 

The other three didn't do any better. Kerouac gave me the length with none of the effect, the sentence rolling on and on and arriving nowhere in particular. Vonnegut sounded like a children's book author floating through the void. Thompson felt like I was injecting caffeine into my veins at 2am with all of my agents cheering me on.

None of that is the model doing a bad job. It did exactly what I asked. It can read any of those four and hand you a clean breakdown of how the engine turns over. What it can't hand you is the soul underneath. So everything it produced felt foreign and cringy, not because the prose was broken, but because it sounded like nothing that had ever come out of me.

Naturally, I did the thing anybody does after sinking real time into something that isn't working. I tried to keep the good parts. Trim the authors back, hold onto their better tendencies, leave the rest. 

It didn’t work. They continued to pop up in the worst possible ways.

I was looking into starting a Substack, and everything the machine was handing me was too cringy to publish and had nothing to do with me or anything I'd actually lived. So I brought in the skill chain, removed most of the rules, fed it my own writing, and started scrubbing the authors out. It still tried too hard to be elegant. The jokes were still awful. I kept pushing and had Claude write me exercises designed to pull more of me out and leave less of them in, and they still snuck through the cracks.

Three months in, I finally broke down and deleted all four of them. Kept every word I'd written myself and never looked back. 

Funny enough, I recently read a story about OpenAI quietly stopping ChatGPT from writing in the style of named authors, living or dead. It's a legal move, copyright suits. The sanctioned workaround is that you can still describe the techniques, you just can't say whose they are.

I think people are going to be pretty disappointed with what that gets them. Not because of the workaround. Because no matter how hard you push a model to copy somebody's style and bolt it onto your own work, it won't feel right putting it out into the world. I ran that experiment on my own time for three months and landed on the same policy, minus the lawyers.

Here's the part I'd want back. Models want to break writing down like a math problem, and mechanics only get you so far in this craft. Becoming a good writer takes time, lived experiences, and sleepless nights spilling your guts onto a keyboard because you need to get it out of you. You start off copying whoever you love, and if you keep typing long enough, all of those influences slowly meld into one voice that only you could have come up with. The same thing applies to teaching a model to capture that voice. There's no version where you hand it four writers' mechanics and get yourself back.

So these days the roster is smaller. No Kerouac, no Thompson, no Sedaris, no Vonnegut. I brain dump an idea into Claude, invoke a skill called /creative-partner, and let it grill me. It digs into everything I've thrown at it, asks questions until the idea becomes coherent. It offers me places to expand or pivot, and after a few rounds of pushing me in different directions, it hands me the final decision. It pulls things out of me instead of pouring somebody else in.

It was never going to give me a voice. The most it could ever do was keep asking where mine was.

Four authors, three months, one delete key. Mother hasn't shown up in a technical essay since.
