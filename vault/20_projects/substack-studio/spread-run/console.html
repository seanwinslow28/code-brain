    I Deleted the Authors

    I taught Claude to write like my four favorite writers. Then I spent three months scrubbing them back out.

    I was commuting home on the Staten Island ferry, re-reading Dharma Bums, when the idea hit me. Kerouac keeps a sentence rolling so it can verbally punch you in the face three, four, five times before it finally lets you off the ropes. I sat there admiring the bruises. Around that same time I'd discovered Nate B. Jones on YouTube, who has a whole run of episodes on teaching LLMs your personal taste. Somewhere over the harbor those two things collided. What if I could make Claude write like this?

    Down the rabbit hole I went. I researched and synthesized four of my favorite writers. Jack Kerouac. Hunter S. Thompson. David Sedaris. Kurt Vonnegut. I broke down their prose, their mechanics, the guts of how each of them builds a sentence, and I crammed all of it into a single skill. The plan was to inject actual good writing into my essays, my documents, my social posts, and whatever else needed saving from typical AI slop.

    The models did exactly what I asked. That turned out to be the problem.

    They can interpret the mechanics just fine. They can chart how a writer writes, graph it, reduce it to numbers on a whiteboard. What they can't provide is the soul behind any of it. Every output came back feeling foreign and cringy, and not because the model whiffed the assignment. It followed my instructions to the letter and kept handing me writing that sounded nothing like ME.

    I tested it out on a technical doc that was leaning towards David Sedaris to see if we can pull off his self-deprecating comedic tone. But Sedaris mines his family for material, so the model read the skill, thought about it for a few minutes, and spit out a paper with an absurdist POV and punch lines about my sisters.

    I'm an only child.

    Reading further, it dove into some agentic system design, but it folded in stories about “spending chilly New York evenings making hot chocolate in the kitchen with Mother”. I had to re-read the sentence a few times to see if I could figure out the connection, but I had a tough time finding the correlation between AI evals and my youth.

    The other three didn't fare much better. Kerouac gave me the length without the effect, sentences that went on and on and never landed the punch. Vonnegut sounded like a children's book author floating through the void. And Thompson felt like I was injecting caffeine into my veins at 2am with all of my agents cheering me on.

    I kept tinkering anyway. For about three months I convinced myself the next tweak would fix it. First I tried the half measure, keeping some of each author's tendencies so I could hold onto the good parts. They still bled through in the worst ways. The writing always felt like it was trying too hard to be elegant, and the jokes it attempted were painfully unfunny.

    What finally broke me was looking into starting this Substack. Everything coming back made me wince and had nothing to do with me or my actual experiences. So I tore the authors out completely. Kept every word I'd written myself, started fresh, chained the skills together, removed most of the rules, and fed the thing my own work. I had Claude run me through writing exercises to get more of me and less of them. When they still popped up here and there, I turned to Alex Lieberman's content machine approach and kept scrubbing.

    Funny timing on all of this. A few weeks ago OpenAI quietly stopped ChatGPT from writing in the style of named authors, living or dead. It's a legal move, copyright suits, and the sanctioned workaround is that you can still describe the techniques. You just can't say the name. I think people are going to be pretty disappointed with the results either way, because no matter how hard you try to have a model copy someone's style and fold it into your own work, it won't feel right putting it out into the world. OpenAI needed lawyers to reach that conclusion. I just needed three months and a fictional set of sisters.

    Here's what I know now that I didn't know on that ferry. The models will break writing down like a math problem, and mechanics can only get you so far in this craft. Becoming a good writer takes time, lived experiences, and sleepless nights spilling your guts onto a keyboard because you need to get it out of you. You start off copying your main influences, and as you keep typing, all of those inspirations slowly meld into one cohesive voice that only you could have come up with. Teaching a model to capture that voice works the same way. There's no shortcut through somebody else's soul.

    So these days the roster is smaller. No Kerouac, no Thompson, no Sedaris, no Vonnegut. I brain dump an idea into Claude, invoke a skill called /creative-partner, and let it grill me. It digs into everything I've spewed at it, asks questions until the idea becomes coherent. It offers me places to expand or pivot, and after a few rounds of pushing me in different directions, it hands me the final decision. It pulls things out of me instead of pouring somebody else in.

    The outputs still aren't Kerouac. They're not supposed to be.

    And Mother hasn't shown up in a technical essay since.
