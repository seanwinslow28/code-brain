# Claude Gave Me Sisters

*I spent three months teaching a machine to sound like my favorite writers. Then I deleted them.*

I was commuting home on the Staten Island ferry, rereading *Dharma Bums*, when I started admiring the way Jack Kerouac could keep a sentence moving long after a normal person would've pulled over. It didn't end so much as wind up and punch you again.

Around the same time, I found Nate B. Jones talking on YouTube about teaching LLMs your personal taste. My brain smashed the two things together.

Could I get Claude to write like this?

That question looked harmless. So does a loose floorboard.

I went down the rabbit hole and started researching four of my favorite writers. Jack Kerouac, Hunter S. Thompson, David Sedaris, and Kurt Vonnegut. I broke down how they worked, synthesized the findings, and stuffed all four into one writing skill like a literary turducken I expected to introduce itself as me.

The model understood the mechanics. That wasn't the problem.

It could keep a Kerouac sentence shambling across the page, but the sentence wasn't hitting anything. It just refused to sit down. Vonnegut came back sounding like a children's book drifting through outer space. Thompson acted like somebody hooked a caffeine bag straight into my veins at 2 a.m. while every agent in the terminal formed a cheer squad.

Sedaris was the most persistent.

Comedic deflation is already part of how I tend to write, so the skill kept leaning in that direction for essays. Unfortunately, it also picked up his family material and started generating punchlines about my mother and sisters.

I'm an only child.

Somehow, an essay about agentic workflows would wander away from the technical point and end with me hanging out with Mom. My imaginary sisters were apparently busy that day.

Mercifully, I can't remember the exact punchline. I only remember the feeling of reading it and watching a stranger wearing my clothes walk into the room. The model had reverse-engineered another writer so thoroughly that it invented a childhood for me.

The annoying part was that Claude wasn't doing a bad job. It followed my instructions. I asked for Kerouac, Thompson, Sedaris, and Vonnegut, and it marched all four of them onto the page wearing little name tags.

I just didn't belong in the group photo.

I kept tinkering for about three months. When I started looking into launching a Substack, the problem became harder to ignore. Everything coming back felt foreign. The sentences tried too hard to be elegant. The jokes walked into the room wearing a tiny bow tie and waited for applause.

So I added more rules. I incorporated a skill chain. I fed it my own work and had Claude give me writing exercises so I could get more of myself into the mix.

Then I tried negotiating with the authors.

I kept some of their tendencies because I still wanted the good parts. That half measure lasted until those tendencies started popping up in the worst places again. Apparently, an author mode is like glitter. You don't keep a tasteful amount. You find it in your socks three weeks later.

I finally removed all four of them, kept everything I'd written, and started fresh. When bits of the old approach still crept through, I turned to Alex Lieberman's content machine approach and changed the job entirely.

A few weeks ago, OpenAI quietly stopped ChatGPT from writing in the style of named authors, living or dead. You can still describe the techniques. You just can't name the writer.

That might solve a naming problem. It doesn't solve the writing problem.

You can remove Kerouac's name and ask for a long sentence that flows through multiple beats. You'll get the length. That doesn't mean you'll feel the punch. You can request comedic deflation without mentioning Sedaris. The machine may still send your fictional sisters over to finish the joke.

Give a model enough measurements and it'll try to solve a voice like an equation. Sentence length plus comedic deflation plus cosmic children's narrator plus caffeine poisoning.

The math can balance and the answer can still sound nothing like you.

Good writing doesn't come from knowing how many commas somebody uses. It comes from time, lived experience, and those sleepless nights when something is clawing around inside you and the only way to get it out is through the keyboard.

Most writers start by copying the people they love. You keep typing and those influences slowly melt together with everything that's happened to you. Eventually, the seams disappear and you're left with a voice nobody else could've assembled.

Teaching a model that voice works the same way.

Now I brain-dump the idea into Claude and have the creative-partner skill grill me. It asks questions, pushes me through multiple rounds, and shows me places where the idea could expand or pivot. It doesn't make the final decision. That's still mine.

Back on the ferry, I thought I needed Claude to become one of my favorite authors. What I needed was a creative partner willing to sit across from me and keep asking questions until I sounded like myself.

If a sister shows up, she gets cut.
