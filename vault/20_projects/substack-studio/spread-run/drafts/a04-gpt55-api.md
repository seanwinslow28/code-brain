# I Built a Writing Skill and Then Deleted the Writers

Claude could copy the mechanics. The problem was that it sounded like everyone except me.

I was commuting home on the Staten Island ferry, re-reading *Dharma Bums*, when I had one of those ideas that feels brilliant for about eight seconds before it starts chewing through your free time like a raccoon in a garbage bag.

I was paying attention to the way Kerouac keeps a sentence moving. The thing just rolls and rolls until it verbally punches you in the face multiple times, somehow without stopping to ask if you’re okay. Around that same time, I had found Nate B. Jones’ YouTube channel. He had multiple episodes about teaching LLMs your personal taste, and that little gremlin thought entered my head.

What if I could make Claude write like this?

Lovely. Perfect. No notes. Somebody get this man a tiny crown and a plaque that says “About To Waste Three Months.”

So I went down the hole.

I researched and synthesized Jack Kerouac, Hunter S. Thompson, David Sedaris, and Kurt Vonnegut. I broke down their mechanics, their prose habits, the sentence shapes, the joke shapes, the rhythm, the whole thing. Then I crammed all of it into one writing skill and expected Claude to become some kind of literary Voltron.

Instead, I built a costume rack.

Claude would reach in, grab a little Kerouac hat, a Sedaris scarf, a Vonnegut lunchbox, and a Thompson jacket soaked in whatever energy drink gets recalled by the FDA, then shuffle onto the stage and go, “Hello. I am Sean now.”

No, my little demon. You are not.

The model wasn’t doing a bad job either. That was the annoying part. It was doing exactly what I asked. It could interpret the mechanics. It could break down how someone writes and rebuild the skeleton. Sentence length. Deflation. Repetition. Cadence. All the little measurable parts.

But writing isn’t only measurable parts.

If it were, every open mic comic with a three-act structure and a dream would be selling out arenas instead of telling jokes to a bartender wiping down the same glass for forty-five minutes.

The Kerouac part technically worked. Claude kept the sentence going on and on, but it didn’t have the punch. It was like watching someone do a long jump in slow motion and land directly into a pile of laundry. The movement was there. The impact was not.

The Sedaris part was worse because it almost made sense.

A lot of my writing leans on comedic deflation. Build something up, then kick its ankles out. Sedaris writes about family a lot, so the model decided that if an essay needed a joke, it should probably land on my mother and sisters.

Small issue.

I’m an only child.

There are no sisters. Never have been. Unless my parents have been running some kind of long con for three decades, in which case, respect. That’s commitment.

So I’d be writing about agentic workflows, trying to explain some technical thing, and suddenly the essay would end with a callback to hanging out with my Mom. Not because the story asked for it. Not because my Mom had anything to do with the workflow. Just because the model found a pattern and started dragging my imaginary siblings into the room like furniture.

Vonnegut sounded like a children’s book author floating through the void.

Thompson felt like I was injecting caffeine into my veins at 2am with all of my agents cheering me on.

None of it was usable. It was too elegant in that fake way. Too polished. Too convinced it had found the secret door behind my taste when all it had done was put on someone else’s pants and ask me how it looked.

Not great, bud. Those are not your pants.

I kept tinkering anyway because that’s what you do when a thing is clearly broken but still close enough to make you think the next fix will save it. I tried keeping some of the author tendencies in there. Maybe I could hold onto the good parts. Maybe I could keep the sentence movement, the deflation, the weird little philosophical turns, and toss the rest.

Nope.

It still popped up in the worst ways.
