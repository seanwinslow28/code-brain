# Claude Gave Me Sisters

*Three months, four literary heroes, and the family I never had.*

I was commuting home on the Staten Island ferry, re-reading Dharma Bums, and admiring the way Kerouac keeps a sentence flowing long enough to verbally punch you in the face multiple times before it lets go. Right around then I'd also found Nate B. Jones on YouTube, who has a bunch of episodes about teaching LLMs your personal taste. Two wires touched. What if I could make Claude write like this?

So I went down the rabbit hole. I researched and synthesized four of my favorite writers. Kerouac. Hunter S. Thompson. David Sedaris. Kurt Vonnegut. I broke down their styles, their mechanics, their whole bag of tricks, and crammed everything into a single skill. I thought I was building a writing tool. I was actually assembling a tribute band.

The thing about tribute bands is they can play every note perfectly and you still know within two seconds.

The annoying part is that the models didn't do a bad job. They did exactly what I instructed. They could interpret the mechanics and break down how these guys wrote. What they couldn't provide was the soul behind it, and I don't mean that in a precious way. I mean every output that came back felt foreign and cringy, not because the writing was bad, but because it sounded nothing like me.

The models leaned Sedaris for a lot of the essays, because of comedic deflation, which is fair, since that's how I tend to write. So Claude reached into the Sedaris toolkit and decided what my technical writing was missing was family. The jokes kept landing on my mother and my sisters. Quick biography check. I'm an only child. There are no sisters. Claude cast siblings for me like it was staffing a sitcom. And it didn't stop there. I'd write about agentic workflows and the thing would end on a callback to hanging out with my Mom. It made sense on paper. Sedaris writes about his family all the time. What didn't make sense was the family drama inside a technical essay. I can't remember a single exact punch line, which tells you how memorable they were. They might still be in the vault somewhere if you want to go digging for them. I will not be joining you.

Kerouac gave me length without the effect. The model definitely kept the sentence going on and on, but none of the punches landed. All cardio, no contact.

Vonnegut always sounded like a children's book author floating through the void.

Thompson felt like I was injecting caffeine into my veins at 2am with all of my agents cheering me on. (The original version of that simile was rated R. We've downgraded to PG-13.)

Four legends. One skill. Zero soul. The tribute band could hit every note. It still wasn't the band.

I kept tinkering for about three months, which is an embarrassing amount of time to spend teaching a machine to be other people. Then, a month or two ago, I finally broke down. I was looking into starting a Substack, and everything coming back felt too cringy and had nothing to do with me or my actual experiences. Hard to launch a personal publication when the person in it keeps getting recast.

First I tried the half measure. Keep some of the authors' tendencies, hold onto the good parts. The good parts kept popping up in the worst ways. So I fired the band. Removed them completely, kept everything I'd written, and started fresh. Skill chain. More rules. My own writing fed in as the source material. Claude ran me through writing exercises to get more of me and less of them onto the page. The authors still snuck back for the occasional encore, which is what pushed me toward Alex Lieberman's content machine approach. That's when things actually started sounding like me. The Substack I was trying to start is the one you're reading, so it worked out.

Then a few weeks ago, OpenAI quietly stopped ChatGPT from writing in the style of named authors, living or dead. Copyright suits. The sanctioned workaround is that you can still describe the techniques, you just can't name the writer. My official review is that people are going to be pretty disappointed with the results. Not because of the legal stuff. Because the workaround misses the point. No matter how hard you try to have a model copy someone's style and add it into your own work, it won't feel right putting it out there in the world. It took OpenAI a team of lawyers to learn what three months of imaginary sisters taught me at the cost of my dignity.

Here's what I know now that I didn't know on that ferry. The models will break writing down like a math problem, but mechanics can only get you so far in this medium. Becoming a good writer takes time, lived experiences, and sleepless nights spilling your guts onto a keyboard because you need to get it out of you. You start off copying your main influences. Everybody does. But if you keep typing, all of those inspirations slowly meld into one cohesive voice that only you could have come up with. The same idea applies to teaching the model. You can't feed it four heroes and skip the melding.

So the job is different now. I brain dump an idea into Claude and invoke the /creative-partner skill, and instead of doing impressions of my heroes, it grills me. It analyzes whatever I spewed into it, asks questions to flesh the idea out, and pushes me through multiple rounds of expansions and pivots I didn't see coming. The final call stays mine. If you want the model to sound like you, feed it you, then let it grill you. The machine went from impersonating my favorite writers to interviewing me, and the writing finally sounds like the guy who lived it.

My sisters could not be reached for comment.
