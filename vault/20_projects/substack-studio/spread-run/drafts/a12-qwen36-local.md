I Crammed Four Writers Into a Skill Chain and Got an Only Child's Family Drama

Trying to force-feed Claude Kerouac, Sedaris, Thompson, and Vonnegut while learning that mechanics can only get you so far.

I was building a set of prompts for agentic workflows. The model leaned heavily on David Sedaris for the comedic deflation because it made sense structurally. The problem was that every joke the machine produced contained a punchline involving my mother and sisters.

First off, I'm an only child. I have no sisters to reference. Second, I'm writing about technical workflows and the output kept ending with some sort of callback to hanging out with my Mom. It made sense because Sedaris wrote about family drama all the time. What didn't make sense was forcing that domestic subplot into a piece of code architecture. The model wasn't hallucinating; it was doing exactly as I instructed. It just sounded nothing like me.

Before the crash, there was the Staten Island ferry. I was commuting home, re-reading Jack Kerouac's *Dharma Bums*, and admiring how he keeps a sentence flowing until it verbally punches you in the face multiple times to really drive the point home. That was around the time I discovered Nate B. Jones' YouTube channel, which had episodes about finding ways to teach LLMs personal taste. I thought, "What if I could make Claude write like this?" and went down a rabbit hole of figuring out the best ways to accomplish that.

So I crammed four authors into a single skill chain: Kerouac, Thompson, Sedaris, and Vonnegut. I researched and synthesized each one, broke down their mechanics, and tried to force them all in. For about three months, the model kept trying to be all four writers at once. It felt foreign and cringy every time an output came back. Not because the models did a bad job—the output was often technically proficient—but because it was trying too hard to be elegant and the jokes it pulled off were painfully unfunny.

Kerouac gave me the length without the punch-in-the-face effect. Vonnegut always sounded like a children's book author floating through the void. Thompson felt like I was injecting caffeine into my veins at 2 AM with all of my agents cheering me on. Sedaris kept trying to draft family drama I didn't have.

About a month or two ago, I finally broke down because I was looking into starting a Substack. Everything being sent my way felt too cringy and had nothing to do with my personal experiences. So I scrapped the authors completely. I kept everything I'd written, started fresh, incorporated a skill chain, injected more rules, and fed in my own work. I had Claude give me writing exercises to get more of *me* and less of *them*.

It was still popping up here and there, which is why I turned to Alex Lieberman's approach with the content machine. Now, instead of trying to clone a ghost writer, I have the model grill me and really dig into the ideas I have. It becomes my creative partner, pulling things out of me and sparking ideas that weren't there before.

You brain dump an idea into Claude, have it invoke the skill to analyze everything you've spewed, and start asking questions to flesh it out. It proposes expansions or where to pivot, but ultimately gives you the final decision after multiple rounds of pushing you in different directions.

What I know now that I didn't on that ferry is that the models will try to break writing down like a math problem, but mechanics can only get you so far in this medium. Becoming a good writer takes time, lived experiences, and sleepless nights spilling your guts onto a keyboard because you need to get it out of you. You start off trying to copy your main influences, but as you keep typing, all of your inspirations slowly meld into one cohesive voice that only you could have come up with.

The same idea applies to teaching the model how to capture that voice. You don't inject mechanics; you use the tool to extract the voice you've already built through the grind. If you're stuck on how to make AI sound like you, stop trying to teach it a ghost writer's habits. Teach it yours.
