**I Tried to Teach Claude to Write Like My Heroes. It Handed Back a Stranger.**

*I broke down four literary legends and stuffed their mechanics into a skill. The machine did exactly what I asked. That was the problem.*

I was on the Staten Island ferry, re-reading *Dharma Bums*, admiring the way Kerouac keeps a sentence going until it turns around and punches you in the face three times before you can breathe. Around that same stretch I’d found Nate B. Jones’ YouTube channel — episodes on teaching LLMs your personal taste — and the thought landed fully formed, the way bad ideas always do when you’re trapped on a boat with nowhere to run: *What if I could make Claude write like this?*

I went home and dove into the rabbit hole. I researched four authors — Kerouac, Hunter S. Thompson, David Sedaris, Kurt Vonnegut — broke down their mechanics, and crammed everything into a single writing skill. The plan was elegant: summon whichever voice fit the piece, inject it into my essays, my social posts, anything that needed better writing than the usual AI slop. I was going to build a little literary engine and slap it onto my Substack.

The models did exactly as I instructed. That was the problem. They could interpret the mechanics — the sentence length, the deflation cadence, the rhythm of a punchline — but they couldn’t supply the soul. Every output came back feeling foreign and cringy. Not because the model was bad at its job. Because it kept handing me prose that sounded nothing like me.

Sedaris got leaned on the hardest for essays, and I understand why: comedic deflation is how I tend to write. But the jokes it wrote kept landing on my mother and sisters. I’m an only child. I don’t have sisters. An essay about agentic workflows would barrel toward a conclusion and then suddenly I’m doing a callback to hanging out with my Mom. It made sense for Sedaris and zero sense for me.

Kerouac gave me the rolling length without the effect. Vonnegut came out like a children’s book author floating through the void. Thompson felt like I was injecting caffeine into my veins at 2 a.m. with all my agents cheering me on. Each voice was technically legible and emotionally vacant, a math problem dressed in a Halloween costume, and every time I read the output I wanted to peel my own face off.

I kept tinkering for about three months. I’d add more rules, tweak the temperature, layer on some new instruction. The machine stayed obedient. The stranger on the page wouldn’t leave.

A month or two ago I broke down. I was looking into starting a Substack and everything the pipeline returned felt like a try-hard version of someone else’s voice that had been stretched over my life like a bad wig. I incorporated the skill chain, injected more rules, fed in my own writing, and began the process of scrubbing the authors out. Then I had Claude give me writing exercises to pull more of me and less of them. The ghost of Sedaris still popped up here and there — a deflationary aside that sounded like a family roast at a dinner I never attended — which is when I turned to Alex Lieberman’s content machine approach.

I scrapped the authors completely. Kept everything I’d written myself and started fresh.

What I know now that I didn’t know on that ferry is this: the models try to break writing down like a math problem, and mechanics can only get you so far. Becoming a decent writer takes time, lived experience, and sleepless nights spilling your guts onto a keyboard because you need to get it out of you. You start off copying your influences, and as you keep typing, all your inspirations slowly meld into one cohesive voice that only you could have come up with. Teaching a model to capture that voice works the same way.

So I don’t ask Claude to sound like someone else anymore. I brain-dump an idea, invoke a creative partner skill, and let the model grill me. It reads everything I’ve spewed into the chat and starts asking questions to flesh the idea out and make it more coherent. It proposes expansions, suggests pivots, pushes me in different directions across multiple rounds. Then I make the final call. It pulls things out of me and sparks ideas that weren’t there before, which is the exact opposite of what the author-skill was doing. That thing was shoving borrowed voices into my mouth. This one digs until it hits bedrock, and the bedrock sounds like me.

A few weeks ago OpenAI quietly stopped ChatGPT from writing in the style of named authors, living or dead — a legal move, copyright suits. The sanctioned workaround is that you can still describe the techniques, you just can’t name the writer. I think people are going to be pretty disappointed with the results, because no matter how hard you try to have a model copy someone’s style and pump it into your own work, it won’t feel right putting it out there in the world. A mechanic without a soul is just a mannequin in a nice coat, and the coat belongs to somebody else.

I still hear that ferry horn from across the Hudson some mornings. The boat still shepherds the same lost souls back and forth. I’m just not on it anymore, and I’m not trying to bring four dead authors along for the ride.
