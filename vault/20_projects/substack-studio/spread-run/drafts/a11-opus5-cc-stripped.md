# Four Authors, No Sisters

*I spent three months teaching Claude to write like my heroes. It handed me a family I don't have.*

I was commuting home on the Staten Island ferry, re-reading Dharma Bums, when I got the idea that cost me three months.

Kerouac does this thing where he keeps a sentence rolling and it punches you in the face, then keeps rolling and punches you again, and by the time it stops the point is lodged somewhere behind your eye. Around that same stretch I'd found Nate B. Jones on YouTube, who has a pile of episodes about teaching a model your personal taste. Those two things shook hands somewhere over the water.

What if I could make Claude write like this?

So I built a writing skill. The plan was that everything after it, my essays, my documents, whatever social post needed to not read like the usual slop, would come out with actual prose behind it. I picked four. Kerouac. Hunter S. Thompson. David Sedaris. Kurt Vonnegut. I researched each one, synthesized how they work, took the style apart down to the moving pieces, and crammed all four into a single skill.

The model understood the assignment perfectly. That was the problem.

Sedaris got the most weight on the essays. He deflates, and that's how I tend to write, so the machine kept reaching for him any time something needed a joke. Which is how I started getting punchlines about my mother and my sisters.

I'm an only child. There are no sisters. There have never been sisters.

I couldn't tell you the exact line anymore. What I remember is where those jokes kept landing. I'd be writing about agentic workflows, dry technical stuff, and the last beat of the whole thing would swerve into a warm little callback about hanging out with my Mom. On paper it tracks. Sedaris writes about his family, so the machine wrote about mine, and wherever mine ran out it just built more. What doesn't track is family drama wandering into a technical essay and pulling up a chair.

The other three were wrong in their own directions. Kerouac gave me the length. The sentence went on and on exactly like I asked and did nothing to anybody. Vonnegut came out sounding like a children's book author floating through the void. Thompson felt like I was injecting caffeine into my veins at 2am with all of my agents cheering me on.

Here's the part that took me the longest to say out loud. The models didn't do a bad job. They did precisely what I told them, every single time, no complaints, no shortcuts. They can read a writer, break the mechanics down, and hand you the parts in a neat little pile. What they can't hand you is the thing underneath the parts. So every output came back competent, foreign, and cringy, and not one line of it sounded like me.

I kept at it anyway, because it kept almost working. Three months of that. Then a month or two ago I broke down. I was looking into starting a Substack and everything getting sent my way was too cringy to publish and had nothing to do with me or anything I've actually lived. It was straining to be elegant in every sentence. And the jokes. Painfully unfunny.

First I tried the half measure. Keep the authors' tendencies, lose the rest, hold onto the good parts. Very surgical of me. It still popped up in the worst possible places.

So I deleted all four. Kept every word I'd written myself, threw out everything they'd given me, and started over. I brought in the skill chain, piled on more rules, fed the machine my own work, and had Claude write me exercises built to pull out more of me and less of them. It still poked its head up here and there, which is how I ended up at Alex Lieberman's content machine approach, which is the thing you're reading right now.

Funny timing on all of it. A few weeks ago OpenAI quietly stopped ChatGPT from writing in the style of named authors, living or dead. Legal move, copyright suits. The sanctioned workaround is that you can describe the techniques all day, you just can't say whose they are.

People are going to be disappointed with what comes out the other side of that. Not because the workaround fails. Because no matter how well a model copies somebody's style and welds it onto your work, it won't feel right putting it out there in the world. I ran the unsanctioned version for three months with the names still attached and it read like a rented tux three sizes off, in every photo, forever.

Here's what I didn't know on that ferry. A model will try to break writing down like a math problem, because that's the only grip it has on the thing. Mechanics carry you a certain distance in this medium and then they quit on you and go home. The rest is time, lived experience, and a bunch of sleepless nights spilling your guts onto a keyboard because it has to get out of you or you're not sleeping anyway.

You do start out copying. Everybody starts out copying. But the more you type, the more your influences melt into each other, until what's left is one voice that only you could have come up with. That's the part you can't hand over as a spec, because it isn't one. It's residue.

Same goes for teaching a machine to capture it.

So it doesn't write like anybody now. It grills me instead.

I brain dump an idea into Claude, invoke /creative-partner, and let it pick through everything I just spewed at it. It asks questions until the idea stops being a puddle. It tells me where to expand, where I might want to pivot, and shoves me toward three directions I hadn't considered. Then it hands the decision back to me. Every round, every time.

That's the trade. It stopped trying to be the writer and became the guy who won't let me leave the room until I say what I actually mean.

Kerouac was never the missing piece. He was doing just fine on that ferry without my help.

And I still don't have any sisters.
