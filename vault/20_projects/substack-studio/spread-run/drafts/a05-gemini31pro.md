I Tried to Automate Hunter S. Thompson
And all I got was a children's book author floating through the void.

I was commuting home on the Staten Island ferry, re-reading Jack Kerouac's Dharma Bums. I sat there admiring how he keeps a sentence flowing to verbally punch you in the face multiple times to really drive the point home. Right around that same time, I discovered Nate B. Jones on YouTube. He had a few episodes about finding ways to teach LLMs your personal taste. I watched them, looked back at the book, and thought, "What if I could make Claude write like this?"

I went down a rabbit hole for about three months. I thought it would be clever to create a writing skill that understood the mechanics and prose of my favorite writers and injected them into my personal essays, documents, and social posts. Anything to avoid the usual AI slop. I researched and synthesized four guys. Jack Kerouac, Hunter S. Thompson, David Sedaris, and Kurt Vonnegut. I broke down their style and crammed everything into a single skill.

It was a complete disaster.

The models did exactly as I instructed. They broke the writing down like a math problem and interpreted the mechanics perfectly. The issue was that they couldn't provide the actual soul behind it. Every time an output popped out, it felt foreign and cringy. It sounded absolutely nothing like me.

The models leaned on David Sedaris for a lot of the essays. I naturally write with comedic deflation, so the machine picked up on the pattern. The problem was that its idea of a joke involved constant punch lines about my mother and my sisters. I am an only child. You don't know true confusion until you read a highly technical essay about agentic workflows that ends on a sentimental callback to hanging out with your Mom. Family drama doesn't belong in a tech essay.

Kerouac gave me the sentence length without the actual effect. Vonnegut sounded like a children's book author floating through the void. Thompson felt like I was injecting caffeine into my veins at 2am with all of my agents cheering me on. It tried way too hard to be elegant, and the jokes it tried to pull off were painfully unfunny.

A month or two ago, I finally broke down. I was looking into starting a Substack and everything being sent my way had nothing to do with me or my personal experiences. 

I tried a half measure first. I kept a few of the authors' tendencies within the skill to try and hold onto the good parts. It still popped up in the worst ways. So I nuked them. I completely removed the authors, kept everything I had actually written, and started fresh. I incorporated the skill chain, injected more rules, fed in my own work, and told Claude to run me through writing exercises to get more of me and less of them. 

It was still haunting the outputs here and there, which is why I turned to Alex Lieberman's approach with the content machine.

Mechanics can only get you so far in this medium. Becoming a good writer takes time, lived experiences, and sleepless nights spilling your guts onto a keyboard because you need to get it out of you. You start out trying to copy your main influences, but as you keep typing, they slowly meld into one cohesive voice that only you could have come up with. The exact same idea applies to teaching a model how to capture that voice.

A few weeks ago, OpenAI quietly stopped ChatGPT from writing in the style of named authors to dodge copyright suits. The sanctioned workaround is that you can still describe the techniques, you just can't name the writer. Honestly, people are going to be disappointed with the results anyway. No matter how hard you try to have a model copy someone's style and add it into your own work, it won't feel right putting it out there in the world.

I do things differently now. I brain-dump an idea into Claude and invoke the /creative-partner skill. The model analyzes everything I spewed into it and starts asking me questions to flesh out the idea. It grills me. It acts as a creative partner to pull things out of me and spark ideas that weren't there before. 

We go through multiple rounds of it pushing me in different potential directions or proposing expansions and pivots. Ultimately, it gives me the final decision. The machine doesn't get to be Kerouac anymore. It just gets to ask the questions. I do the punching.
