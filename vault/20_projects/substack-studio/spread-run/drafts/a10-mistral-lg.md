The Staten Island Ferry, My Mother, and the AI That Couldn’t Write Like Me

I thought it would be clever to teach an AI how to write like me. Not just the mechanics—how I string words together, where I put the commas—but the actual soul of it. The part that makes it sound like me and not like some soulless intern who’s been trained on a thousand strangers’ voices.

So I did what any self-respecting nerd would do: I picked four of my favorite writers—Kerouac, Thompson, Sedaris, Vonnegut—and tried to cram their styles into a single skill. I broke down their prose like it was a math problem. Sentence length, rhythm, the way they deflate a moment with a joke. I fed it all in, expecting it to spit out something that sounded like me, just sharper, funnier, more *me*.

What I got instead was a disaster.

The AI leaned hard on Sedaris, which made sense—his whole thing is comedic deflation, the way he turns a mundane moment into something absurd. But the jokes it wrote kept landing on my mother and sisters. The problem? I’m an only child. There are no sisters to reference. And yet, there they were, in every essay, like some kind of AI-generated family drama. An essay about agentic workflows would end with a callback to hanging out with my mom. The machine didn’t know I didn’t have sisters. It didn’t *care*. It just knew Sedaris wrote about his family, so it assumed I did too.

Kerouac was another mess. The AI nailed the long, flowing sentences—those run-ons that feel like a punch to the face, over and over. But the effect wasn’t there. It was just length without the rhythm, like a jazz solo played by someone who’s never heard music.

Vonnegut sounded like a children’s book author floating through the void. Thompson, meanwhile, felt like I was ripping lines of blow at 2 a.m. with all my agents cheering me on. Which, for the record, I’ve never done. But the AI didn’t know that.

I kept tinkering. I added more rules, fed in my own work, scrubbed out the authors one by one. But it always felt like it was trying too hard to be elegant, like it was performing *writing* instead of just doing it. The jokes were unfunny. The voice was foreign. And no matter how many adjustments I made, it still didn’t sound like me.

So I tried something new. Instead of teaching the AI to mimic my influences, I started grilling it. I’d dump a brain dump into Claude and have it ask me questions—real, pointed questions—to flesh out the idea. It would propose expansions, suggest pivots, but the final decision was always mine. And slowly, something started to click. The writing began to sound like me, not because I’d reverse-engineered my style, but because I’d stopped trying to force it.

The models can break writing down like a math problem, but mechanics only get you so far. Becoming a good writer takes time, lived experiences, and sleepless nights where you spill your guts onto a keyboard because you *have* to get it out of you. You start by copying your influences, but eventually, all those voices meld into something that’s only yours.

And that’s the thing about teaching an AI to write like you. It’s not about giving it a formula. It’s about showing it the world through your eyes—your weird, specific, slightly unhinged eyes—and letting it learn from there.

Now, when I write, I don’t just hand the AI a prompt and hope for the best. I treat it like a creative partner. I let it push me, challenge me, ask me the questions I haven’t thought to ask myself. And when it spits out something that sounds like me, I know it’s not because it’s mimicking my style. It’s because it’s finally starting to *get* me.

Which is more than I can say for some of the essays it used to write about my nonexistent sisters.
