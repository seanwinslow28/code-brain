# The Skill That Gave Me Sisters

*I spent three months teaching Claude to write like four authors I love. It came back with jokes about a family I don't have.*

I was on the ferry home, re-reading Dharma Bums, watching Kerouac keep a single sentence going long enough to punch me in the face four or five separate times before he let it stop. Around that same stretch I'd found Nate B. Jones on YouTube, who has a pile of episodes about teaching an LLM your personal taste. Those two things met somewhere in the middle of the harbor and turned into a thought.

What if I could make Claude write like this?

So I went down the rabbit hole. I picked four. Kerouac, Hunter S. Thompson, David Sedaris, Kurt Vonnegut. I researched each one, synthesized how they build a sentence, broke their moves down into mechanics, and crammed all four into a single writing skill. From then on, every essay, every document, every social post would come out the other end sounding like it had been raised by better parents than the usual AI slop.

The skill worked. That was the problem.

It leaned Sedaris for the essays, and honestly, that tracked. Comedic deflation is how I tend to write anyway, so of course that's the door it walked through. What didn't track was where the deflation landed. The punch lines kept coming back aimed at my mother and my sisters. I don't have sisters. I'm an only child. So there I'd sit, reading a piece about agentic workflows that closed on a warm little callback to hanging out with my Mom. I couldn't repeat the exact line to you if you held a gun to my head. That one's gone and I'm not going looking for it.

The other three didn't do any better. Kerouac gave me the length with none of the effect, the sentence rolling on and on and arriving nowhere in particular. Vonnegut sounded like a children's book author floating through the void. Thompson felt like I was injecting caffeine into my veins at 2am with all of my agents cheering me on.

None of that is the model doing a bad job. It did exactly what I asked. It can read any of those four and hand you a clean breakdown of how the engine turns over. What it can't hand you is the soul underneath. So everything it produced felt foreign and cringy, not because the prose was broken, but because it sounded like nothing that had ever come out of me.

Naturally, I did the thing anybody does after sinking real time into something that isn't working. I tried to keep the good parts. Trim the authors back, hold onto their better tendencies, leave the rest. They kept popping up in the worst possible ways, like a guy you fired who still shows up to the office holiday party.

About a month or two ago I finally broke down. I was looking into starting a Substack, and everything the machine was handing me was too cringy to publish and had nothing to do with me or anything I'd actually lived. So I brought in the skill chain, injected more rules, fed it my own writing, and started scrubbing the authors out. It still tried too hard to be elegant. The jokes were still painfully unfunny. I had Claude write me exercises designed to pull more of me out and leave less of them in, and they still snuck through the cracks. That's what sent me looking at Alex Lieberman's content machine approach.

Three months in, I deleted all four of them. Kept every word I'd written myself. Started fresh.

A few weeks ago, OpenAI quietly stopped ChatGPT from writing in the style of named authors, living or dead. It's a legal move, copyright suits. The sanctioned workaround is that you can still describe the techniques, you just can't say whose they are.

I think people are going to be pretty disappointed with what that gets them. Not because of the workaround. Because no matter how hard you push a model to copy somebody's style and bolt it onto your own work, it won't feel right putting it out into the world. I ran that experiment on my own time for three months and landed on the same policy, minus the lawyers.

Here's the part I'd want back. Models want to break writing down like a math problem, and mechanics only get you so far in this medium. Becoming a good writer takes time, lived experiences, and sleepless nights spilling your guts onto a keyboard because you need to get it out of you. You start off copying whoever you love, and if you keep typing long enough, all of those influences slowly meld into one voice that only you could have come up with. The same thing applies to teaching a model to capture that voice. There's no version where you hand it four writers' mechanics and get yourself back.

So the job changed. I stopped asking it to write like anybody. Now I brain dump an idea into Claude, invoke the creative-partner skill, and let it grill me on the pile I just spewed in. It asks questions until the idea holds together, points at where it could expand, points at where I might want to pivot instead, and it does that across multiple rounds. Then it hands the decision back to me.

It was never going to give me a voice. The most it could ever do was keep asking where mine was.

Four authors, three months, one delete key. Still an only child.
