\# The Pulitzer Prize Won’t Save You

\*Three months of teaching Claude to write the wrong words.\*

I built a skill that would help me write some technical documents with the help of some of my favorite authors sprinkled in. The idea came to me as I was commuting home on the Staten Island ferry, re-reading Dharma Bums, admiring the way Kerouac keeps a sentence flowing to verbally punch you in the face multiple times to really drive the point home. Around the same time, I stumbled upon Nate B. Jones' YouTube video about teaching LLM’s your personal taste.

I thought, “What if I could make Claude write like this?”

So I went down a rabbit hole. I researched and synthesized some of my favorite authors. Jack Kerouac, Hunter S. Thompson, David Sedaris, Kurt Vonnegut. I broke down their style and crammed everything into a single skill, and I wanted to point it at my essays, documents,  social posts, anything that required good writing instead of typical AI slop.

I tested it out on a technical doc that was leaning towards David Sedaris to see if we can pull off his self depreciating comedic tone. The model read the skill, thought about it for a few minutes, and spit out a paper with an absurdist POV and punch lines involving my sisters.

I'm an only child. 

Reading further, it dove into some agentic system design, but it folded in stories about “spending summers in my childhood home with Mother”.

I mean… AI evals don’t really make me reminisce about the days of old. 

Kerouac, sort of got there. The model definitely kept the sentence going on and on, but it didn't have the same “verbal punch” effect.

The other two struggled as well. Vonnegut always sounded like a children's book author floating through the void. Thompson felt like I was injecting caffeine into my veins at 2am with all of my agents cheering me on.

I thought I had solved the riddle of teaching the model my preferred taste, but I ended up with a sophisticated tech nerd in a tailcoat and a second family I didn’t know about. 

The models weren't doing a bad job, necessarily. They were doing exactly as I instructed. They can interpret the mechanics and break down how they write, but they couldn't provide the actual soul behind the writing. Every output that came back felt fraudulent and cringy.

By then I'd been at it for about three months.

So obviously I didn't want to completely let it go. I kept some of the authors' tendencies in there to see if I could try and bring out the good parts. It still popped up in the worst ways.

About a month or two ago, I was looking into starting a Substack, and everything being sent my way still felt off. I realized the stories had nothing to do with me and my personal experiences because these authors didn’t live my life.

I finally broke down and completely removed them. That experiment was a failure, but one that I could learn from.

I incorporated a skill chain to help with story structure, injected more rules, and fed in dozens of random stories that only I could tell. I had Claude give me writing exercises to get more of me and less of them. It was still trying to focus on mechanics and forced in callbacks like a *Family Guy* episode. I turned to Alex Lieberman's approach with his content machine project. It helped bring in more interviews and strict judgement. A better harness that I try to sustain and improve every day.

The problem is that models will try to break writing down like a math problem, and placing words in specific places can only get you so far in this craft. Becoming a good writer takes time, lived experiences, and sleepless nights spilling your guts onto a keyboard because you need to get it out of you. You can start off copying your main influences, but as you keep typing, all of your inspirations slowly meld into one cohesive voice that only you could have come up with.

The same idea applies to teaching the model how to capture that voice.

So now I have it grill me instead. I brain dump an idea into Claude and have it invoke my /creative-partner skill to analyze everything I gave it. It starts asking me questions to flesh the idea out and make it more coherent. It gives me some ideas on how to expand or where to pivot. Then, after multiple rounds of pushing me in different potential directions, it hands it back over to me to make the final decision.

I read an article about OpenAI quietly stopping ChatGPT from writing in the style of named authors due to copyright suits, but the workaround is that you can still describe the techniques, you just can't name the writer.

I think people are going to be pretty disappointed with the results, regardless. No matter how hard you try to have a model copy someone's style and add it into your own work, it won't feel like you.
