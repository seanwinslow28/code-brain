# Correct Was Never Defined

I built a machine whose whole job is to catch you before you let an agent run on a half-written idea. The first thing I did was point it at my own work. Not some throwaway file. The spec I was proudest of, the one I'd sharpened for months, the rulebook the entire machine was built on top of. It scored me a 23 out of 25.

Two points. One of them was a success metric I'd written as something the agent *does* instead of something that's *true* when it's finished. I wrote the rulebook on defining correct, and I still left a gate open in the one document where I'd have sworn every gate was bolted shut.

Here's why those two points kept me up. That gap is the exact spot where agents go wrong. Not the dramatic way everybody writes think-pieces about. The quiet way. You ask for a thing, and the agent hands you a thing adjacent to the thing, and then explains, with total confidence, why that's actually what you wanted. Right enough to ship. Wrong enough to hurt. And you don't find out until it's already in front of a customer.

The whole market is selling you a bigger engine. A more autonomous agent, a smarter model, a generator that spits out more and faster. I went digging through what people actually complain about, the real threads, not the keynote slides, and the sharpest line in the whole pile wasn't about any of that. It was this: the missing layer isn't autonomy. It's structured intent. Everybody's building bigger engines and leaving the steering wheel in the box.

Let me show you the gap with a real one. A while back I had Claude Code clean up a batch of my skills before I put them out in public, and I gave it one job: audit three specific files for some old gunk I knew was buried in there. It did that. Then, on its own, it ran the same check across all the others, found the same junk hiding in eight more skills I hadn't flagged, an old employer's name sitting in a prompt here, a stray internal reference there, and patched every one before the batch ever left the house.

My instruction was "audit three files." What I actually wanted was "don't hand me something I'll regret in public." The agent honored the second one. That time. The whole reason I built it is the times it won't.

Because the bug was never the model. When an agent faceplants in front of real users, the post-mortem points at the model, or the prompt, or the training data. It almost never points at the intent, for the dumb little reason that nobody wrote the intent down. There was nothing to point at. When Klarna walked back how hard it had leaned on its support agent, the headlines made it sound like the AI wasn't smart enough. It was plenty smart. What was missing was a definition of done that included "and the customer isn't worse off than when they walked in," and a rule for when it should shut up and go get a human. That's not a capability problem. That's a blank where the intent should have been.

So the fix isn't a better model. It's boring, and it sits upstream of everything: define correct before you build. Three things, written down, before a single token gets spent. The boundaries, what's off the table. The success criteria, what's true when this is actually done, a state, not an activity. The decision rules, what it does when it hits a fork, including when to stop and ask. Do that, and the agent's failures show up loud and early, in a doc you can read, instead of quiet and late, in front of the people you're trying to keep.

That's the bet the whole tool makes. The audit is the eval. You don't wait for production to grade your intent. You grade it from inside the same harness the agent already runs in, before it runs.

You can run this on your own stuff this weekend. I packaged it as an MCP server called [intent-engineering](https://github.com/seanwinslow28/sw-mcp-intent-engineering), `@swins/intent-engineering-mcp`. Point its audit tool at whatever you're about to hand an agent, a spec, a SKILL.md, a prompt you've been quietly trusting, and it grades that out of 25 and names the gaps before they cost you anything. No model upgrade required.

And if you don't feel like installing anything, do the cheap version tonight. Open the doc you're about to turn an agent loose on and write down the three. One boundary. One success state, phrased as something that's true, not something that happens. One stop rule. If you can't fill all three, congratulations, you just found the part the agent was going to guess at.

I built it to catch other people defining correct too late. It caught me first, in the one document I'd have bet my name on. That's not the embarrassing part of the story. That's the entire point of it. The machine doesn't care who wrote the rulebook. It only asks if you meant it, then waits to see if you can prove it.
