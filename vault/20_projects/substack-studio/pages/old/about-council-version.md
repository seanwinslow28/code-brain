# About

I was the kid who couldn't shut it off. Saturday mornings were a sacrament. I'd plant myself three feet from a static TV with a bowl of cereal balanced on my knee and inhale Rugrats, Rocko, Looney Tunes, whatever the networks were willing to fling at me. The second the credits rolled, something had to come back out. Crayons. Stolen printer paper. Stupid little flipbooks in the corner of a notebook. Plays my poor sister was forced to act in for an audience of zero. The intake had to become output or I'd lose my mind.

That part never really went away. It just got more expensive.

I taught myself animation the slow way, which is the humiliating way. Tutorials at 2 a.m., exporting a ten second clip and finding out at frame 184 that I'd been working in the wrong resolution the whole time. Shitty coffee, cursing at After Effects like it owed me money, drawing the same thing badly, then drawing it badly again, then realizing the first version had more charm and wanting to walk into the ocean. My first animated comedy short was called Break Time. In my twenties I wrote eleven screenplays, because apparently one or two wasn't enough evidence I had a problem. Years of earned taste. No machine in sight. Just me, a Wacom, and the dim sense that I was either getting better or getting weirder, possibly both.

Then the machines dropped out of the sky.

You remember it. One Monday everyone on your feed was a Miyazaki character. By Thursday your aunt had a children's book. By the weekend the whole internet looked like the same wet fart of a stock illustration with slightly different hats. Pristine lighting, perfect anatomy, absolute garbage. Everybody baptized in plastic. And every maker I knew was sitting in a kitchen at 1 a.m. asking the ceiling fan if they were obsolete.

I was right there with them. Six beers deep, staring at a generated picture of what was supposed to be my own character and looked instead like a Pixar reject left in a hot car. I watched the AI bros doing their IT'S OVER victory laps on Twitter and felt the exact cold sweat you did. The sick, sinking thought that you'd just wasted your best years learning a dead language. I tried the doomer pose on. It fit like shit.

So, naturally, I did the healthy thing and built a system around the anxiety.

That system is called anima. The word's Latin for breath, for soul, which sounds precious until you've watched a character's face melt between frames and realized you'd sell a kidney for a little consistency. It's a pipeline for 2D animation run by a human and a fleet of agents, and it lives by one rule: I own the timing, the casting, and the taste. The agents own everything that can be made cheap, parallel, and structured. They propose. They never decide.

The core trick is something I call the Character Bible. Instead of begging the model to understand a character through adjectives, I hand it receipts. A folder of references: an anchor, turnarounds, expressions. You drag the model by its collar until it stops drifting like a drunk on roller skates. Stop describing your character. Start handing the machine evidence. A critic stack of agents proposes fixes. I pick what lives. The one law over all of it, what I call its Engine Truth, is brutally plain: if the loop plays smoothly and the character is recognizably itself in its intended medium, it ships. And anima itself came out of shipping one real animated piece, the Pencil Test.

Somewhere in the middle of building that thing, the fear flipped over. I didn't get replaced. I got promoted to director.

Which is annoying, because "director" sounds classier than it feels. Most days it still feels like standing in a cold garage telling a room full of hyperactive interns to quit making the guy's jaw migrate into his ear. But it is different. The crew never sleeps, never pouts, never asks for a raise. I point, reject, shape, laugh, wince, and say, no, that's not it, the guy looks like he's apologizing to a sandwich.

That is the part I care about.

Pencil & Prompt exists for makers who tried AI once, got the same glossy nothing everybody else got, and walked away sure the whole thing had no soul. I'm not going to tell you you were wrong. You probably weren't. The default taste is oatmeal with a LinkedIn account. But the default is not the ceiling. AI isn't here to replace you. It's an intern with real talent and zero taste, and nobody ever taught it yours. That part's on you now. Your job is to take the little bastard to happy hour, buy it cheap pints on an empty stomach, and show it what good looks like through your eyes, until the work that comes out the other end carries your fingerprints instead of Silicon Valley's.

Every piece here takes one real creative job, starts on the cringe first pass that makes you want to chuck your laptop out a window, and walks it move by move until it has a pulse. I show the bad pass. I show the notes. I show where the machine gets confused, where I get lazy, where the whole mess smells like burnt hair, and the exact move where it finally wakes up. Then I hand you the recipe, chopped up and stripped down, so you can toss it in a pot and make your own stew.

I'm doing this because the kid eating cereal three feet from the TV wanted everyone else making things too. He still does. He's just taller now, and meaner, and he knows where the export settings are.
