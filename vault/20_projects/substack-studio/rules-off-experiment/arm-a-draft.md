# I Fired My Favorite Authors

*They did exactly what I asked. That was the problem.*

I was commuting home on the Staten Island ferry, re-reading Dharma Bums, admiring the way Kerouac keeps a sentence flowing so it can punch you in the face three or four times before it lets you go. Around that same time I'd found Nate B. Jones on YouTube, who has a whole run of episodes on teaching LLMs your personal taste. The two collided somewhere over the harbor and out came the thought. What if I could make Claude write like this?

So I went down the rabbit hole. I picked four of my favorite writers. Jack Kerouac, Hunter S. Thompson, David Sedaris, and Kurt Vonnegut. I researched each one, broke their styles down to the mechanics, and crammed all of it into a single writing skill. The plan was to inject them into my essays, my posts, and anything else that deserved better than the typical AI slop.

The outputs came back cringy and foreign. Not because the models did a bad job. They were doing exactly as instructed. The models can interpret the mechanics and break down how somebody writes, but they can't supply the soul behind the writing. Every draft sounded like everything except ME.

They leaned on Sedaris the hardest, because comedic deflation is how I tend to write anyway. But Sedaris writes about his family, so the jokes kept landing on my mother and my sisters. I'm an only child. I'd be reading a technical essay about agentic workflows and it would end on a warm little callback to hanging out with my Mom.

Kerouac gave me the length without the effect. The sentence kept going and going and never hit anything on the way. Vonnegut sounded like a children's book author floating through the void. Thompson felt like I was injecting caffeine into my veins at 2am with all of my agents cheering me on.

I kept tinkering with it for about three months. Then I started looking into starting a Substack, and that's when I finally broke down. You can shrug off a bad output when it's sitting in a folder. You can't publish one. Everything being sent my way was trying too hard to be elegant, the jokes were painfully unfunny, and none of it had anything to do with me or my actual experiences.

My first move was a half measure. I kept some of each author's tendencies in the skill, trying to hold onto the good parts. The good parts still popped up in the worst ways. So I removed them completely. Deleted the author modes, kept every word I'd written myself, and started fresh. I built a skill chain, injected more rules, fed in my own work, and had Claude run me through writing exercises to get more of me and less of them. Their fingerprints still showed up here and there, which is why I eventually turned to Alex Lieberman's content machine approach, where a draft can only be built from words that came out of my own mouth.

Funny timing on all of this. A few weeks ago, OpenAI quietly stopped ChatGPT from writing in the style of named authors, living or dead. It's a legal move over the copyright suits. The sanctioned workaround is that you can still describe the techniques, you just can't name the writer. People are going to be pretty disappointed with the results either way. No matter how hard you try to have a model copy someone's style and staple it onto your own work, it won't feel right putting it out into the world. I burned three months proving that with the authors fully named.

Here's what I know now that I didn't know on that ferry. The models will break writing down like a math problem, and mechanics can only get you so far in this medium. Becoming a good writer takes time, lived experiences, and sleepless nights spilling your guts onto a keyboard because you need to get it out of you. You start off copying your influences, but as you keep typing, they slowly meld into one cohesive voice that only you could've come up with. Teaching a model to capture that voice works the same way. You can't hand it four ghosts and expect it to conjure you.

So these days the model doesn't imitate anybody. It interrogates me instead. I brain dump an idea into Claude, it invokes a skill called /creative-partner, and it starts grilling me. It digs into everything I've spewed at it, asks questions until the idea gets coherent, and pushes me toward directions that weren't there before. After a few rounds it offers a pivot or two, then hands the final decision back to me. It pulls things out of me instead of pouring other people in.

Kerouac went back on the shelf. He can still punch me in the face any time I open the book. He just doesn't get to swing for me anymore.
