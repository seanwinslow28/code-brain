Audience: one listener, Sean — a product manager with NO engineering background who runs a 12-agent autonomous AI system in production every day.

CALIBRATION FOR THIS MODULE: unlike the previous module, this one is NOT recognition. Say so early and plainly. His fleet has exactly one human in it and that human is him — he is the operator with ground truth, never a user who cannot check the answer. Do NOT tell him he already knows this material and do NOT hunt for flattering parallels. Three things genuinely transfer and they are narrow: (1) his portfolio site falls back to evergreen copy rather than showing a fabricated number when the data is stale, which is an honesty-preserving degradation designed for strangers; (2) his discovery council drops any claim not traceable to a real fetched URL, which is abstention built for machines and never for an interface; (3) he has personally been burned by a confidently-wrong local research run that fabricated software libraries and fabricated Microsoft URLs, which he kept as a specimen — he knows from the inside what an uncalibrated system costs. Everything else here is new: designing for someone who cannot verify, deciding what a system says when it does not know, designing a handoff to another human, putting a reviewer in a loop and thinking about that reviewer in month four, testing an interaction before building the system, and asking who is affected by a system and never touches it. Do NOT teach him what an agent, a harness, a model, an API or a router is.

THE TRAP TO DISARM EARLY: the instinct is "make users trust it more." That is the wrong goal. The goal is CALIBRATION — trust that tracks actual reliability, case by case. Overtrust and undertrust both cost real money.

- DO define every term in plain language on first use: trust calibration, appropriate reliance, overtrust and automation bias, undertrust and algorithm aversion, complementary knowledge, abstention, n-best, categorical versus numeric confidence, context error, failstate, background error, risk versus stakes, escalation contract, human-in-the-loop insertion points, reviewer drift, rubber-stamping, gold cases, inter-rater agreement, anchoring, Wizard of Oz, post-hoc rationalisation, operator/subject/absorber, model card.
- CRITICAL RULE: never mention a company, incident, paper, researcher or tool without a one-sentence setup first explaining what it is and why it is being mentioned. Assume no prior familiarity. This is the single most important instruction.

The M4 lesson is among the selected sources and carries the full detail. Your job is emphasis and sequencing, not transcription. Cover, in this order:

1. Open on the honest diagnosis and do not soften it. His twelve agents run in a world with exactly one human in it; he is the operator with ground truth, not a user. Every hard problem here was pre-solved for him by the fact that he already knew the answer. Name the three narrow transfers from the calibration note, then say plainly that the rest is new.

2. The five relationship pairings as a map of the territory. Set the source up honestly: an April 2025 essay by Ryan Wilson, a design leader, guest-posted on Nate B Jones' newsletter — Jones being a product writer Sean reads. Say plainly it is a practitioner blog post rather than research, that ChatGPT assistance is disclosed, and that it coins terms nobody else uses which he should not repeat in an interview. Then walk the five pairings and their characteristic failure modes. The payoff: his fleet lives entirely in the two rows that contain no human, which is the whole explanation for the gap. Close on the three longitudinal metrics — relational trust, behavioural alignment, friction recovery — and why they differ in kind from task completion.

3. Trust, defined and calibrated. Set up Google's PAIR team — People plus AI Research, which publishes Google's practitioner guidance on human-AI design. Their definition, then the three ingredients: ability, reliability, benevolence. Then both failure directions, overtrust and undertrust, and why each costs money. Land the key line hard: model calibration is a property of the number, trust calibration is a property of the person — you can ship a perfectly calibrated model and still produce badly calibrated users, because the interface rendered every answer in the same confident prose.

4. Communicating uncertainty, told honestly. Setup: in 2020 three IBM researchers — Yunfeng Zhang, Vera Liao and Rachel Bellamy — ran a controlled experiment at a conference on fairness and accountability in computing, with people predicting income levels alongside an AI right about 75 percent of the time. Give all three findings in order, including both null results. Then the product lesson: displaying uncertainty pays off only when the person can do something with it the model cannot do for itself — so the question is never show-or-hide, it is what different action happens at 55 percent versus 85 percent, and if the answer is nothing the number is decoration. Then PAIR's four representations, and four alternatives to a number: abstention, showing evidence instead of a score, deliberately hedged language, and uncertainty expressed as behaviour by asking a clarifying question.

5. Failure UX as a product surface with the same standing as the happy path. Give PAIR's taxonomy sorted by who notices — context errors and failstates, which the user perceives, then happy accidents and BACKGROUND ERRORS, which nobody perceives. Spend real time on background errors: no feedback signal, so they cannot be found through feedback channels and need a dedicated quality-assurance process. That is the bridge into the next module. Then the risk gauge, with risk and stakes as independent axes. Then the three paths forward, including assume-subversive-use — make failure safe, boring and unremarkable, and do not over-explain it, since a spam filter that explains why a message was missed is a tutorial for spammers. Close by connecting to the previous module: every internal stop reason needs a user-facing counterpart, and that mapping table is the failure-UX spec he will write.

6. The anchor — Intercom Fin. Setup: Intercom is a customer-support software company whose product is the messenger and inbox companies use to answer customer questions; Fin is its AI support agent, answering automatically and handing off to a human when it should not. Its full outcome taxonomy is public because Intercom bills on it. Walk the eight outcomes and their prices. Then four takeaways, each labelled observed or inferred out loud, because Sean is being trained to do that. Observed: a stop-reason taxonomy pointed at customers instead of logs. Observed: silence scores as success, and bills. Inferred: a customer who gives up in frustration is indistinguishable from one whose problem was solved — opposite experiences, identical telemetry, a measurement decision with revenue attached. Make the larger point without cynicism: nobody had to act in bad faith; someone chose the cheaper definition of resolved and the incentive gradient did the rest. Caveat honestly that competing vendors publish lower figures and are commercially interested — the lesson needs no number: when the seller defines the metric, read the definition before the figure.

7. The backup anchor, briefly. GitHub Copilot is GitHub's AI coding assistant, suggesting code inline as you type; it instruments accept-or-not. In 2024 a GitHub team led by Albert Ziegler published a study in Communications of the ACM finding acceptance rate predicted developers' PERCEIVED productivity better than other measures. Stress the precision. Accepted code can be wrong; a rejected suggestion may still have helped someone think. Acceptance is the industry's most available interaction metric and it is a proxy — knowing what a metric is a proxy for is most of the job.

8. Human-in-the-loop is four things, not one: pre-action approval, sampled post-hoc review, exception routing, user-invoked escalation. Give what each costs. Then the tie back to module one: the confidence threshold that decides what escalates IS the size of the review queue, so it is a quality decision, a cost decision and a staffing decision at once — and in most organisations three different people own those and never meet. Then what happens to the reviewer, which nobody plans for: automation bias; throughput pressure producing rubber-stamping, where review time falls, agreement rises, and the quality dashboard looks BETTER; drift over months; and contamination back into the data pipeline, since reviewers are usually also the label source, so a degraded loop teaches the model its errors were fine. Give the countermeasures, including treating a rising agreement rate as something to investigate rather than celebrate. Close on the ethnographic point: the reviewer is a user for whom nobody has designed anything.

9. Wizard of Oz. Define it plainly — you build the interface and a human secretly performs the AI's job behind it; decades old in human-computer interaction, named for the man behind the curtain. Everything above is testable before the model exists, because you are testing an interaction, not a capability. Three payoffs, ending on the sharpest: if you cannot write instructions a person can follow, your spec was never a spec.

10. Amershi's eighteen guidelines. Set up the provenance, because that is what makes this citable: in 2019 a Microsoft Research team led by Saleema Amershi, with Eric Horvitz and thirteen others, published Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction at CHI, the main academic conference on human-computer interaction. More than 150 scattered recommendations consolidated to twenty, cut to eighteen, evaluated by forty-nine design practitioners against twenty popular AI products, then expert-reviewed; eight of the eighteen trace to principles Horvitz proposed in 1999. Group them by phase — initially, during interaction, when wrong, over time — and read them with a plain gloss each, briskly. Then: use it as a silent review checklist, never recite it. Finish on the three hardest in a language-model product — guideline two, because no single accuracy number exists for open-ended generation; guideline eleven, because a model can produce a fluent explanation that is not the actual cause, and post-hoc rationalisation manufactures unearned trust; and guideline fourteen, because a model version swap is a user-trust event that teams file as an infrastructure ticket.

11. Close on the design-thinking half — who gets hurt, and why teams ship anyway. Three populations: the operator, usually the only one in the requirements document; the SUBJECT, who the system decides about, often never touches the interface, cannot see or correct it, and bears the largest share of a bad output; and the ABSORBER, who inherits the mess. Then four structural forces, presented without cynicism because the honest answer is not that people are bad: the metric is authored by the party who benefits from it; the counterfactual is never measured; costs land outside the organisation booking the benefit; and launch pressure converts unknowns into assumptions, because "we'll monitor it" is where a missing decision goes to be forgotten. End on the four ownership questions to ask before launch rather than after an incident: who can turn it off and can they at two in the morning without a deploy; who owns a threshold change; who owns the data the system learns from; and when it is wrong in public, whose name is on the statement.

Warm, direct, concrete, unhurried. No hype. Be honest with him that this module asks him to learn something rather than recognise something.
