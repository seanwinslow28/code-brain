Audience: one listener, Sean — a product manager with NO engineering background who runs a 12-agent autonomous AI system in production every day. He builds harnesses, routers, circuit breakers and budget caps by instinct.

CALIBRATION FOR THIS MODULE SPECIFICALLY: he HAS hands-on experience with the mechanics of a data pipeline — he runs an embedding pipeline, he chunks documents, he stores chunk-level provenance, he built quality gates that reject thin articles with logged reasons, and he built a gate that refuses any claim not traceable to a fetched URL. Do NOT teach him what an embedding or a chunk is. He has NOT touched anything involving other humans: labeling by other people, instructions written for strangers, inter-rater agreement, selection effects created by a product own behaviour, feedback that changes the training distribution, or data contracts. Teach that half properly, from the ground up, without flattering him that he already knows it.

- Never explain what an AI agent, a harness, a model or an API is.
- DO define every technical term in plain language on first use: ground truth, labels, inter-rater agreement, labeler drift, datasheet, selection effect, adverse selection, concept drift, distribution shift, degenerate loop, data contract, provenance, freshness, chunking, embedding drift, position-of-gold bias.
- CRITICAL RULE: never mention a company, incident, paper or researcher without a one-sentence setup first explaining what it is and why it is being mentioned. Assume no prior familiarity. This is the single most important instruction.

Cover, in this order:

1. Open by being honest about what he already knows here and what he does not — he has the pipeline mechanics and none of the human layer. Do not oversell either half.
2. Where data comes from: data you already have (cheapest, most dangerous, because it was collected for another purpose and carries its shape), data you go and get, and data your product generates by running. Note Google PAIR research group's point that teams spend more on model development than data quality and that this allocation is backwards.
3. LABELING, taught properly and at length, because this is his largest genuine gap. Someone has to say what the right answer was. Instructions are a product and yours are ambiguous — walk through the support-ticket example where "urgent" means three different things to three labelers, and the model learns the average of three incompatible definitions. Inter-rater agreement as a measurable ceiling: if two careful humans agree 70% of the time, no model trained on those labels beats 70%, and low agreement is a SPECIFICATION bug, not a labeler problem. Labeler drift and fatigue. How the choice of who labels changes what "correct" means.
4. Datasheets for datasets: set it up properly — a 2018 paper by Timnit Gebru and colleagues proposed that every dataset ship with documentation the way electronic components ship with a datasheet stating operating characteristics. What it answers and why it fixes most of the above.
5. THE CENTRAL IDEA — selection effects. Set up Zillow properly: Zillow is an American real-estate listings company; from 2018 it ran a house-flipping business called Zillow Offers that used an algorithm to make instant cash offers on homes; it shut that business in November 2021 and laid off about a quarter of its workforce. Then the mechanism, slowly, because it is the most valuable idea in the module: the algorithm made an offer and THE SELLER CHOSE whether to accept. Offers that were too generous got accepted; offers that were too low got declined. So the homes Zillow actually bought were systematically enriched for the cases where its algorithm was wrong in the expensive direction. Its own purchasing behaviour filtered reality into a biased sample. Name this adverse selection, borrowed from insurance — and be explicit that the academic case study in the sources analyses the closure WITHOUT using that term, so the mechanism is in the facts and the framing is borrowed. Add concept drift as the compounding second cause. Land the generalisation hard: if your product's behaviour decides which data comes back, your data is not a sample of the world, it is a sample of your product's mistakes. Give the other instances — a recommender only learns about what it recommended, a fraud model only learns outcomes for what it let through.
6. Feedback loops: the deliberate kind that works, and three degenerate kinds — training on your own output, popularity mistaken for quality, and the most common failure of all, the thumbs-up nobody routes anywhere. Give the test: what decision does this signal change, who sees it, how long until it takes effect.
7. Retrieval as data infrastructure. A peer-reviewed 2026 taxonomy presented at TrustNLP, a workshop on trustworthy natural-language processing, catalogues 33 failure modes across seven pipeline stages, each graded by evidence strength. Cover stale data, layout parsing errors, chunking boundary errors as a product decision disguised as a technical parameter, embedding drift, and position-of-gold bias. Then the finding worth carrying into a room: 12 of the 33 modes have no dedicated peer-reviewed evidence and all 8 agentic modes are among them — the paper calls it an evidence desert. Saying "I would instrument that rather than assume it, because the evidence is thin there" is a more senior move than reciting a confident mitigation.
8. Close on data contracts, provenance and freshness, connecting each back to something he already built — chunk-level provenance in his vault index, the URL traceability gate in his research council, the staleness checks in his synthesizer. He has been doing three of these by instinct.

Warm, direct, concrete, unhurried. No hype.
