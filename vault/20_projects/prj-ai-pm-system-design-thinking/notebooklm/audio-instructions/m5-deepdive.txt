The M5 lesson is among the selected sources and carries the full detail. Your job is emphasis and sequencing, not transcription. Cover, in this order:

1. Open on the partial mirror, drawn sharply. Name what he built — instrumentation, a rejection-logging quality gate, a scheduled automated critic, projecting cost caps, and above all the real launch gate with numeric thresholds and a named human sign-off that he built for his own website. Then land the distinction that carries the module: cost control is not unit economics, he is excellent at the first and has never done the second. Then list what is genuinely new.

2. Instrumentation and the unit of analysis. Set up the source: Hamel Husain is a machine-learning engineer who consults on production language-model products, and his writing is the spine of this module. Define a trace plainly. Quote his rule — you must remove all friction from the process of looking at data — and his point that teams with a domain-specific trace viewer iterate roughly ten times faster. Then the unit of analysis: the thing you count, per turn or per conversation or per task, and why that choice decides everything downstream. Tie it to the previous module: Intercom's Fin counts conversations, which is why a customer who gives up quietly and one who was helped land in the same bucket. Then the two sampling jobs — random tells you the rate and is the only thing that can; error-weighted is where the learning is and is a biased rate estimate by construction. You need both.

3. Error analysis, and spend the most time here of anywhere in the episode, because it is the highest-leverage thing he can do and it requires no engineering. Quote Husain: the people best positioned to improve your AI system are often the ones who know the least about AI. And: generic metrics are worse than useless, they actively impede progress. Then the method, naming its provenance — it is the coding process from grounded theory, how qualitative researchers build a taxonomy out of interview transcripts. Open coding: read traces one at a time, write a free-form note, no categories yet; this step requires domain knowledge. Axial coding: after thirty to fifty hand-coded notes, group them into five to ten failure modes, with a language model helping only after the hand-coding. Then count frequencies and prioritise by frequency times cost — the first module's error economics applied to real data at last. Give his reported numbers, labelled honestly as consulting write-ups rather than controlled studies: three issues accounting for over sixty percent of all problems, and one fix moving date handling from thirty-three percent to ninety-five. Close on the two structural outputs — the taxonomy becomes your eval categories, the labelled traces become the golden dataset, and neither can be bought. This is also the only real answer to the previous module's background errors.

4. Invariants, golden datasets, holdout hygiene. Give the cost ladder — level one cheap assertions every change, level two human and model evaluation on a cadence, level three a live A/B test only after major changes — and the rule that cost of three exceeds two exceeds one, which dictates cadence. Note Rechat runs hundreds of level-one tests and that test inputs can be synthesised rather than waiting for traffic. Then the line worth memorising: your pass rate is a product decision — the first module's threshold lesson in engineering clothes. Then how the holdout rots: every prompt fix that targets a specific eval case makes that case stop measuring, and eval examples leak into prompts as few-shot examples, so the model has now seen the test.

5. LLM-as-judge, in two halves. First the good method: find THE principal domain expert, one person, because a committee produces contradictory labels and you cannot align a judge to a contradiction — and Husain is blunt that developers acting as their own domain expert is a recipe for disaster. Collect written critiques, not scores, detailed enough that a new employee could understand them, and use them as few-shot examples. Start with about thirty examples and continue until no new failure modes appear. Then the measurement sentence that separates competent from not: using raw agreement is misleading when classes are imbalanced, so measure precision and recall separately — if ninety-five percent of outputs are fine, a judge that always says fine scores ninety-five percent agreement and is worthless. In his Honeycomb case, three iterations reached over ninety percent agreement, and he is explicit that alignment must be rechecked periodically. Second half, the failure modes, from a continuously-revised academic survey of judging with language models, so date it: position bias, the best evidenced by far, where the judge favours whichever answer it sees first; length bias; self-enhancement, where a judge prefers its own model family; and concreteness bias, where answers full of specific numbers and citations score higher whether or not the specifics are true — sit with that one, because it rewards the exact surface features of a confident hallucination. Then Goodhart's law, plainly: when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure. Applied as weak-judge Goodharting — optimise against a judge weaker than your system and you optimise toward the judge's biases, so the eval score climbs and the product does not. Land on: a judge is a measuring instrument, and an uncalibrated instrument is not a cheap one, it is a decorative one.

6. The anchor. Set it up: Rechat is a software company for real-estate agents, and Lucy is its conversational AI assistant, built to replace clicking through a CRM with a conversation; Husain worked on it and wrote the story up. Early progress from prompt engineering, then a plateau, with three symptoms — fixing one failure mode made others appear, described as a game of whack-a-mole; no visibility into performance across tasks beyond subjective impressions; and prompts growing into long unwieldy forms trying to cover every edge case. Then the diagnosis, which is the point: those three symptoms together mean the team had no measurement, so every fix was a blind bet and a fix that broke something else was indistinguishable from one that worked. The escape was not a better model. It was hundreds of unit tests and a systematic evaluation loop. Say the implication out loud: the plateau is not a capability ceiling, and reaching for a bigger model when you hit it is the most expensive available way to avoid writing an eval.

7. The ML Test Score, and stress that this is the rubric to steal. Set it up: in 2017 Eric Breck, Shanqing Cai, Eric Nielsen, Michael Salib and D. Sculley at Google published it at an academic conference on big data — the same Sculley as the technical-debt paper from the architecture module. Twenty-eight tests in four sections of seven: data, model, infrastructure, monitoring. Then the scoring design, which is the part worth stealing: half a point for running a test manually with results documented, a full point if it runs automatically on a repeated basis, and the final score is the MINIMUM of the four sections rather than the total — because a system is exactly as production-ready as its weakest area. Their scale bottoms out at "more of a research project than a productionised system." They interviewed thirty-six teams across Google, and one of them, asked a routine question, discovered a thousand-line completely untested file generating their input features. Then the rows that matter most for a language-model product, especially the one everyone skips: offline proxy metrics must correlate with actual online impact — your eval score is a proxy, and has anyone checked that moving it moves anything a user or the business feels? Also quality on all important data slices, staleness, canary, fast rollback, and monitoring for dependency changes and quality regression on served data. Be honest that it is pre-language-model, that roughly a third of the rows do not map, and that knowing which third is the calibrated read.

8. Rollout — five stages between "it works" and "everyone has it": shadow mode, where the new version runs on real traffic with output logged and never shown, absurdly underused; canary; champion versus challenger; the A/B test, the only thing establishing causation; and the kill switch, a config flag rather than a deploy. Three things teams get wrong: randomise the right unit, usually the user not the request; decide in advance, in writing and in numbers, what would make you stop — and note he already does exactly this on his website build; and rollback is a time budget, not a capability, so the question is how long it takes, who can do it, and whether anyone ever has.

9. Drift, four kinds. Covariate shift — inputs change, the relationship does not. Label shift — the base rate changes, so a threshold tuned to the old rate is now wrong. Concept drift — the relationship itself changes, same input with a different correct answer, and the nastiest because nothing about the data looks different. And the language-model-native fourth: the model changes underneath you when a provider deprecates a version. Then the honest difficulty: the strongest signal, prediction quality on served data, is usually the one you cannot measure because served data has no labels — which is why the earlier sampling and error analysis are the only ground truth you will have about live behaviour.

10. Unit economics at the product level. Not cost per call — cost per successful outcome, held next to what that outcome is worth. Explain why the shape differs from classic software: traditional software ran eighty to ninety percent gross margins because serving one more user cost almost nothing, while AI serving cost scales with usage. Then the survey, set up properly: ICONIQ Growth invests in software companies and publishes an annual survey of them; it surveyed roughly three hundred executives building AI products, fieldwork April and December 2025, published under a 2026 title — mention that gap. Expected gross margins around fifty-two percent on average in 2026; fifty-eight percent keep a subscription component, thirty-five percent charge on consumption, eighteen percent have moved to outcome-based pricing, thirty-seven percent plan to change pricing within twelve months. Flag the tier honestly: a venture firm surveying a population that includes its own portfolio, self-reported and projected. Then the whiteboard calculation: define the unit as the thing the customer values; count everything one unit consumes INCLUDING failed attempts that still cost money and human review minutes on escalations; put revenue beside it; then ask the margin, which direction it moves as usage grows, and at what usage level a customer becomes unprofitable. Note routing is the biggest lever, and that he built a router and never pointed it at a margin. Close the section on the trap that links back to the previous module: the moment you price per outcome, the definition of "outcome" becomes a revenue decision — which is precisely what assumed resolution was.

11. Close on ownership and incidents. The four ownership questions answered rather than asked: who can disable it and how fast, who approves a threshold change, who owns the data it learns from, and whose name is on the public statement. Review boards are useful when they have a decision right and a written standard, and theatre otherwise — the test being whether the board has ever said no. Then what makes an AI incident different: you often cannot reproduce it, so capturing the trace is the first job; the fix is usually a prompt, a threshold or a document rather than code, and those often move through no review at all; and "the model got it wrong" is not a root cause, it is the observation. End on the postmortem's two questions — what would have caught this earlier, and which test gets added today — which closes the loop, because every incident should end by growing the golden dataset.
