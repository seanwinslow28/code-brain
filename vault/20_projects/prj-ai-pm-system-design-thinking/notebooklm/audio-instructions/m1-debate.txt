Audience: one listener, Sean — a product manager with no engineering background who runs a 12-agent AI system in production daily. Never explain what an agent or a model is. DO define statistical terms plainly on first use. CRITICAL: never mention a company, incident, or paper without a one-sentence setup first.

Format: a genuine DEBATE, argued honestly and forcefully from both sides, with no premature resolution.

The motion: "SHIP IT AT 85%."

A team has an AI feature measured at roughly 85% accuracy. The launch window is now.

Side A argues SHIP: real-world value is delivered now rather than theoretically later; the non-AI status quo it replaces is itself error-prone and nobody audits human error rates the way they audit model error rates; you learn more from production in two weeks than from six months of offline evaluation; you can bound the damage with a narrow scope, a human review step, and a rollback trigger; and waiting has its own cost that never appears on anyone's dashboard.

Side B argues DO NOT SHIP: 85% of what, measured on which distribution, and how does that decompose into the two error types — because if the 15% falls entirely on the expensive error, the headline number is a lie of omission. Nobody has stated who bears the cost of a wrong answer or whether they have any recourse. There is no error budget agreed in advance, so degradation will produce an argument rather than a decision. And once it ships, the throughput metric will arrive daily while the quality signal arrives in two months, which means the incentive to look closely disappears the moment it launches.

Have them genuinely engage: Side A should press on the cost of delay and on the double standard applied to human error; Side B should press on the fact that "85%" without an error breakdown is not yet a number you can reason about.

Land, at the end, not on a winner but on what would settle it — the specific facts that turn this from an argument into a decision. Make clear that the ability to name those facts, rather than to pick a side, is what competence looks like here.

Two distinct voices, real disagreement, no strawmen.
