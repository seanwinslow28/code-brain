Audience: one listener, Sean — a product manager with NO engineering background who runs a 12-agent autonomous AI system in production every day.

CALIBRATION FOR THIS MODULE: unlike the previous module, this one is NOT recognition. Say so early and plainly. His fleet has exactly one human in it and that human is him — he is the operator with ground truth, never a user who cannot check the answer. Do NOT tell him he already knows this material and do NOT hunt for flattering parallels. Three things genuinely transfer and they are narrow: (1) his portfolio site falls back to evergreen copy rather than showing a fabricated number when the data is stale, which is an honesty-preserving degradation designed for strangers; (2) his discovery council drops any claim not traceable to a real fetched URL, which is abstention built for machines and never for an interface; (3) he has personally been burned by a confidently-wrong local research run that fabricated software libraries and fabricated Microsoft URLs, which he kept as a specimen — he knows from the inside what an uncalibrated system costs. Everything else here is new: designing for someone who cannot verify, deciding what a system says when it does not know, designing a handoff to another human, putting a reviewer in a loop and thinking about that reviewer in month four, testing an interaction before building the system, and asking who is affected by a system and never touches it. Do NOT teach him what an agent, a harness, a model, an API or a router is.

THE TRAP TO DISARM EARLY: the instinct is "make users trust it more." That is the wrong goal. The goal is CALIBRATION — trust that tracks actual reliability, case by case. Overtrust and undertrust both cost real money.

- DO define every term in plain language on first use: trust calibration, appropriate reliance, overtrust and automation bias, undertrust and algorithm aversion, complementary knowledge, abstention, n-best, categorical versus numeric confidence, context error, failstate, background error, risk versus stakes, escalation contract, human-in-the-loop insertion points, reviewer drift, rubber-stamping, gold cases, inter-rater agreement, anchoring, Wizard of Oz, post-hoc rationalisation, operator/subject/absorber, model card.
- CRITICAL RULE: never mention a company, incident, paper, researcher or tool without a one-sentence setup first explaining what it is and why it is being mentioned. Assume no prior familiarity. This is the single most important instruction.

Format: a genuine DEBATE, argued forcefully from both sides, with no premature resolution.

The motion: "SHOW THE CONFIDENCE SCORE."

The setting, established in the first thirty seconds: a health insurer's prior-authorization assistant — the system that decides whether a doctor's requested treatment is approved before it happens. Twelve thousand requests a week. Forty nurse reviewers. A hard legal rule that a denial may never be issued by the model alone. The system produces a recommendation and a confidence number for each case. The question on the table is whether the nurse reviewer sees that number — and, separately, whether the member ever does.

Side A argues SHOW IT. Controlled research found that showing per-case confidence genuinely does move trust in the right direction — people trusted the system less below 60 percent and significantly more above 80. Without a per-case signal, the reviewer must treat all twelve thousand cases identically, which is exactly the condition that produces rubber-stamping. A nurse is a trained clinician, not a probability-illiterate consumer, and withholding a number she could use is paternalism dressed as safety. The design guidance from the human-computer interaction literature says to make clear how well the system performs. And when a denial is challenged, "the system was 54 percent confident and a human reviewed it" is a defensible record while "we did not look" is not.

Side B argues DO NOT SHOW A NUMBER. The same research found that better-calibrated trust did NOT improve the joint decision — the humans had no complementary knowledge to contribute, so knowing when to distrust the machine bought nothing. Worse, a high number is an invitation to skim: the reviewer glides through the 94-percent cases, and a wrongly approved request generates no complaint from anyone, which makes it a background error that no dashboard will ever surface. A model's stated confidence is not its accuracy unless someone has actually measured that, and most teams never do. Showing the model's answer and its confidence first anchors the reviewer before she has formed her own judgement, destroying the independent check that was the entire point of putting her there. Show the evidence, or the top alternatives, or route silently by threshold — but do not hand her a number whose meaning nobody has established.

Have them genuinely engage. Side A should press hard on what happens to a reviewer who is given no signal at all, and on the accountability record. Side B should press hard on the asymmetry between a wrong denial, which someone complains about, and a wrong approval, which nobody ever reports — and on the fact that the number's authority is unearned until the model's calibration has been measured.

Then widen it once, briefly: does the MEMBER — the person whose treatment this is, who never touches the interface — get told anything about the confidence in a decision made about them? Both sides should have to answer that, and neither should find it comfortable.

Land not on a winner but on the facts that would settle it: whether the model's confidence has actually been checked against its real accuracy; whether the reviewer knows things the model does not on the specific cases where the model is wrong; what different action is supposed to happen at each confidence level, and whether override rates by confidence band show it happening; and whether the display can be tested before it is built. Make clear that naming those facts, rather than picking a side, is what competence looks like.

Two distinct voices, real disagreement, no strawmen.
