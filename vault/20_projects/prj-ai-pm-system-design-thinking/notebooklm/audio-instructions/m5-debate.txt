Audience: one listener, Sean — a product manager with NO engineering background who runs a 12-agent autonomous AI system in production every day.

CALIBRATION FOR THIS MODULE: this is a PARTIAL mirror, so draw the line sharply and do not blur it in either direction. What he HAS built, and you should name: instrumentation (health ledgers and nightly manifests separate from the conversation), a quality gate that emits its rejection reasons rather than only recording passes, an automated nightly critic running over another agent's output on a schedule, hierarchical cost caps that project spend and refuse before the call, and — the strongest one — a real launch gate with written numeric thresholds and a named human sign-off, which he built for his personal website by instinct: three browser engines green, a seven-route sweep at 7 of 7, a 95th-percentile frame budget under 16.7 milliseconds, a one-pixel settle tolerance, a pinned byte budget, and the rule that nothing merges until he has personally wheeled through every page. THE DISTINCTION THAT CARRIES THE MODULE: cost control is not unit economics. A cap stops a bill; unit economics asks whether the thing makes money when it works. He is genuinely good at the first and has never once done the second — there is no revenue side to his fleet, no cost per successful outcome, no margin. Say that plainly.

- CRITICAL RULE: never mention a company, incident, paper, researcher or tool without a one-sentence setup first explaining what it is and why it is being mentioned. Assume no prior familiarity. This is the single most important instruction.

Format: a genuine DEBATE, argued forcefully from both sides, with no premature resolution.

The motion: "USE AN LLM JUDGE."

The setting, established in the first thirty seconds: a B2B software company has shipped an AI agent that resolves inbound support tickets. Thirty thousand tickets a month. They charge customers one dollar per resolved ticket, so the definition of "resolved" is also the invoice. They need to know, continuously, whether the agent's answers are actually good. They can pay humans to read a sample, or they can have another language model grade every single one.

Side A argues USE THE JUDGE. Nobody can human-review thirty thousand tickets a month, and a sample of a hundred gives error bars wide enough to hide the five-point regression you actually care about. A judge is cheap enough to run on every prompt change, which converts evaluation from a quarterly event into a gate that blocks a bad deploy. The practitioner method for building one is well documented and works — collect written critiques from a single domain expert, use them as few-shot examples, iterate, and three rounds can reach over ninety percent agreement. And the alternative is not pristine: human reviewers under throughput pressure rubber-stamp, drift over months, and anchor on whatever the system already said, which the previous module covered in detail. A calibrated judge is more consistent than an uncalibrated human panel with no rubric.

Side B argues KEEP HUMANS IN THE MEASUREMENT. The judge's number is only ever as good as the set of human labels it was aligned against, so you never escape humans — you only decide how few you use and how honest you are about it. The documented biases are exactly wrong for this domain: a judge that favours longer answers, answers that appear first, answers from its own model family, and answers stuffed with specific-sounding numbers is a judge that rewards a confident hallucination, which is the single worst failure mode in customer support. Weak-judge Goodharting means the team can spend a quarter improving the eval score while the product does not move. Raw agreement hides class imbalance — if ninety-five percent of answers are fine, a judge that always says "fine" reports ninety-five percent agreement and measures nothing. And the sharpest point: the company bills per resolution, so an automated grader that is biased toward saying "resolved" is not an efficiency, it is a conflict of interest with a revenue number attached.

Have them genuinely engage. Side A should press hard on the arithmetic — what a human-only regime actually detects at feasible sample sizes, and what it costs to detect a real regression. Side B should press hard on the fact that nobody in the argument has yet said whether this particular judge has ever been checked against a person, and on the billing conflict, which does not go away by making the judge better.

Land not on a winner but on the facts that would settle it: whether the judge has been validated against a named domain expert with precision and recall reported separately rather than raw agreement; what the class balance actually is; whether the judge comes from a different model family than the generator; whether anyone has tested it for position and length sensitivity by rerunning the same pairs in a different order; how large a human sample the specific decision actually requires; and who audits the grader when the grade is the invoice. Make clear that naming those facts, rather than picking a side, is what competence looks like.

Two distinct voices, real disagreement, no strawmen.
