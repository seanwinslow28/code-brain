Audience: one listener, Sean — a product manager with NO engineering background who runs a 12-agent autonomous AI system in production daily. He built routers, circuit breakers, fail-closed cost guards and budget caps by instinct, without knowing their names. The gap is vocabulary and formal structure, NOT hands-on experience — but do not over-flatter him either: eliciting error costs from a stakeholder, setting an error budget, calibration, and class imbalance are genuinely new to him.

Therefore:
- NEVER explain what an agent, harness, model, or API is.
- DO define every statistical term in plain language on first use.
- CRITICAL RULE: never mention a company, incident, paper, or researcher without a one-sentence setup first, explaining what it is and why it is relevant. Assume no prior familiarity. A previous version of this material failed exactly here.

Cover, in this order:
1. The question that comes before architecture: does this need a probabilistic system at all? What is the non-AI baseline, and what must AI beat? Introduce the "stochastic tax" — the ongoing operating burden a probabilistic system imposes even when nothing is broken — and note it comes from a 2026 research preprint, so it is fresh thinking rather than settled canon.
2. Automate versus augment, and why that is a product decision rather than an engineering one.
3. Error economics as the heart of the module: false positives and false negatives are almost never equally expensive, and a single accuracy number destroys the only information that matters. Use the spam filter as the running example throughout — a false negative is junk in the inbox, five seconds of annoyance; a false positive is a real message silently quarantined, and if it was a job offer the cost is unbounded AND invisible. Then invert it with cancer screening to show the same two errors with opposite costs.
4. Where the decision physically lives: the threshold. You cannot reduce both errors at once; you choose which one you would rather have. This is a product manager's decision and is most often left by default to whoever writes the code.
5. Turning diagnosis into disposition — threshold with reasoning, error budget with a number, kill criteria, and somewhere the number is actually visible. Stress that a system design answer ending at "and then we would measure it" is unfinished.
6. Who gets hurt and why teams ship anyway. When the person harmed is not the operator, the incentive loop is broken by construction. Name the organizational reasons known-flawed systems ship: launch dates, throughput metrics, quality signals arriving months after ship signals.
7. The anchor: Amazon's résumé screening tool. Set it up properly — in 2014 Amazon began building an internal tool to score job applicants automatically; Reuters reporter Jeffrey Dastin revealed in October 2018 that it had been scrapped. It was trained on ten years of past résumés, most from men, and it learned that preference; it reportedly penalised the word "women's". Run the module's questions against it. Be explicit that Reuters' account is single-sourced, Amazon disputed that it was ever used to evaluate candidates, and the details are not independently verifiable — then point out that separating what is observed from what is inferred is itself the skill being taught.

End by connecting it back: he already made a precision-over-recall decision when he built a gate that drops any research finding not traceable to a real fetched URL. He chose to lose true findings rather than publish fabricated ones. He has never called it that. Now he can.

Warm, direct, concrete, unhurried. No hype.
