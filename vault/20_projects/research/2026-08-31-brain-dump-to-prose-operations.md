# The missing layer: named operations between the brain dump and the piece

Companion to [[2026-08-28-story-hooks-and-narrative-through-line]] — that note owns openings, orientation, through-line, scene/summary, and abandonment evidence; none of it is re-covered here. This note owns what happens *to the words*: the drafting/revision operations, the spoken-to-written register shift, and sentence-level "written-ness." Same evidence tags: **[science]** = empirical/peer-reviewed, **[craft]** = established craft teaching, **[lore]** = practitioner folklore, **[analysis]** = construction from sources, flagged so it never gets cited as if a source said it.

The one-line answer to the run-#3 question ("what does a human writer do between the brain dump and the finished piece"): the pass between brain dump and finished piece is not one operation. Composition research and craft literature name roughly ten, and they sort into three tiers — **reorganizing for the reader** (the big one the current drafts skip), **register conversion** (speech to writing, measurable), and **sentence work** (rhythm, syntax, compression). The drafts read as competently assembled but dead because the machine is doing tier 3 lightly and tiers 1–2 not at all — an interview transcript organized by topic is, in Linda Flower's exact vocabulary, *writer-based prose shipped to a reader*.

---

## 1. The named operations, ranked by leverage for this system

### 1. Knowledge-transforming (vs. knowledge-telling) — Bereiter & Scardamalia [science]

The umbrella finding, and the precise diagnosis of "organized brain dump." Bereiter & Scardamalia (1987) showed novice writers **knowledge-tell**: retrieve content from memory cued by topic and genre, and write it down in retrieval order. Experts **knowledge-transform**: they run a dialectic between a *content problem space* (what do I believe? is it consistent?) and a *rhetorical problem space* (what does this reader need, in what order, framed how?), and the content itself changes under rhetorical pressure — writing generates new knowledge rather than transcribing existing knowledge. Crucially, knowledge-transforming is "a radical change in how the writing task is defined," not a polish applied afterward. An interview transcript is knowledge-telling in its purest form; a pipeline that reorders and smooths it is *organized* knowledge-telling. ([Scribd summary](https://www.scribd.com/document/358307209/The-Bereiter-and-Scardamalia-Model-of-Writing-Process); [ResearchGate diagram/discussion](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Bereiter-Scardamalias-1987-knowledge-transforming-model-of-writing_fig1_253418546). Cited via secondary summaries; the 1987 book *The Psychology of Written Composition* has no legal full text online.)

### 2. Writer-based → reader-based transformation — Linda Flower [science] (protocol-study-grounded theory)

The operation with the best name for the pipeline. Flower ("Writer-Based Prose: A Cognitive Basis for Problems in Writing," 1979; "Revising Writer-Based Prose," JBW 1981): writer-based prose is "the record and working of [the writer's] own verbal thought" — egocentric focus, **narrative or survey structure that replays the discovery process** ("and then I looked at… another thing I noticed…"). Reader-based prose replaces the discovery sequence with an **issue-centered rhetorical structure** built around what the reader needs. Flower's two named sub-operations: (a) **reorganize around the point, not the process** — turn "how I found out" order into "why it matters" order; (b) **transform private language into shared language** — expand saturated personal shorthand into terms carrying meaning for a cold reader (connects directly to the companion note's F2.2 given-new finding). She insists this is "a necessary, specific and learnable skill" — a discrete pass, not a talent. A spoken interview answer is writer-based prose *by construction*: it replays the speaker's retrieval order. ([Flower 1979 PDF](https://mwover.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/flower-writer-based-prose.pdf); [Flower, "Revising Writer-Based Prose" PDF](https://wac.colostate.edu/jbw/v3n3/flower.pdf); [Writing Commons overview](https://writingcommons.org/section/style/writing-styles/reader-based-prose-style/))

### 3. Global (meaning) revision vs. surface revision — Sommers; Faigley & Witte [science]

Two classic empirical studies define what "revision" means when experts do it. Sommers (1980) compared student and experienced adult writers: students revised by **rewording** — they "perceive words as the unit of discourse" and treat revision as an afterthought; experienced writers revised by **"finding the form or shape of their argument"** — a recursive process with "different levels of attention and different agenda for each cycle." Faigley & Witte ("Analyzing Revision," CCC 1981) built the standard taxonomy: **surface changes** (formal edits + meaning-preserving paraphrase) vs. **meaning changes** (microstructure and macrostructure changes that alter the text's actual content/structure), and showed expert revision is disproportionately meaning-level. Pipeline implication: a grammar-and-flow pass over a transcript is 100% surface change; the operations that make writing feel written are the meaning-level ones. ([Sommers PDF](https://fyse2015.files.wordpress.com/2015/09/sommers_revision.pdf); [Faigley & Witte, NCTE](https://publicationsncte.org/content/journals/10.58680/ccc198115887); [taxonomy summary](https://datadatabase.wordpress.com/2011/03/11/faigley-and-witte-revision-taxonomy/))

### 4. Internal revision vs. external revision — Donald Murray [craft]

Murray ("Internal Revision: A Process of Discovery," 1978) splits revision into two passes with different readers. **Internal revision**: the writer reads the draft to *discover what they actually have to say* — audience of one. **External revision**: the writer re-reads *as the audience*, editing for how the ideas land. His slogan: writing is rewriting; the first draft is a "discovery draft." The transferable point: there are two distinct read-throughs with different questions ("what am I really saying?" then "how does this land?"), and collapsing them into one pass loses the first entirely. ([CUNY OpenLab summary](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/schmerlereng1121e106spring2019/2019/05/17/donald-murray-internal-revision-a-process-of-discovery-3/); [Wikipedia, writing process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_process))

### 5. Cooking and growing — Peter Elbow [craft]

Elbow (*Writing Without Teachers*, 1973) supplies the generative half: **freewriting** (uncensored production — the interview transcript already is this), then **growing** (getting words "to evolve through a series of stages" — a chaotic draft develops a **center of gravity**, and the writer reorganizes around the emergent center, often discarding the original frame), and **cooking** (the interaction of contrasting ideas/frames that generates new material). The operational core of "growing": *find the sentence the draft was secretly trying to write, promote it, and rebuild around it.* That is a nameable pipeline step — the transcript's best-realized idea is rarely its stated topic. ([OUP page](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/writing-without-teachers-9780195120165); [Elbow freewriting excerpt PDF](http://mjreiff.com/ELBOW.pdf); [analytical summary](https://mrandersonwrites.wordpress.com/2015/08/17/x-y-peter-elbows-still-revolutionary-developmental-approach-to-writing/))

### 6. Down draft / up draft / dental draft — Anne Lamott [craft, borderline lore]

*Bird by Bird* (1994): the **down draft** (get it down), the **up draft** (fix it up — "you try to say what you have to say more accurately"), the **dental draft** ("check every tooth, to see if it's loose or cramped or decayed"). Less operationally precise than Flower/Sommers, but the most widely shared vocabulary for the same three-tier structure: produce → restructure → tune. The interview stage of the content machine is the down draft; the current pipeline jumps from down draft to dental draft. ([Full text PDF, U. Kentucky](https://wrd.as.uky.edu/sites/default/files/1-Shitty%20First%20Drafts.pdf))

### 7. The cohesion/coherence pass — Joseph Williams [craft, with psycholinguistic support]

*Style: Toward Clarity and Grace* (1990) names the most mechanical, most automatable operations on this list: (a) **characters as subjects** — put the story's actors in the grammatical subject slot; (b) **actions as verbs** — un-bury nominalized actions ("made a decision" → "decided"); (c) **old-to-new information flow** — begin sentences with information the reader already has, end with the new and most stressworthy (the sentence-level twin of the given-new contract at the companion note's F2.2); (d) **cohesion vs. coherence** — sentence-to-sentence linkage is separate from whole-passage unity, and both must be checked separately. The closest thing in the literature to a deterministic revision algorithm. ([Full text PDF, Williams & Bizup edition](https://www.clc.hcmus.edu.vn/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/Style_-_Joseph_M._Williams_Joseph_Bizup.pdf); [DeLong's chapter summary](https://www.bradford-delong.com/2006/10/williams_style_.html); [principles digest](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-joseph-m-williams-joseph-bizup/))

### 8. Compression: clutter-pruning and greening — Zinsser; McPhee [craft]

Zinsser (*On Writing Well*): "the secret of good writing is to strip every sentence to its cleanest components"; "rewriting is the essence of writing." McPhee (*Draft No. 4*, "Omission") gives compression its best-named exercise: **greening** — the *Time*-magazine proof instruction "Green 5" meant delete five lines' worth *without the reader ever noticing anything is gone*, which forces the discovery that most drafts contain 10–15% ballast at sentence level. His larger principle: "what you leave out is as important as what you put in" (kin to Le Guin's leaping at the companion note's F4.3, but at word/line grain rather than beat grain). ([LitCharts on Zinsser's clutter chapters](https://www.litcharts.com/lit/on-writing-well/themes/simplicity-vs-clutter); [Nieman Storyboard on Draft No. 4](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2017/12/21/draft-no-4-the-legendary-john-mcphees-master-class-in-the-writers-craft/); [PopMatters review covering greening](https://www.popmatters.com/john-mcphees-draft-no-4-provides-easygoing-wisdom-for-readers-and-writers-2504899586.html))

### 9. The rhythm pass — Le Guin; Klinkenborg [craft]

Le Guin (*Steering the Craft*, "The Sound of Your Writing"): prose is heard; "the rhythm of prose depends very much — very prosaically — on the length of the sentences," a sentence's first responsibility is coherence and "their second is rhythm," and "the changing sentence rhythms embody and express the emotions" of the material. Operationally: read aloud, vary length deliberately, let rhythm carry emphasis. Klinkenborg (*Several Short Sentences About Writing*, 2012) adds the sharpest transcript-relevant tools: (a) **shorten until each sentence carries one thing**, because "the longer the sentence, the less it's able to imply" — and **implication** ("the ability to suggest more than the words seem to allow") is what dead prose lacks; (b) **cut transitions** — "if you've written clearly… the reader will never get lost… without transitions," a direct inversion of transcript-smoothing, which *adds* connective tissue ("so," "and then," "basically") instead of deleting it; (c) test every sentence — "only revision will tell you whether a sentence that offers itself is worth keeping." ([NPR interview with Le Guin](https://www.npr.org/2015/08/29/435549081/ursula-k-le-guin-steers-her-craft-into-a-new-century); [Medium on Le Guin's sentence-length teaching](https://medium.com/%C3%A7at%C4%B1-kat%C4%B1/sentence-length-and-variation-97deefcb849b); [Klinkenborg quotes, Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/18333546-several-short-sentences-about-writing); [Sivers' notes](https://sive.rs/book/ShortSentences))

### 10. Syntax architecture: cumulative and periodic sentences — Christensen [craft, with classroom-experiment support]

Christensen ("A Generative Rhetoric of the Sentence," 1963): the **cumulative sentence** — a short base clause plus **free modifiers** trailing after it, each adding texture/detail/qualification, giving "a flowing and ebbing movement." Its mirror, the **periodic sentence**, withholds the main clause to the end for suspense and weight. The craft rule: cumulative mimics natural speech and observation; periodic builds to revelation; variety between them (plus plain short sentences) is what makes syntax feel *composed* rather than transcribed. Classroom experiments with the Christensen method showed significant writing-quality gains. Note the irony relevant to run #3: speech is *not* actually cumulative in Christensen's sense — spoken clauses chain with "and/so"; the written cumulative packs subordinate free modifiers around a base, which is precisely the "integration" Chafe measures (§2). ([ERIC study PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED491490.pdf); [generative rhetoric overview](http://ustudies.semo.edu/gaskins/en140/generative_rhetoric.htm); [periodic-vs-cumulative craft treatment](https://www.socratopia.app/library/ap-argument-toolkit-en/chapter-16) — a teaching site, not an authority; the doctrine traces to Christensen 1963 via the ERIC PDF)

## 2. Speech-to-prose: what the register gap is, and what transcript editors actually do

### The gap is measured, not folkloric [science]

- **Chafe (1982/1985):** spoken and written English differ on two dimensions — **fragmentation vs. integration** and **involvement vs. detachment**. Speech chains short clauses with little subordination (fragmented) and is saturated with audience-engagement markers (involved); writing packs more into each clause via nominalization, attributive adjectives, participles, subordination (integrated). ([Chafe & Danielewicz, Discourse Processes 1987](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01638538409544580); [feature tables](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Features-Defining-the-Involvement-Detachment-Fragmentation-and-Integration-Scales_tbl1_232859260))
- **Halliday:** written language has high **lexical density** (content words per clause: roughly 3–6 for writing vs. 1.5–2 for speech) while speech has high **grammatical intricacy** (more clauses, more elaborately chained, each carrying less). Converting speech to prose means *repacking* meaning: fewer clauses, denser clauses. ([Wikipedia, lexical density, with Halliday figures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexical_density); [Halliday explainer](https://reclaimingthelanguage.blog/2018/09/01/halliday-on-spoken-and-written-language/))
- **Biber (1988):** corpus factor analysis of 23 registers; Dimension 1, **Involved vs. Informational Production** — conversation loads on private verbs, contractions, present tense, second person; writing loads on nouns, longer words, prepositions, higher type-token ratio. Register is a continuum, and a transcript sits at the far involved/fragmented pole. ([Biber's cross-linguistic MDA papers](https://jan.ucc.nau.edu/biber/Biber/Biber%20(1992).pdf); [review of Dimensions of Register Variation](https://kilgarriff.co.uk/Publications/1995-K-BiberReview.asc))

**[analysis]** This gives the content machine a checkable target: a finished draft whose lexical density and subordination profile still look like speech hasn't been converted, only punctuated. It also warns against overshooting — the dive-bar register *wants* some involved features (contractions, second person); the fix is repacking clauses, not de-voicing them. It also explains the measured MATTR gap (dictated speech 0.733 vs written prose 0.843, per the run-#3 continuation prompt): speech is lexically narrower than prose *by register*, so a words-locked draft of a transcript inherits speech's lexical profile no matter how well it is shaped.

### What transcript-to-prose editors report doing [craft]

- **Staged passes (oral-history/profile editing, Cornell FIT117 guide):** (1) format — punctuate, paragraph, **delete the interviewer's questions** (the questions' work must be absorbed into the prose); (2) basic — remove repetition, complete or cut fragments, eliminate **verbal tics**; (3) complex — **reorganize for narrative coherence** and resolve what's grammatical in speech but not in writing; (4) approval — the speaker reviews. Save each stage separately. ([Cornell guide](https://courses2.cit.cornell.edu/fit117/CP_E.htm))
- **Radical distillation with fidelity to experience, not to wording (Columbia oral history / Voice of Witness):** ~200,000 words of transcript become 6,000–7,000 of finished text. Editors "delete words and move phrases around," select the **imagery** that "gives the reader a portrait," and defend the position that skillful editing yields text "more closely aligned to a narrator's lived experience" than the raw transcript — *accessibility over verbatim authenticity* is a stated professional value, not a guilty secret. ([Columbia Oral History, "The Power of Editing"](https://oralhistory.columbia.edu/blog-posts/People/the-power-of-editing))
- **Ghostwriters build a voice template before drafting [craft/lore]:** record long interviews, then mine the recordings for **signature diction, recurring metaphors, narrative habits, sentence-length preferences, emotional tone per story type**, and draft while "hearing" the client speak the sentences; the transcript's job is to supply the *voice fingerprint* and the *material*, never the sentence sequence. Also reported: clients sound most like themselves when relaxed and story-telling, not when trying to be impressive — early-interview material outranks polished-answer material. ([The Writers For Hire on capturing personality](https://www.thewritersforhire.com/the-art-of-ghostwriting-how-to-someone-elses-personality-in-your-words/); [River on first-interview voice capture](https://rivereditor.com/blogs/how-ghostwriters-capture-client-voice-first-interview); [Jane Friedman on the ghostwriting process](https://janefriedman.com/hiring-ghostwriter/)). Specific numbers (interview lengths, gear) are [lore]; the pattern-mining practice is consistent across sources.
- **Not found:** a rigorous craft text specifically on the as-told-to *magazine essay* form. The oral-history and ghostwriting literatures above are the documented adjacent practice; treat any more specific as-told-to "rules" as unverified.

**[analysis] The composite professional workflow, assembled from these sources:** absorb the questions → strip tics/repetition/false starts → *select* (the 30:1 cut) → reorganize around the story, not the interview order → repack clauses to written density → reinstate the speaker's fingerprint diction and metaphors on top of the rewritten frame. Note the order: voice is re-applied *after* restructuring, not preserved *instead of* restructuring. The content machine's runs #2–3 failure is plausibly that it preserved sentence-order and clause-grain fidelity (where speech lives) and lost fingerprint diction (where voice lives) — the exact inverse of professional practice.

## 3. Which operations ADD material vs. pure rearrangement/compression

This matters for the pipeline's honesty budget — what a transcript-faithful system may synthesize vs. merely reshape.

**Pure rearrangement/compression (no new content):**

- Surface changes and meaning-preserving paraphrase (Faigley & Witte)
- Clutter-pruning, greening, omission (Zinsser, McPhee)
- Verbal-tic/repetition removal, question deletion, selection (oral-history staging)
- Clause repacking to written density (Halliday-driven — same propositions, fewer clauses)
- Cohesion reordering: old-to-new, characters-as-subjects (Williams — reorders within existing propositions)
- Rhythm/sentence-length variation (Le Guin, Christensen re-shaping of existing content)

**Requires ADDING material not in the transcript:**

- **Reader-based restructuring** (Flower): shared-language expansion of private shorthand, plus the issue-centered frame itself — the "why this matters to you" scaffolding a speaker never says aloud
- **Knowledge-transforming** (Bereiter & Scardamalia): content generated under rhetorical pressure — new examples, sharpened claims the speaker didn't reach
- **Bridging context** the interviewer's questions used to supply (Cornell stage-1 deletion creates a hole the prose must fill)
- **Image work**: speech gestures at images ("it was crazy"); prose must land a concrete one. Selection sometimes suffices (Voice of Witness "imagery that gives a portrait" is *selected* from the transcript), but a transcript with no realized image forces a choice: add one or go without
- **Periodic/suspense syntax** (Christensen): near-absent in natural speech; imposing it is authorial addition at the syntax level even when propositions are unchanged
- **Transitions are the trap [analysis]:** the amateur addition is connective filler; Klinkenborg's position is that a well-ordered piece needs almost none. Adding transitions is the *cheapest and worst* form of adding; adding frame, context, and image is the expensive and correct form.

**[analysis] Pipeline mapping:** the interview engine already produces Elbow's freewrite / Lamott's down draft. The missing middle passes, in professional order: **grow/find-the-center (Elbow) → reader-based restructure (Flower) → meaning-level revision at beat grain (Sommers — already owned by storytelling-architecture) → register repack (Chafe/Halliday) → cohesion pass (Williams) → compression/greening (McPhee/Zinsser) → rhythm/syntax pass (Le Guin/Christensen/Klinkenborg — partly owned by writing-voice-modes)**. The two passes no existing skill in the chain owns are the Flower restructure and the register repack — and those are the two the "organized brain dump" symptom points at.
