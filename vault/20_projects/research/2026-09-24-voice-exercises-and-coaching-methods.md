# Voice exercises and coaching methods for an AI writing partner

**Question.** Which exercises, questioning methods and practice evidence should shape an AI coach whose job is to get a lot of authentic writing out of a writer, as voice samples, instead of polishing drafts?

**Source tiers:** A academic · B primary (the author's own book or site) · C trade press or institutional · D forum, blog or personal post.

## Takeaway

1. **Volume first, questions second, feedback rarely.** In one large college dataset (Astin, cited by Kellogg), the number of chances to write predicted gains more strongly than the amount of feedback (partial β .31 vs .12). Doing the reps without trying to improve is not enough ([Kellogg & Whiteford 2009, A](https://hillkm.com/EDUC_715/Unit_6/kellogg_whiteford_2009.pdf)).
2. **The obvious thought comes first. That's how it works, not a personal failing.** Goldberg, Elbow and Altucher each build the exercise to *use up* the first thoughts quickly so the later ones show up: keep writing without stopping, loop back, push past idea six. Treat the stock take as the warm-up.
3. **Ask neutral questions to get raw material, then opinionated ones to provoke.** Journalism's best-documented method is open, neutral questions asked one at a time ([Sawatsky via Poynter, C](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2008/learning-from-john-sawatsky-interviewing-methods/)). The user's "push me with options and opinions" should come *after* the neutral pass, so the AI's framing doesn't leak into the samples.
4. **Stories beat stances as raw voice.** Ghostwriters and StoryCorps both work from "walk me through the day…" rather than "what did you learn" (tier D and B sources below).
5. **Talking is a good way in, but it isn't the sample.** Dictation matches writing on quality after a few hours of practice ([Gould 1978, A](https://research.ibm.com/publications/how-experts-dictate)). Transcribed speech is structurally different from prose, though ([Chafe 1982, A](https://www.skase.sk/Volumes/JTL05/4.pdf)). Keep the transcript and his own edit of it; the difference is voice data.
6. **Keep imitation out of the sample set.** Imitating models trains sentences (Franklin; Graham & Perin effect size .25), but it pulls toward the model's voice.

## 1. Exercises that surface voice and generate volume

**Peter Elbow: freewriting.** Write without stopping for a fixed stretch (classically ten minutes). Don't edit, don't reread, and if nothing comes, write that nothing comes. *Good for:* fluency and getting past the internal editor. Source: *Writing Without Teachers* (1973), ch. 1 (B, from the book; not re-checked online for this note). A 15-week study of 70 ten-minute freewrites found fluency rose from 19.5 to 30.1 words per minute and carried over to structured tasks. The participants were second-language university students ([Yasuda, *Language Teaching Research*, A](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13621688221084899)).

**Elbow: loop writing.** A set of short, focused freewrites, each approaching the topic from a different side. *First thoughts* (at least 15 minutes nonstop). *Prejudices*: your biases before you research. *Instant version*: a fake finished draft written as if you already knew everything. *Dialogues* between opposing voices. Then narrative thinking, stories, scenes, portraits and others ([*Writing with Power* ch. 8 excerpt, B](https://nowcomment.com/documents/loops/combined?print=yes)). *Good for:* many angles on one topic in one sitting, each in a different mode. The closest existing match for "push me to an original take."

**Elbow: the believing and doubting game.** Doubting means testing an idea by hunting its flaws. Believing means deliberately inhabiting an idea that seems wrong until you find what's true in it ([Elbow, "The Believing Game or Methodological Believing," A/B](https://scholarworks.umass.edu/eng_faculty_pubs/13/)). *Good for:* breaking out of the stance you'd take by default.

**Natalie Goldberg: the rules of writing practice.** Keep your hand moving. Don't cross out. Don't worry about spelling or grammar. Lose control. Don't think, don't get logical. Go for the jugular: if something scary or exposed comes up, go straight into it. The stated aim is to burn through first thoughts to where the internal censor isn't working ([*Writing Down the Bones*, "First Thoughts," excerpt, B](https://hcommons.org/app/uploads/sites/1004024/2024/05/Goldberg-First-Thoughts-on-Writing.pdf)). *Good for:* timed sprints. "Jugular" licenses the coach to steer toward what the writer flinched from.

**Lynda Barry: image-first, hand-drawn, timed.** Two methods. (a) *List and locate:* list about ten examples of a category ("cars you remember"), pick one, answer concrete sensory questions about it, then write by hand for a short timed run, keeping the pen moving and doodling when stuck ([*What It Is*, 2008, via EBSCO summary, C](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/literature-and-writing/what-it); [reader walkthrough using a 7-minute timer, D](https://twowritingteachers.org/2024/10/07/book-review-lynda-barrys-what-it-is/)). (b) *Daily diary,* from *Syllabus* (2014): quadrants for things you did, things you saw, something you heard, and a quick drawing ([Barry's course Tumblr, B](https://www.tumblr.com/thenearsightedmonkey/111125141634/above-variations-on-our-daily-diary-practice); [NLM class handout, C](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/graphicmedicine/materials/UM-Class-1-Keeping-a-Daily-Diary.pdf)). The point is to surface images you noticed without knowing it. *Good for:* visual thinkers; it starts from a picture, not an argument.

**Julia Cameron: morning pages.** Three longhand pages of stream of consciousness first thing each morning, private, not meant to be art ([juliacameronlive.com, B](https://juliacameronlive.com/basic-tools/morning-pages/); *The Artist's Way*, 1992). *Good for:* a daily volume habit. Private by design, so the writer chooses what, if anything, becomes a sample.

**Brenda Ueland: *If You Want to Write* (1938).** The chapter titles are the method: "Be Careless, Reckless! Be a Lion! Be a Pirate! When You Write," "Microscopic Truthfulness," and "Keep a Slovenly, Headlong, Impulsive, Honest Diary" ([table of contents via archive.org record, B](https://archive.org/details/ifyouwanttowrite0000uela_z8y3)). *Good for:* permission. Truthfulness over impressiveness.

**Anne Lamott: short assignments and shitty first drafts.** *Short Assignments:* write only what you can see through a one-inch picture frame, one small scene at a time. *Shitty First Drafts:* the first draft is meant to be bad, because that's how the good second draft gets found ([*Bird by Bird*, 1994, chapter excerpt, B](https://wrd.as.uky.edu/sites/default/files/1-Shitty%20First%20Drafts.pdf)). *Good for:* shrinking a big topic down to one concrete moment.

**Roy Peter Clark: *Writing Tools* (2006).** The generative tools include #22 "Climb up and down the ladder of abstraction," #14 "Get the name of the dog," #31 "Build your work around a key question," #34 "Write from different cinematic angles," #44 "Save string," and #48 "Limit self-criticism in early drafts" ([Poynter quick list, B](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2006/fifty-writing-tools-quick-list/)). *Good for:* the ladder gives the coach a two-way move: ask for the concrete detail when the writer is abstract, and for the meaning when he's only concrete.

**William Zinsser.** *Writing to Learn* (1988) holds that writing is thinking on paper. In *On Writing Well*, the chapter "The Audience" tells writers to write for themselves, not an imagined mass audience ([summary with quotes, D](https://leonyeh.com/blog/writing-to-learn-william-zinsser-summary); books are B). *Good for:* dropping the imagined reader while generating.

**James Altucher: ten ideas a day.** Write ten ideas daily on a waiter's pad, about anything. If you can't get to ten, write twenty, because forcing bad ideas gets past the perfectionist. Throw most of them away. Combine lists ("idea sex") ([Altucher, B](https://archive.jamesaltucher.com/blog/the-ultimate-guide-for-becoming-an-idea-machine/)). *Good for:* pushing past the first few obvious ideas. He puts the hard part at around idea six.

**The list of 100.** Put a question at the top and write 100 answers in one sitting. Repeats and odd entries show up in the later stretch. Attributed to Kathleen Adams, *Journal to the Self* (1990), in its own chapter (B, book not checked directly; [practitioner summary, D](https://www.jennibick.com/blogs/news/tackle-any-issue-with-a-list-of-100)). *Good for:* making a topic give up non-obvious angles. It's a solo exercise, though, not a live one.

**Constraint exercises (Oulipo).** Raymond Queneau's *Exercises in Style* (1947) tells one trivial bus anecdote 99 ways: sonnet, telegram, lipogram and so on ([overview, C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exercises_in_Style)). *Good for:* separating content from form. When the form is fixed by a rule, word choice is where the voice shows.

**Same idea at three lengths.** I found **no single primary source** that names this exercise. It's a composite of Queneau's one-content-many-forms idea and the screenwriter's logline-to-synopsis-to-treatment ladder. It's listed here as a synthesis, not as a sourced method.

## 2. Questioning methods that turn a topic into a take

**Journalist: open, neutral, lean (John Sawatsky).** Ask what, how and why rather than yes/no. Keep your own values out of the question ("What's it like being X?", not "Is it hard being X?"). Ask one question at a time. Follow up with "What was that like?" and "Tell me more about that." ([Poynter 2008, C](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2008/learning-from-john-sawatsky-interviewing-methods/); [Signal v. Noise summary, D](https://signalvnoise.com/archives2/john_sawatsky_and_the_power_of_simple_questions)). **Live fit: excellent.** It's the default mode for the coach's first pass.

**Memoir and oral history: "tell me about a time."** StoryCorps publishes a bank of story-first questions ("Can you tell me about a moment when…") and a question generator ([StoryCorps Great Questions, B](https://storycorps.org/participate/great-questions/)). Ghostwriters report the same approach: ask about a specific day ("walk me through the day you decided…"), record everything for rhythm, and avoid questions about meaning or lessons, which push people into analytical, guarded answers ([River, D](https://rivereditor.com/blogs/how-ghostwriters-capture-client-voice-first-interview); [Erick Mertz, D](https://erickmertzwriting.com/ghostwriting-interview-tips/)). **Live fit: excellent.**

**Five whys.** Taiichi Ohno's root-cause method: ask why about five times until the underlying cause shows ([Lean Enterprise Institute, C](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/5-whys/)). Applied to an opinion, it finds the belief underneath it. **Live fit: good but risky.** Repeated whys climb the ladder of abstraction and can end in platitudes, so alternate with "tell me about a time" to pull back down.

**Steel-manning (Rapoport's rules).** Before criticizing a view, restate it so well its holder would wish they'd put it that way. Then list the points of agreement and what you learned. Only then rebut ([Dennett, *Intuition Pumps*, 2013, via The Marginalian, C](https://www.themarginalian.org/2014/03/28/daniel-dennett-rapoport-rules-criticism/)). **Live fit: excellent.** The AI argues the other side and the writer has to steel-man it before answering.

**Socratic questioning.** Paul and Elder's taxonomy covers questions that probe clarification, assumptions, evidence, implications and viewpoints ([*Thinker's Guide to Socratic Questioning*, B](https://www.criticalthinking.org/files/SocraticQuestioning2006.pdf)). **Live fit: good** as a follow-up checklist; asked in a row they feel like cross-examination.

**The contrarian-truth family.** Thiel's interview question is "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" A good answer takes the form "most people believe X, but the truth is the opposite." Familiar debates don't count ([*Zero to One*, 2014, ch. 1, B; summary, D](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/zero-to-one-peter-thiel)). **Live fit: poor as an opener, strong after a warm-up.**

**Pre-mortem.** Imagine the project has already failed and explain why. Klein cites 1989 research finding that this "prospective hindsight" improved people's ability to identify reasons for future outcomes by 30% ([Klein, HBR 2007, C](http://homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Performing-a-Project-Premortem.pdf)). For a writer it becomes: "It's 2028 and your take on X aged badly. Why?" **Live fit: excellent.**

**Surprise as the target.** Paul Graham argues an essay should start from a question, not a thesis, and aim for what surprises the writer. He says most of his essay ideas come to him while writing ([The Age of the Essay, 2004, B](https://paulgraham.com/essay.html); [Putting Ideas into Words, 2022, B](https://paulgraham.com/words.html)). A useful closing question for any session: "What did you say today that surprised you?"

**Best in live back-and-forth:** Sawatsky neutral probes, "tell me about a time," steel-man duels, the pre-mortem, and the Thiel question after a warm-up. **Better solo:** the list of 100, morning pages, and the Barry diary.

## 3. What the evidence says about developing writing skill

- **Stages.** Kellogg describes a path from *knowledge-telling* (writing down what you know) to *knowledge-transforming* (writing changes what you think) to, for professionals, *knowledge-crafting* (juggling content, text and the reader's likely reading at once). The whole path takes more than two decades ([Kellogg 2008, *J. Writing Research*, A](https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/view/759)).
- **What deliberate practice needs.** Kellogg & Whiteford list motivation to improve, interest, tailored tasks, feedback on results, and heavy repetition over years. Going through the motions doesn't count. Biographical data put first publication 10–20 years after starting ([Kellogg & Whiteford 2009, A](https://hillkm.com/EDUC_715/Unit_6/kellogg_whiteford_2009.pdf)).
- **Volume vs. feedback.** In Astin's college data, the number of writing-course opportunities outweighed the amount of instructor feedback. The authors argue that intermittent feedback may help long-term retention, but say there's no direct writing evidence yet (same source).
- **Imitation.** In the Graham & Perin meta-analysis, sentence combining (effect size .50) and studying model texts (.25) both had positive effects on writing quality ([Writing Next, A](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED499256.pdf)). Franklin's method: make hints from *Spectator* essays, set them aside, rewrite from the hints, compare with the original and correct. He also turned prose into verse and back, and shuffled his hints to practice arranging ideas ([*Autobiography*, B](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20203/20203-h/20203-h.htm)). Ericsson and Pool present this as deliberate practice without a teacher, where the original essay does the job of feedback (*Peak*, 2016, B; [summary, D](https://commoncog.com/peak-book-summary/)).
- **Caveat on deliberate practice.** A meta-analysis found deliberate practice explained 26% of performance variance in games and 21% in music, but only 4% in education and under 1% in professions ([Macnamara et al. 2014, *Psych. Science*, A](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614535810)). Critics dispute its broad definition.
- **Gap:** I found no controlled study of how *voice* specifically develops. The claims about voice here come from practitioners (Elbow, Goldberg, Barry), not experiments.

**Implication.** Aim for many short, varied, effortful reps with the goal of pushing thinking, and give feedback sparingly. Imitation builds sentence skill, but for this purpose it should stay out of the sample set.

## 4. Speaking vs. writing

- **Dictation works about as well.** Gould found that after a few hours of practice, adults dictated one-page letters as well as they wrote them, in both time and rated quality. Executives experienced at dictation were 20–65% faster dictating than writing. Planning took about two-thirds of composing time whatever the method, so composing is the bottleneck, not the medium ([Gould 1978, IBM Research summary, A](https://research.ibm.com/publications/how-experts-dictate)).
- **Transcribed speech is not prose.** Chafe describes speech as *fragmented* (idea units strung together with "and") and *involved* (lots of first person). Writing is *integrated* (more packed into each unit) and *detached* (passives, nominalizations). The cause is how much time each mode allows ([Chafe 1982 as summarized in SKASE, A](https://www.skase.sk/Volumes/JTL05/4.pdf)).
- **Field report.** Fourteen junior scientists who dictated whole article drafts reported flow and speed. Their drafts had poor coherence, long sentences and redundancy, and needed several rounds of feedback. Those who revised right away kept their momentum better ([BMC Med Educ 2013, A](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3751781/)).
- **Practitioners.** Elbow's *Vernacular Eloquence* (2012) argues writers can borrow speech's naturalness and fluency ([OUP, B](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/vernacular-eloquence-9780199782512)). Graham's test is to keep a sentence only if it's how you'd say it to a friend ([Write Like You Talk, 2015, B](https://paulgraham.com/talk.html)), but he also says writing is a stricter test of an idea than talking ([Putting Ideas into Words, B](https://paulgraham.com/words.html)).

**Implication.** Use voice memos to catch unguarded phrasing and first-person involvement. Then have the writer turn the transcript into prose himself. Store both versions.

## 5. Session menu for a live AI coach

Rules that apply to every format: the AI asks neutral questions before giving opinions, and any options the AI offers are tagged and never counted as samples. Only the writer's own words go into the corpus.

| # | Format | Drawn from | Time | Sample produced | Why it breaks the obvious thought |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Timed freewrite, then interrogation** | Elbow freewriting; Goldberg rules; Sawatsky probes | 10 + 10 min | Raw stance, fragments | The stock take gets written first and used up; neutral follow-ups work on what's left |
| 2 | **Loop sprint** | Elbow loop writing | 30–40 min | 4–5 short pieces in different modes | Prejudices, an instant draft and a dialogue each come at the topic from a different side |
| 3 | **Stance duel** | Believing/doubting game; Rapoport's rules | 15–20 min | Stance + counterargument | He has to steel-man the other side before rebutting, which usually shifts his position |
| 4 | **Three positions: pick, reject, or write the fourth** | Thiel's "most believe X" form; Socratic viewpoints | 10–15 min | Stance, signature line | Rejecting is easier than generating; the fourth position is often the original one |
| 5 | **Story behind the opinion** | StoryCorps; ghostwriter "walk me through the day"; Sawatsky | 15–25 min | Story | The opinion is shared; the scene is only his |
| 6 | **Why-ladder** | Five whys + Clark's ladder of abstraction | 10 min | Stance with its root belief | Finds the belief underneath, then returns to a concrete example |
| 7 | **List and locate** | Barry, *What It Is* | 15 min, handwritten | Memory scene | Starts from an image, not an argument, which suits a visual artist |
| 8 | **Daily diary check-in** | Barry, *Syllabus* | 5–8 min daily | Observation bank | Unnoticed observations; feeds formats 5 and 7 |
| 9 | **Ten ideas, keep the weird ones** | Altucher; list of 100 (Adams) | 15–30 min | Idea list, then one short post | The first few items are the obvious ones; the later items are forced past them |
| 10 | **Same idea at three lengths** | Queneau (composite; see §1) | ~20 min | Signature line (≤15 words), short post (~150), long-form (~600) | Compressing to one line forces him to name the claim; expanding forces evidence |
| 11 | **Pre-mortem of a take** | Klein | 15 min | Stance with stakes, often a story | Hindsight framing surfaces withheld doubts |
| 12 | **Contrarian truth** | Thiel, ch. 1 | 10 min, after a warm-up | Stance, signature line | Familiar debate positions don't count |
| 13 | **Talk it, then write it** | Gould; Elbow, *Vernacular Eloquence*; Graham | 5-min memo + 15-min rewrite | Spoken sample + written sample | Speech gets past the internal editor; his rewrite shows his written voice |
| 14 | **One-inch frame** | Lamott; Clark's "get the name of the dog" | 10 min | Micro-scene, short post | Narrowing to one concrete moment rules out the generic |
| 15 | **Self-Franklin** | Franklin's *Spectator* method, applied to his own best piece | 20 min | Rewrite of his own piece + comparison | Builds skill from his own voice instead of someone else's |

**Unverified or weak points.** The three-lengths exercise has no single primary source. The list of 100 is attributed to Adams but the book wasn't checked directly. The freewriting fluency evidence comes from second-language students. The ghostwriting method comes from practitioner blogs (D). There's no experimental research on voice development itself.
