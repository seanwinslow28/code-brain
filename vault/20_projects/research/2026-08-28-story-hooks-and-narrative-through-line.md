# Openings, in medias res, and the narrative through-line

Research brief for hardening [`.claude/skills/storytelling-architecture`](../../../.claude/skills/storytelling-architecture/SKILL.md). Extends, does not duplicate, [`references/story-mechanics.md`](../../../.claude/skills/storytelling-architecture/references/story-mechanics.md) — the six mechanics, the four scaffolds, the anti-formula guard and the Zeigarnik honesty note are already sourced there and are not re-argued here.

## Evidence discipline

Same tags as the reference file, plus one new one the reference will need:

- **[science]** — replicated empirical work, peer-reviewed.
- **[craft]** — working writers and editors stating technique. Doctrine, not data.
- **[lore]** — widely repeated, thinly evidenced.
- **[industry data]** — proprietary telemetry from a vendor with a commercial interest. Real numbers, unaudited methods, never peer-reviewed. This tag is new; §5 is where it becomes necessary.
- **[analysis]** — my own construction from the sources, flagged so it never gets cited as if a source said it.

**Source-tier caveat.** Several craft claims below are anchored to secondary write-ups (blogs, workshop pages) because the primary is a printed book with no legal full text online. Where that happens I name the book and page-level concept so the claim is checkable against the physical source, and the link is the best available public confirmation, not the authority itself.

## 1. What an opening actually does, mechanically

### F1.1 The craft literature's name for it is the *contract* or *promise* [craft]

The mechanism Sean is describing — one sentence telling you what the whole ride will feel like — is not called a hook in the craft literature. It has two names, and they cover different halves of the job.

Jacqui Banaszynski, writing at Nieman Storyboard, states the promise as a piece of direct address from writer to reader: *"You are in good hands. I know where I'm going, so relax and follow me. I hope to inform, engage and even surprise you along the way. But I won't mislead or jerk you around for reasons of my own indulgence or incompetence."* ([Nieman Storyboard, 2018](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2018/11/21/readers-want-to-feel-secure-in-the-hands-of-the-author/)). Note what the promise is *about*: competence and good faith, not subject matter.

Camilla Nelson (Univ. of Notre Dame Australia) uses the harder word: *"First sentences establish a contract with the reader about what is to come."* She adds a useful physical metaphor — a first sentence has an **"angle of lean"** that appears to direct every sentence after it ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/first-sentences-establish-a-contract-with-the-reader-about-what-is-to-come-57984)).

Albert Liau, in CRAFT Literary's taxonomy of openings, calls the opening both an "invitation" and a "contract with — the reader," and splits all openings into exactly two families: **in medias res** and **setup** ([CRAFT Literary, 2020](https://www.craftliterary.com/2020/10/06/openings-taxonomy/)).

**So Thompson's stated reason generalizes, and the term of art is "contract" or "promise."** But the sources are also clear that the contract is a promise about *tone, competence and kind* — not about content. That distinction matters for the skill, which currently treats the hook mostly as a question-raiser.

### F1.2 Poe got there first, and framed it as *effect* rather than information [craft]

Poe's "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846) is the earliest hard statement of the tonal-signature idea, and it is more absolute than any modern version: the writer decides the single intended effect *before writing begins*, then subordinates tone, theme, setting, character, conflict and plot to it — and *"If his very initial sentence tend not to the outbringing of this effect, then he has failed in his first step"* ([Poetry Foundation full text](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/69390/the-philosophy-of-composition)). Poe also ties the doctrine to length: unity of effect is achievable in short forms because they are read at one sitting.

For a Substack-length piece, Poe is the more useful frame than the modern hook literature, because he makes the opening accountable to the *whole piece's* felt effect — which is precisely Thompson's stated reason, 125 years earlier.

### F1.3 The second, co-equal job of an opening is orientation [craft]

Liau quotes Benjamin Percy's three questions the opening must answer for a reader: **"where am I, when am I, who am I?"**, and Alexander Chee on giving "that sense of the world, the character in it and the relationship between the character and the world" ([CRAFT Literary](https://www.craftliterary.com/2020/10/06/openings-taxonomy/)).

That triad is not a craft coinage. It is the name of a clause type in Labov and Waletzky's structural model of oral narrative, built from hundreds of recorded personal narratives: **abstract / orientation / complicating action / evaluation / result / coda**, where *orientation* supplies "the time, the place, the persons and the behavior involved" ([Labov lecture PDF, ENS Lyon](https://cle.ens-lyon.fr/anglais/fichiers/william-labov-a-structural-analysis-of-narrative-mode-de-compatibilite-769-_1366115192183-pdf); [Cambridge summary chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/learner-language-discourse-and-interaction/beginning-the-study-of-narrative/35686B8009B7512473B65A97F73327D7)).

**An opening therefore has two jobs running simultaneously: promise (tone/effect) and orientation (who/when/where).** The skill's mechanic 2 currently gates only for concreteness and a raised question. It has no orientation gate at all. That is the gap §2 turns out to sit in.

### F1.4 The mechanism: the first thing named becomes the foundation [science]

Gernsbacher's Structure Building Framework holds that comprehension is the building of a mental structure through three subprocesses — **laying a foundation**, mapping incoming coherent information onto it, and **shifting** to a new substructure when incoming information does not map ([Gernsbacher, *Language Comprehension as Structure Building*, framework paper PDF](https://gernsbacherlab.org/wp-content/uploads/papers/StrucBding_Frwk_Bt95.pdf)).

The empirical payoff is the **Advantage of First Mention**: participants mentioned first in a sentence are more accessible in memory than those mentioned later, *because* they form the foundation onto which everything else is mapped ([Gernsbacher & Hargreaves, via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4266409/)).

This is the closest thing to a hard scientific account of why openings are load-bearing: whatever the first sentence names is not merely first, it is *structural*. Everything after it is mapped onto it or forces a costly shift.

### F1.5 How much a single sentence can install: the event-indexing model [science]

Zwaan, Langston & Graesser's event-indexing model says readers track narrative events along five dimensions — **time, space, protagonist, causality, intentionality** ([*Psychological Science* 6:292–297, 1995](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1995.tb00513.x)).

The reading-time evidence has an asymmetry the skill can actually use: readers reliably slow at **temporal, causal, goal-related and protagonist** discontinuities, **but not at spatial ones** ([Zwaan et al.; and the direct test in Rinck & Weber, "Who when where: An experimental test of the event-indexing model," *Memory & Cognition*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03195811)).

### F1.6 [analysis] Reading the Thompson line against F1.4/F1.5

*"We were somewhere around Barstow on the edge of the desert when the drugs began to take hold."*

- *We* — first mention, protagonist, becomes the foundation (F1.4).
- *somewhere around Barstow / edge of the desert* — space, and deliberately vague; per F1.5 that vagueness is the cheapest thing in the sentence to leave imprecise.
- *when* — temporal index.
- *the drugs began to take hold* — an in-progress causal and intentional event, unresolved.

Four of the five event-indexing dimensions are installed in one clause chain, the fifth (space) is the one the research says can be left loose, and the unresolved verb keeps the situation open. It also discharges Poe's unity-of-effect obligation in the same breath. **This is my construction, not a claim any source makes** — no one has run the experiment. But it explains why the line generalizes into a rule rather than staying a Thompson idiosyncrasy: *maximum situation-model installation per word, with the one skippable dimension skipped.*

Note also that the one serious craft reading of this specific line does *not* go where Sean goes. David L. Ulin's Nieman "Why's This So Good?" essay on *Fear and Loathing* is about hyperbole as a reporting method — "How do we evoke the feeling of a situation?" — not about the opening's mechanics ([Nieman Storyboard, 2017](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2017/04/13/whys-this-so-good-hunter-s-thompson-and-fear-and-loathing-in-las-vegas/)). Sean's reading is his own, and it is better supported by F1.4/F1.5 than by the Thompson criticism.

### F1.7 The limits, stated plainly

- Nelson, who wrote the contract sentence, also concludes that opening lines "only get you so far" in determining a book's quality ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/first-sentences-establish-a-contract-with-the-reader-about-what-is-to-come-57984)). A great first line is not evidence of a working piece.
- **Sources disagree on how much to withhold.** Vonnegut's rule 8 is *"Give your readers as much information as possible as soon as possible. To heck with suspense. Readers should have such complete understanding of what is going on, where and why, that they could finish the story themselves, should cockroaches eat the last few pages"* ([Gotham Writers, from the *Bagombo Snuff Box* introduction](https://www.writingclasses.com/toolbox/tips-masters/kurt-vonnegut-8-basics-of-creative-writing)). That is in direct tension with the skill's mechanic 5 (withhold the rescue) and mechanic 3 (open loops). **[analysis]** The reconciliation the sources support: Vonnegut is talking about *orientation* information (who/where/why-this-matters), the skill is talking about *outcome* information. Withhold outcomes; never withhold orientation. Vonnegut himself immediately says the rules can be broken and exempts Agatha Christie, so this is doctrine arguing with doctrine, not evidence arguing with evidence.

## 2. In medias res: when it works, when it fails

**Sean's hypothesis — "opening on a scene works, opening on a result fails because the punchline has no runway" — is confirmed in substance and mislabelled in its terms.** The failing variable is not scene-vs-result. It is two separate variables that a "result" opening happens to fail at once: *orientation* and *scene-vs-summary*. Separating them makes the rule enforceable.

### F2.1 The disorientation is a known, named failure [craft]

Every craft treatment of in medias res names the same risk: dropping a reader into chaos with no explanation reads as confusing, and confused readers put the piece down. The standard mitigations are consistent across sources — supply **one named character or clear role, sensory grounding, and a visible action or decision happening now**, then drip-feed backstory rather than dumping it in an apologetic block right after the opening ([Jericho Writers](https://jerichowriters.com/in-medias-res/); [Dabble](https://www.dabblewriter.com/articles/in-medias-res); [The Write Practice](https://thewritepractice.com/in-medias-res/)). This tier is craft-blog consensus, not authority, but it is unanimous and it matches F1.3 exactly.

### F2.2 The precise mechanism of "rambling person in the subway car": the given-new contract [science]

Clark & Haviland's **given–new contract**: a speaker is obliged to package information the listener already has as *given*, and information they do not have as *new*. When a listener cannot locate the antecedent for something presented as given, they must construct a **bridging inference** — an added proposition — which costs measurable processing time and can fail outright ([Clark & Haviland, "Comprehension and the given-new contract," in Freedle (ed.), *Discourse Production and Comprehension*, 1977 — author's PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/Clark,%20H.H.%20_%20Haviland,%20S.E.%20_Comprehension%20and%20the%20given-new%20contract_%201977.pdf); [Clark's publication list](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/pubs.html)).

This is the exact description of the subway-monologue failure. The speaker holds a complete situation model and treats all of it as given; the listener holds none and is forced to bridge on every clause until they stop trying. **The bad in-medias-res draft is a given-new contract violation, not a structure problem.** That is a diagnosis the skill can gate on, because it is checkable: for each referent in the first beat, is it actually given to a cold reader?

### F2.3 Which dimensions you may withhold, and which you may not [science]

From F1.5: temporal, causal, protagonist and goal discontinuities cost reading time; spatial ones do not ([Rinck & Weber](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03195811)). This yields a non-obvious, actionable asymmetry:

> You can drop a reader into an unnamed *place* cheaply. You cannot drop them into an unnamed *protagonist*, an unexplained *causal chain*, or an unstated *goal* cheaply.

This is a sharper rule than "give some context," and it is the one the failing draft most likely broke.

### F2.4 The scene / result hypothesis, sharpened

The craft distinction that does the work is **scene versus summary**, not scene versus result. Burroway: *summary* covers a long period in short compass, *scene* covers a short period at length; summary is "the mortar," scenes are "the bricks"; a confrontation, turning point or crisis "cannot be summarized"; and the canonical beginner error is *"to summarize events rather than to realize them as moments"* (Burroway, *Writing Fiction: A Guide to Narrative Craft* — see [Writers Write summary of her four aspects of narrative time](https://www.writerswrite.co.za/janet-burroways-4-aspects-of-narrative-time/) and [Anna Schell's craft note on heightening scene with summary](https://www.annaschellauthor.com/writing-craft-blog/from-janet-burroway-heighten-scene-with-summary)).

A "result" opening is summary. It compresses a resolved span into a statement, supplies no sensory particulars, and therefore gives the reader nothing to build a situation model from.

**But a result is not automatically disqualified**, and the sources make that explicit in the opposite direction:

- Vonnegut's rule 5: **"Start as close to the end as possible."** ([Gotham Writers](https://www.writingclasses.com/toolbox/tips-masters/kurt-vonnegut-8-basics-of-creative-writing))
- The screenwriting maxim **"enter late, leave early"** — attributed variously to Mamet, Goldman and Syd Field; Goldman's version is *"You always attack a movie scene as late as you possibly can"* ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/start-late-leave-early); [Scott Myers, Go Into The Story](https://gointothestory.blcklst.com/screenwriting-mantra-enter-late-exit-early-5b06e1e70bf3)). Attribution here is genuinely unsettled — treat as [craft] doctrine of unclear parentage, not a quotable rule with an owner.

**[analysis] The line that reconciles them:** "late" means late *inside a moment that is still unresolved*, not after the moment has finished. The test is not "is this a scene or a result." It is:

> **Is the reader inside something still happening, or being told about something that already finished?**

Thompson's line passes: the drugs *began* to take hold — present, unresolved, in progress. A result opening fails because it enters after the turn, so there is nothing left to turn.

### F2.5 Journalism engineered a repair device for exactly this, and then argued about it [craft — live disagreement]

**The pro-nut-graf position.** Chip Scanlan, Poynter: the nut graf *"tells the reader what the writer is up to; it delivers a promise of the story's content and message"*; it is *"a core statement that answers the basic question lurking in the mind of every reader: 'Why should I bother with this story?'"* It justifies the story, transitions from the lede, and explains the lede's connection to the rest ([Poynter, 2003](https://www.poynter.org/archive/2003/the-nut-graf-part-i/)). NPR teaches lede-and-nut-graf as a pair that orients the reader in three to five sentences ([NPR Training](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-64764/nut-graf-and-lead-duos-that-point-readers-in-the-right-direction)).

**The anti-nut-graf position — from the same author.** Scanlan later argues nut grafs are overused and, in narrative, destructive: a harsh nut graf *"would mimic the ear-shattering 'beep-beep-beep' of a truck in reverse. The dream would be broken."* His evidence is John Branch's "Snow Fall" — 17,000 words, no nut graf — with Branch saying *"A nut graf would have given a lot of that away."* Scanlan's alternative is *transitions*: "subtle, elegant turns" borrowed from fiction. He frames the whole fight as editors saying "the reader needs to know what the story is about up high" versus writers saying "let the reader find out by reading it" ([Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/stories/nut-grafs-overused-misused-or-merely-misunderstood/)).

The "dream" he is invoking is Gardner's (see F4.2). **Both camps agree orientation must land early. They disagree only on whether it gets its own dedicated unit or is dissolved into transitions.** The skill should record the disagreement and enforce the shared requirement, not pick a winner.

### F2.6 The bait-and-switch check [craft]

Poynter's hard failure mode for anecdotal/delayed openings: *"A common problem with the anecdotal lead, it must be noted, is the way the character or story that charmed us at the top never appears again — a kind of narrative bait and switch."* The stated fix: the opening anecdote works "if that character, action, or setting recurs" ([Poynter, 2024](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2024/the-best-pulitzer-leads-or-ledes-in-2024/)).

This is checkable at beat-map time and the skill currently cannot catch it: the open-loop ledger tracks *questions*, not *the opening's concrete material*.

## 3. Through-line / narrative spine

### F3.1 The named units, and why none of them is the beat map [craft]

- **McKee, spine (a.k.a. through-line, super-objective):** the protagonist's deep desire to restore the balance of life disrupted by the inciting incident; the unifying force to which every scene, image and word relates ([McKee, *Story*; principles digest PDF](https://desertscreenwritersgroup.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/mckee-story-principles.pdf)).
- **McKee, controlling idea:** a single governing value **proven through action, not stated**, expressible in one sentence, with two components — *value* (the charge at the climax) and *cause* (why it came out that way).
- **Truby, designing principle:** *"the internal logic of the story, what makes the parts hang together organically so that the story becomes greater than the sum of its parts"* — explicitly distinct from premise, because you must "distinguish what the story is, from how you intend to tell it. They are not the same things." Truby's blunt version: all stories have a premise; only good stories have a designing principle ([Teambooktu digest of *The Anatomy of Story*](https://teambooktu.com/john-trubys-designing-principle); [Stage 32 discussion thread](https://www.stage32.com/lounge/screenwriting/Please-help-me-to-understand-better-Trubys-important-concept-of-the-designing-principle)).

### F3.2 For nonfiction the sharpest formulation is Gornick's, and it is the direct diagnosis [craft]

Vivian Gornick, *The Situation and the Story: The Art of Personal Narrative*: **"the situation is the context or circumstance, sometimes the plot; the story is the emotional experience that preoccupies the writer; the insight, the wisdom, the thing one has come to say"** ([The Marginalian's treatment](https://www.themarginalian.org/2015/06/22/vivian-gornick-the-situation-and-the-story-personal-narrative/); [full-text course PDF, St. Lawrence University](https://library.stlawu.edu/system/files/2021-08/situation_and_the_story_gornick_0.pdf)).

A draft that reads *"I did this, then this, then this"* is **all situation and no story**. That is the whole finding. Causality between beats does not supply a story in Gornick's sense; it supplies a better-connected situation.

### F3.3 Forster explains why but/therefore is necessary and not sufficient [craft]

E. M. Forster, *Aspects of the Novel* (1927): *"The king died and then the queen died"* is a story; *"the king died and then the queen died of grief"* is a plot. **"If it is a story we say 'and then?' If it is in a plot we ask 'why?'"** ([full chapter text](https://hackneybooks.co.uk/books/41/1284/Plot.html)).

Read against the skill: the but/therefore test converts chronology into plot — it buys you *why*. It does not buy the reader's *and then?*, which Forster locates in curiosity about what is still coming. A piece can be fully causal and still have no pull, because pull is an unanswered forward question, not a satisfied backward one. **Both are needed; the skill currently gates only one.**

### F3.4 The granularity answer — and a terminology bug in the skill [craft]

McKee's hierarchy is **beat → scene → sequence → act → story**, where a **beat is "an exchange of behavior in action/reaction"** — a unit *inside* a scene ([Storm Writing School on McKee's beats](https://stormwritingschool.com/beats/); [McKee principles PDF](https://desertscreenwritersgroup.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/mckee-story-principles.pdf)).

**What `storytelling-architecture` calls a "beat" is McKee's *scene*, not McKee's *beat*.** The skill cites McKee for its step-outline, so this should be corrected in the reference file or the citation is wrong.

More importantly, McKee's gate at that level is not but/therefore. It is **the turn**, and he states it on his own site:

> *"A SCENE is an action through conflict in more or less continuous time and space that turns the value-charged condition of a character's life on at least one value with a degree of perceptible significance."*
> *"If the value-charged condition of the character's life stays unchanged from one end of a scene to the other, nothing meaningful happens"* — a **nonevent**.
> *"No scene that doesn't turn. This is our ideal."*
> And on exposition-only scenes: a disciplined writer trashes them and weaves the information in elsewhere.
> ([McKee Seminars, "Do Your Scenes Turn?"](https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/))

**This is the exact bug Sean observed.** But/therefore is a test of the *seam between* two beats. The turn is a test of what happens *inside* one. A beat map can pass every seam while every beat is internally a nonevent — a sequence of well-connected non-happenings. That reads as "I did this, then this, then this," because that is literally what it is.

### F3.5 The sentence-level analogue [craft]

Vonnegut's rule 4: **"Every sentence must do one of two things — reveal character or advance the action."** ([Gotham Writers](https://www.writingclasses.com/toolbox/tips-masters/kurt-vonnegut-8-basics-of-creative-writing)). This is the turn test at sentence granularity.

Honest caveat: this is a rule of thumb from the introduction to a story collection, not a studied finding, and Vonnegut appends that the rules can be broken. It is also **out of scope for this skill to enforce** — the chain contract puts sentences in `writing-voice-modes`. It belongs in the beat map only as a note passed downstream, never as a phrasing instruction.

### F3.6 Why the flat draft can be specific and still inert [craft]

Roy Peter Clark's **ladder of abstraction** (from S. I. Hayakawa's *Language in Action*, 1939): at the bottom are "bloody knives and rosary beads, wedding rings and baseball cards"; at the top are "freedom" and "literacy"; good writers move up and down — and **"beware of the middle rungs of the ladder where bureaucracy and public policy lurk"** ([Poynter Writing Tool #13](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2004/writing-tool-13-show-and-tell/); [Clark's Twenty Writing Tools](http://www.swopnet.com/misc/writing/writing_tools.html); [The Open Notebook's applied treatment](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2023/05/30/using-the-ladder-of-abstraction-to-elevate-science-stories/)).

"I did this, then this, then this" is mid-rung prose: too abstract to see, too low to mean anything. **The skill's specificity gate catches genericity but not mid-rung reporting** — a beat can be perfectly specific to this piece and still sit on the middle rung.

### F3.7 Forward pull is not only a section-end device [craft]

Clark's **gold coins**: reward the reader with anecdotes, examples, quotes, startling images or interesting facts — *especially in the middle of a story* — dropped "like gold coins along a forest path." Readers who find a few early begin to expect them, which is how they acquire confidence the piece is worth finishing. Clark's justification is Barney Kilgore's (Wall Street Journal) line: **"The easiest thing for the reader to do is to quit reading."** ([Nieman Storyboard on Clark's Writing Tools](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2023/02/07/roy-peter-clark-writing-tools-narrative-craft/); [Twenty Writing Tools](http://www.swopnet.com/misc/writing/writing_tools.html))

The skill's mechanic 6 places forward pull only at section ends. Gold coins are a separately named, separately checkable mid-section device.

## 4. Scene vs summary

### F4.1 The distinction and the standard error [craft]

Burroway, as in F2.4: summary = long time, short compass; scene = short time, at length; summary is the mortar, scene the bricks. **Summary's legitimate jobs** are enumerated and worth keeping: give information, fill in background, let us understand a motive, alter pace, create a transition, leap moments or years. **Scene is mandatory** at confrontations, turning points, and crises, which "cannot be summarized." The named beginner error: summarizing events rather than realizing them as moments ([Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.co.za/janet-burroways-4-aspects-of-narrative-time/); [Anna Schell](https://www.annaschellauthor.com/writing-craft-blog/from-janet-burroway-heighten-scene-with-summary); [Edan Lepucki, "On Summary"](https://edan.substack.com/p/dispatch-50-on-summary)).

### F4.2 Why summary breaks the reader's hold: Gardner's fictive dream [craft]

John Gardner, *The Art of Fiction*: good fiction sets off "a vivid and continuous dream." **Vivid** because if we are not clear who and where the characters are, what they are doing and why, "our emotions and judgments must be confused, dissipated or blocked." **Continuous** because a repeatedly interrupted flow of action necessarily has less force. Vividness is achieved *by detail* ([craft summary of the doctrine](https://medium.com/the-craft-of-fiction/the-vivid-and-continuous-dream-ffefb617fbb4); [The Blue Route](https://widenerblueroute.org/2021/01/21/fictional-dream/)).

Note the overlap with F1.3: Gardner's "who and where and what and why" is Percy's triad is Labov's orientation. Three independent traditions converge on the same list.

### F4.3 The same decision expressed as rhythm, not taxonomy [craft]

Le Guin, *Steering the Craft*, "Crowding and Leaping." **Crowd**: "load every rift with ore" (Keats), never ten vague words where two exact ones will do — vivid, exact, concrete, accurate, dense, rich. **Leap**: **"What you leap over is what you leave out. And what you leave out is infinitely more than what you leave in. Only the relevant belongs."** Her drafting advice is order-of-operations: crowd in the first draft, then cut what pads, repeats, slows or impedes ([Lane7 chapter notes](https://lane7.wordpress.com/2009/10/18/crowding-and-leaping-ursula-le-guin/); [Fantasy Author's Handbook chapter walkthrough](https://fantasyhandbook.wordpress.com/2025/11/25/books-for-fantasy-authors-xlix-steering-the-craft-part-10/)).

This is more useful to a beat map than Burroway's taxonomy, because it is a *per-beat allocation decision*: crowd here, leap there.

### F4.4 The other direction of failure [craft]

Elmore Leonard's rule 10: **"Try to leave out the part that readers tend to skip."** He names the offence **hooptedoodle** — borrowed from Steinbeck's *Sweet Thursday*, which titles two chapters "Hooptedoodle" as a warning to readers — and defines it as another shot at describing the weather, or the character's thoughts when the reader already knows them or doesn't care ([The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/08/21/elmore-leonard-10-rules-of-writing/); [Zinzin's full text of the NYT "Writers on Writing" essay, 2001](https://www.zinzin.com/observations/2022/hooptedoodle-elmore-leonards-10-rules-of-writing/)).

**Both directions are real failures.** All-summary produces the "and then" draft. All-scene produces hooptedoodle and Burroway's missing mortar. The cost of getting it backwards is not symmetric in kind: too much summary loses the reader's *presence*; too much scene loses their *patience*.

### F4.5 The empirical proxy, with its ceiling stated [science]

Mar, Li, Nguyen & Ta (2021), "Memory and comprehension of narrative versus expository texts: A meta-analysis," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 28:732–749 — **75+ unique samples, 33,000+ participants**: narrative texts are better comprehended and better recalled than expository ones; the result was robust, not driven by any single effect size or study, and not moderated by study characteristics ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-020-01853-1); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33410100/)). The offered explanation is that stories resemble everyday experience — temporally ordered causal events organised around personal goals — while expository text carries unfamiliar concepts and vocabulary.

**Ceiling, stated plainly:** this compares narrative *texts* to expository *texts*. It is not a within-piece test of scene-vs-summary passages. It is the closest empirical proxy available and it should be cited as a proxy, not as a direct finding.

A second, weaker support: the **concreteness effect** — concrete, imageable words are better recalled than abstract ones, explained by Paivio's dual coding (concrete words recruit both a verbal and an imagistic system; abstract words mainly the verbal one). This is among the most replicated findings in memory research ([dual coding overview](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/neuroscience/dual-coding-theory); [Paivio-tradition recall study, *Memory & Cognition*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03214222)). **Same ceiling, harder:** this is a word-level lab effect being extrapolated to passage-level craft. Tag any skill rule built on it as [craft] with [science] support, never as [science].

### F4.6 The journalism version, and a warning about scaffolds [craft]

Jack Hart (*Storycraft*, longtime Oregonian writing coach) distinguishes **reports** (abstract, informational) from **stories** (specific, experiential), and says writers should master "the highly specific scenic narrative, where you're describing specific events" rather than staying in "abstract middle-ground writing" — the same middle rung as F3.6.

Hart's second point is a caution the skill should absorb: writers frequently force material into "a full-blown story narrative, with a narrative arc and a climax and a denouement" when a different structure would serve better. His advice is to know several narrative forms and match the material to one, rather than defaulting to the arc ([Nieman Storyboard interview, 2011](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2011/10/20/jack-hart-storycraft-narrative-nonfiction-interview/)).

This *supports* the skill's existing anti-formula guard and its "form not formula" framing of the four scaffolds. Nothing there needs weakening.

## 5. What makes a reader stop

This is the weakest-evidenced of the five questions, and the honest headline is a negative one.

### F5.1 There is no empirical literature on essay and narrative-nonfiction abandonment specifically

I looked for it directly. What exists is three things, none of which is the study the skill would want:

1. web-analytics scroll telemetry on news and magazine articles,
2. publisher e-book completion telemetry,
3. lab studies of mind wandering during **expository** reading.

Nobody has run a study of where readers drop out of essays or narrative nonfiction. **Every "the reader disengages here" rule in the skill is therefore [craft], and must stay tagged that way.** The current mechanic 3 language ("in short form a loop closes within ~1-3 beats/paragraphs or the reader disengages from frustration") is already tagged [lore] in the reference; that tagging is correct and should not be upgraded.

### F5.2 The scroll-depth data, with its caveats [industry data]

Chartbeat's analysis of Slate, reported by Farhad Manjoo with data from Chartbeat data scientist Josh Schwartz ([Slate, 2013](https://slate.com/technology/2013/06/how-people-read-online-why-you-wont-finish-this-article.html)):

- **38%** of people who land on a page leave immediately.
- ~**5%** of those remaining never scroll at all.
- **Median scroll depth ≈ 50%** of the article (about the 1,000th pixel).
- Only ~**25%** of readers get past the 1,600th pixel.
- Across other Chartbeat-monitored sites, median scroll depth ~**60%**, with ~10% never scrolling.
- **Sharing does not track depth:** "articles that get a lot of tweets don't necessarily get read very deeply. Articles that get read deeply aren't necessarily generating a lot of tweets."

Caveats that must travel with these numbers: 2013, largely one publisher, proprietary methodology, never peer-reviewed, and **scroll position is not reading**. The last bullet is the most useful and most robust of the set, because it is a *dissociation* rather than a level — engagement metrics and depth metrics measure different things.

### F5.3 The book-completion data [industry data]

Jellybooks (Andrew Rhomberg), from opt-in beta readers instrumented in e-books:

- Average completion rate **40–50%**.
- Only **5%** of books tested were finished by more than 75% of readers.
- **"The completion rate is often decided in a book's first 100 pages"**; Rhomberg's stated figure for male readers is 20–50 pages to capture attention.
- The peak reader count is usually chapter 1, but is "not unusual" to see in the **front matter — before the reader reaches chapter 1 of the actual storyline**.

([Jellybooks completion-rate page](https://www.jellybooks.com/about/jellybooks-radar/completion-rate); [Jane Friedman's write-up and author reactions](https://janefriedman.com/over-the-readers-shoulder-reactions-to-jellybooks-analytics/); [BigDATAwire](https://www.hpcwire.com/bigdatawire/2016/03/16/tracking-bookworms-reader-analytics/))

Caveats: books not essays; self-selected beta readers; a commercial vendor reporting on its own product; not peer-reviewed. The front-matter finding is the interesting one and the one most likely to generalise — **a meaningful fraction of loss happens before the story starts at all**, which is an argument against front-loaded setup rather than an argument about structure.

### F5.4 The academically respectable proxy is mind wandering, and it comes with a replication caveat [science]

Meta-analysis of mind wandering and reading comprehension (*Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 2022; 25 full texts, 73 correlation coefficients): pooled **r = −0.21**, a significant negative relationship ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-022-02141-w)).

**The honesty note that belongs in the skill alongside the Zeigarnik one:** the same meta-analysis found that *how you measure it* changes the answer by more than the answer itself. Using trait-based questionnaires instead of in-the-moment probes shifted the correlation by ~0.30 — **to null**. The effect is real when you catch readers in the act and vanishes when you ask them about their general tendencies. Any craft rule built on "readers zone out" is standing on an effect that is measurement-fragile.

Mind wandering also specifically hinders recognition and recall of a text's most central ideas and impairs the development of a coherent situation model — which links directly back to F1.4/F1.5 and F2.2.

### F5.5 What predicts it: difficulty and interest [science]

- Soemer & Schiefele (2019), "Text difficulty, topic interest, and mind wandering during reading," *Learning and Instruction* 61:12–22 — 125 eighth-graders reading easy, moderate or difficult versions of an expository text. **Both voluntary and involuntary mind wandering were negatively related to topic interest**, and interest appears to mediate the difficulty→mind-wandering relationship ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S095947521830389X)).
- A follow-up finds **mind wandering increases linearly with text difficulty** ([*Psychological Research*, 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-021-01483-9); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8821482/)).

**Caveat:** expository text, school-age readers. Extrapolating to adults reading essays is an inference, not a finding. But the direction is the one the craft literature assumes, and the *linear* form is worth noting — there is no cliff, which argues against any "you have N paragraphs" rule.

### F5.6 The negative finding on transportation — do not cite it for this

Narrative transportation is the best-measured construct in the neighbourhood and it **does not measure whether people finish**:

- Green & Brock (2000), "The role of transportation in the persuasiveness of public narratives" — transportation as absorption into a story involving imagery, affect and attentional focus; a validated 15-item scale ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11079236/); [short-form scale preprint](https://www.mcm.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06110300/user_upload/Publikationen/Appel-_Gnambs-_Richter-_-_Green_-_TS-SF_-_PREPRINT.pdf)).
- Busselle & Bilandzic (2009), Narrative Engagement Measure, *Media Psychology* 12:321–347 — four dimensions: **narrative understanding, attentional focus, narrative presence, emotional engagement** ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/15213260903287259); [full text PDF](http://hypermedia468.pbworks.com/w/file/fetch/80687372/measuring%20narrative%20engagement.pdf)).
- van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti & Wetzels (2014), *Journal of Consumer Research* 40:797 — meta-analysis of 132 effect sizes from 76 articles; mean r ≈ .40 for attitudes, .28 for intentions ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/40/5/797/2907487)).

**All of these are measured on people who already read the thing.** They are persuasion and absorption outcomes, not persistence outcomes. Do not cite transportation as evidence that a structure makes people finish. That would be the same category error as the Zeigarnik memory claim the reference already corrects.

**The one usable bridge:** Busselle & Bilandzic put **narrative understanding** inside engagement as a dimension of it. That is a scholarly warrant for the claim §2 needs — *comprehension failure does not merely make a reader confused, it registers as disengagement.* Disorientation and stopping are the same axis, not two problems.

### F5.7 The craft position, which the data does not contradict [craft]

Kilgore's line, via Clark: **"The easiest thing for the reader to do is to quit reading."** ([Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2023/02/07/roy-peter-clark-writing-tools-narrative-craft/)) Continuing is the thing that must be earned continuously, and gold coins (F3.7) are the named response.

## 6. Things that did not survive, or that came back weaker than expected

- **Freytag's pyramid.** The brief asked about Freytag and his critics. Findings: it was built to dissect **five-act classical tragedy** — much of Freytag's own vocabulary is tragic, including "catastrophe" — and the flat baseline segments in the familiar diagram were **added by later theorists, not drawn by Freytag** ([Mythcreants critique](https://mythcreants.com/blog/freytags-pyramid-is-outdated-heres-what-to-do-instead/); [a defence, Storm Writing School](https://stormwritingschool.com/freytags-pyramid/)). It has no purchase on essay or newsletter structure and the skill is right not to include it among its scaffolds. Recommend not adding it.
- **The Thompson opening has no craft-critical literature behind Sean's reading of it.** The one serious essay on the line goes elsewhere (F1.6). His reading holds up under the discourse-processing research, but it is *his*, and the note should say so rather than manufacture an authority.
- **Bruner's narrative vs paradigmatic modes** ([*Narrative and Paradigmatic Modes of Thought*, 1985](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/016146818508600606)) is frequently cited in this territory — narrative mode aims at verisimilitude and meaning, paradigmatic mode at truth and verification. It is a good frame for *why* the maker-post genre works, but it produces no gate the skill can enforce. Noted and left out.
- **No source supports a numeric loop-closure window.** The "~1-3 beats/paragraphs" figure in mechanic 3 has no empirical backing and none appeared. F5.5's linear difficulty effect actively argues against a cliff. Keep the figure, keep it tagged [lore], and consider softening "or the reader disengages" to a hedge.

## What this changes in storytelling-architecture

Fourteen amendments, each pointing at the finding that licenses it. Not a plan — findings the skill owner decides on.

**Openings (mechanic 2)**

1. **Add an orientation gate as a co-equal second half of mechanic 2.** The cold open must supply *who* (protagonist), *when* (temporal footing) and *what is happening right now*. Currently the mechanic gates only for concreteness and one raised question. → F1.3, F2.1.
2. **Make the orientation gate dimension-aware, not generic.** Place is the one dimension that can be left vague cheaply; protagonist, time, causality and goal cannot. This is a sharper, more checkable rule than "give some context." → F1.5, F2.3.
3. **Add a given-new check to the first beat.** Every referent the opening treats as already-known must actually be known to a cold reader; anything else forces a bridging inference. Name the failure mode in the skill — this is the mechanism behind the subway-monologue draft. → F2.2.
4. **Add "promise" language alongside "hook."** Mechanic 2 is currently about raising a question. The sources say an opening also signs a tonal contract about what the whole piece will feel like. Two obligations, one sentence. → F1.1, F1.2.

**In medias res (mechanic 2, cont.)**

5. **Replace "scene vs result" with the enforceable test:** *is the reader inside something still happening, or being told about something that already finished?* Results are not banned; entering after the turn is. Record that Vonnegut's "start as close to the end as possible" and the enter-late/leave-early maxim mean late *within* an unresolved moment. → F2.4.
6. **Add the bait-and-switch check to the Handoff Block.** The open-loop ledger tracks questions; it does not track the opening's concrete material. Whatever the opening puts on stage must recur. → F2.6.
7. **Record the nut-graf vs fictive-dream disagreement in the reference rather than resolving it,** with the rule both camps share: orientation lands early; whether it gets its own beat is a form choice, not a correctness question. → F2.5.

**Through-line and drive**

8. **Add the turn test as a peer of the but/therefore test.** But/therefore checks the seam *between* beats; the turn checks *inside* one. A beat whose value charge is unchanged end-to-end is a nonevent, and a map of well-connected nonevents is exactly the "I did this, then this" draft. This is the single highest-value amendment in this note. → F3.4.
9. **Fix the beat/scene terminology in the reference.** What the skill calls a beat is McKee's *scene*; McKee's *beat* is an action/reaction exchange inside it. The skill cites McKee for the step-outline, so the mislabel makes the citation wrong. → F3.4.
10. **Add a spine field to the Handoff Block: the Gornick "story."** The block currently names central loop, crest and seam — three positional coordinates and no statement of *what the piece has come to say*. Add it as a required field distinct from the situation. → F3.2, F3.1.
11. **Add an abstraction-rung check to the specificity gate.** The gate catches generic beats. It does not catch mid-rung reporting, which can be entirely specific to this piece and still inert. Reject beats written on the middle rung. → F3.6, F4.6.
12. **Add gold coins to mechanic 6 (or as a seventh mechanic).** Forward pull at section ends is not the only pull device; a mid-piece reward is separately named and separately checkable. → F3.7.

**Scene / summary**

13. **Add a scene/summary budget to the beat map.** Mark each beat SCENE or SUMMARY. Turning points, confrontations and crises must be SCENE; summary is legitimate and necessary for background, motive, pacing, transitions and time-leaps. A map that is all SUMMARY is the diagnosed failure; a map that is all SCENE is hooptedoodle. Le Guin's crowd/leap is the better per-beat framing than the taxonomy. → F4.1, F4.3, F4.4.

**Evidence floor**

14. **Add the `[industry data]` tag and a second honesty note to the reference.** Specifically: (a) there is *no* empirical literature on where readers abandon essays or narrative nonfiction — the abandonment claims rest on 2013 scroll telemetry, publisher e-book telemetry and expository-text mind-wandering studies; (b) the mind-wandering effect (r = −0.21) goes to *null* under trait-based measurement, so it is measurement-fragile; (c) **do not cite narrative transportation or the Narrative Engagement Measure as evidence that a structure makes people finish** — they are measured on people who already finished, and citing them that way is the same category error as the Zeigarnik memory claim the reference already corrects. → F5.1, F5.2, F5.3, F5.4, F5.6.

**Explicitly not changed.** The anti-formula guard, the "form not formula" treatment of the four scaffolds, and the chain contract (structure owns order, voice owns sentences) all survive contact with these sources and are reinforced by Hart's warning against forcing material into a default arc (F4.6) and Le Guin's placement of voice below the level any beat framework reaches. Do not add Freytag (§6). Do not import Vonnegut's sentence rule as a phrasing instruction — it belongs downstream (F3.5).
