## 1. Structured Methods for Anticipating AI System Failures

The integration of probabilistic LLMs into deterministic software pipelines has rendered traditional ad-hoc risk brainstorming insufficient. To engineer reliable AI systems, the industry has adapted classical safety and reliability engineering frameworks. The efficacy of these methods depends entirely on how they accommodate the non-deterministic, opaque, and context-dependent nature of modern machine learning models, as well as their alignment with emerging international standards such as ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF), and IEC 62443 [cite: 1, 2, 3].

### Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) and ML-FMEA
FMEA is a highly structured, step-by-step methodology for identifying all possible failures in a design, a manufacturing process, or a product or service. Its traditional procedure involves decomposing a system into components, identifying potential failure modes for each, determining their effects, and assigning a Risk Priority Number (RPN) based on severity, occurrence, and detectability [cite: 4, 5]. 

In the context of AI systems, traditional FMEA has been specifically adapted into ML-FMEA, which treats machine learning not as a static artifact but as an iterative, eleven-step lifecycle pipeline spanning from initial data collection and processing to model training, deployment, and continuous monitoring [cite: 6, 7]. The adaptation shifts the focus from component hardware failures to systemic algorithmic risks such as data distribution shifts, feature engineering errors, and model decay [cite: 4]. Crucially, ML-FMEA has been augmented with Artificial Intelligence techniques itself. Researchers have utilized Large Language Models (LLMs) and ontology-based knowledge graphs to automate failure prediction, prioritize RPNs, and extract knowledge from operational maintenance logs to combat the traditionally time-consuming, document-centric nature of the process [cite: 5, 8]. This approach connects each pipeline step with failure causes and known mitigations, enabling risk transparency with safety experts while aligning with ISO/IEC 42001 governance standards [cite: 3, 4].

### System-Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA/STAMP)
Originating from the Systems-Theoretic Accident Model and Processes (STAMP), STPA approaches safety as a control problem rather than a component failure problem. The procedure involves defining system boundaries and identifying losses and hazards, modeling the control structure (including controllers, control actions, and feedback loops), identifying Unsafe Control Actions (UCAs), and generating loss scenarios describing the causal factors leading to hazards [cite: 9]. 

The adaptation of STPA to AI systems is particularly potent for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and multi-agent LLM systems, as it excels at identifying emergent hazards caused by complex system interactions rather than isolated component breakdowns. Research indicates that STPA applied to RAG-LLM architectures yields highly specific critical hazard scenarios, such as unauthorized user reliance, undetected unacceptable output, and control loop bypasses [cite: 9, 10]. While generative AI has been utilized as a "co-pilot" to accelerate STPA by drafting control structures via preparatory and persona prompting, empirical studies demonstrate that current models struggle with genuine "systems thinking" [cite: 9]. AI performance in STPA remains non-deterministic, often favoring traditional linear risk assessment approaches over understanding dynamic, non-linear interactions, which requires heavy human supervision and validation [cite: 9].

### Fault Tree Analysis (FTA)
Fault Tree Analysis is a top-down, deductive failure analysis methodology utilizing Boolean logic (AND/OR gates) to combine lower-level events into a top-level catastrophic failure [cite: 11, 12]. FTA is heavily relied upon in functional safety domains, such as aerospace and automotive engineering, to calculate probabilistic risks [cite: 13]. 

The adaptation of FTA to machine learning systems, notably termed Co-Hazard Analysis (CoHA), leverages LLMs and Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) to process highly complex, sensor-rich industrial data [cite: 14, 15]. A significant documented change is the transition from static, image-based fault trees to dynamic, textually represented trees (e.g., JFTA syntax) that LLMs can ingest and navigate [cite: 12]. While generative AI significantly accelerates the proposition of new sub-causes and automates fault pattern recognition, research underscores that LLMs must act strictly in a supportive role [cite: 11, 15]. Automated FTA generation often lacks the necessary domain specificity unless anchored by rigorous Retrieval-Augmented Generation frameworks or domain-specific Knowledge Graphs, demonstrating that human-in-the-loop validation remains indispensable [cite: 13, 14].

### Hazard and Operability Study (HAZOP)
HAZOP is a systematic, qualitative technique utilizing specific guide words (e.g., MORE, LESS, NONE, REVERSE) applied to process parameters (flow, temperature, pressure) to identify deviations from design intent within divided process nodes [cite: 16, 17]. When a HAZOP identifies a severe risk, a Layers of Protection Analysis (LOPA) typically determines the required Safety Integrity Level (SIL) [cite: 17].

The adaptation of HAZOP to AI systems—and the reciprocal use of AI to conduct HAZOP (CAHAZOP)—is an area of intense research. Recent studies evaluating the capacity of LLMs (such as GPT-4o and Gemini) to automate HAZOP worksheets directly from piping and instrumentation diagrams (P&IDs) report high superficial similarity to human output, exceeding 86% syntactic alignment [cite: 17, 18]. However, strict peer-reviewed evaluations reveal a stark contrast in semantic validity: only 19% to 37% of LLM-generated scenarios are technically robust [cite: 16, 18]. Models exhibit severe biases toward administrative paperwork rather than active engineering safeguards and hallucinate protective layers [cite: 16, 18]. Therefore, the genuine adaptation of HAZOP for AI systems involves integrating multimodal LLMs for entity extraction, node definition, and documentation cleanup, while retaining human engineering judgment for the ultimate safety filter and causal logic evaluation [cite: 16, 18, 19, 20].

### Chaos Engineering
Pioneered by organizations operating massive distributed systems, chaos engineering involves proactively and intentionally injecting faults into a system in production to verify its resilience, uncover hidden weaknesses, and prevent catastrophic degradation [cite: 21, 22, 23]. 

For LLM architectures, chaos engineering has been adapted to address vulnerabilities unique to neural generation and multi-agent systems (LLM-MAS). The framework has been modified to simulate non-deterministic disruptions such as context contamination, embedding server dropouts, vector database latency, prompt injection payloads, and agent communication delays [cite: 21, 24]. Research from 2025 and 2026 details platforms like ChaosEater, which utilize LLMs themselves to automate the entire lifecycle of chaos experiments [cite: 22]. This shifts the discipline from manual fault injection to continuous, agent-driven adversarial testing across data preprocessing, model inference, and serving stages, dynamically revealing bottlenecks in the model's workflow [cite: 22, 23].

### Pre-Mortem and Prospective Hindsight
The pre-mortem, rooted in the psychological concept of prospective hindsight popularized by Gary Klein, assumes a project has catastrophically failed in the future and works backward to identify the causes [cite: 25, 26]. Empirical evidence demonstrates that assuming failure as a factual certainty surfaces significantly more actionable risks than abstract brainstorming [cite: 25]. 

In the AI domain, this has evolved into "AI Pre-mortems," where simulated panels of adversarial LLM personas (e.g., a skeptic, a security chief, an auditor, a regulator) independently generate specific failure narratives and trace them back to foundational assumptions [cite: 25]. Furthermore, research has introduced "HindSight" evaluation frameworks for AI-generated systems. HindSight scores negatively correlate with LLM-judged novelty, demonstrating that prospective hindsight methodologies are strictly necessary to separate theoretical capability from operational reality [cite: 27].

### Threat Modeling: STRIDE and ASTRIDE
Microsoft's STRIDE (Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclosure, Denial of Service, Elevation of Privilege) is the foundational standard for mapping data flows, explicitly tagging trust boundaries, and identifying security threats before code is written [cite: 28, 29, 30]. 

Because autonomous AI agents face attacks not captured by classic software paradigms, the 2025 and 2026 adaptation, ASTRIDE (adding an 'A' for AI-specific attacks), explicitly maps unique threat vectors [cite: 28]. ## 3. Tacit-to-Explicit Expertise: Converting Skill to Articulable Reasoning

The ability to design complex AI systems relies heavily on tacit expertise—the deep, internalized knowledge gained through years of hands-on experience that is inherently difficult to formalize. Converting this internal tacit skill into explicit, articulable reasoning is critical for passing rigorous technical assessments, executing engineering design reviews, and responding to production incidents. Learning science and cognitive psychology establish distinct mechanisms for how this transfer occurs and how environmental pressures impact articulation.

### Cognitive Task Analysis and Think-Aloud Protocols
To externalize internal reasoning, researchers and usability engineers rely heavily on Cognitive Task Analysis (CTA), utilizing techniques like the Think-Aloud Protocol (TAP) rooted in Ericsson and Simon’s foundational framework [cite: 35, 36, 37]. The methodology forces practitioners to verbalize their cognitive processes step-by-step while navigating complex tasks, theoretically making the invisible mental process visible [cite: 35].

However, research explicitly distinguishes between Concurrent Think-Aloud (speaking while executing a task) and Retrospective Think-Aloud (explaining reasoning post-completion) [cite: 36, 38, 39]. Empirical findings demonstrate that concurrent verbalization imposes a severe cognitive load. In complex engineering and usability tasks, the dual-task requirement of solving a technical problem while simultaneously narrating the underlying logic creates immense interference, heavily taxing working memory [cite: 36, 40]. Studies demonstrate this cognitive load can reduce task success rates significantly; for example, success rates dropped from 47% to 37% when participants were forced to narrate their actions concurrently [cite: 36]. 

This dynamic is closely tied to the expertise-reversal effect. Highly skilled professionals operate using automated, internalized routines (reflexive processing). When forced to break these routines down into explicit, step-by-step explanations, they often stumble, as their attention is redirected from the task itself back to conscious control processes, degrading their overall performance [cite: 41].

### Scaffolding, Case-Based Reasoning, and the "Memorization Trap"
To build the capacity for explicit articulation, learning science leverages worked-example and faded-scaffolding effects. Novices benefit from studying fully solved case studies (worked examples) to build mental models. Over time, scaffolding is faded, forcing the practitioner to engage in self-explanation and case-based reasoning to solve novel problems.

In the context of software architecture, failure to properly transition from worked examples to deep causal reasoning results in what industry experts term the "memorization trap." Engineers may memorize standard architectural patterns (the worked examples) but lack the underlying competence to explain *why* the components interact as they do [cite: 42]. When forced to articulate reasoning, they rely on pattern recall rather than genuine understanding. Consequently, when constraints change (e.g., a network partition occurs), they cannot articulate the necessary trade-offs, exposing a shallow level of expertise [cite: 42].

### Dual-Task Processing, Time Pressure, and Speech Fluency
High-stakes engineering environments inherently feature time pressure. Research investigating performance under pressure differentiates between "choking" (systematic suboptimal performance due to pressure interfering with automated control processes) and "clutching" (increased performance in effort-based tasks) [cite: 40, 41]. 

The attentional focus model suggests that a lack of time resources restricts an individual's attention scope, causing them to focus intensely on task-related factors [cite: 43]. However, the intersection of dual-task conditions (thinking technically plus speaking fluently) and time pressure significantly degrades speech fluency [cite: 44]. Experts under this compounded pressure exhibit marked disfluencies, false starts, and linguistic overload—struggling to access the necessary vocabulary to explain their systemic logic [cite: 44, 45]. Furthermore, studies on neurobiologically-inspired dual-learning systems reveal a direct competition between the reflective (conscious) and reflexive (procedural) learning systems; performance pressure attenuates the reflective system, making rule-based, explicit explanation highly vulnerable to breakdown [cite: 40].

### Training Fluent Verbal Articulation of Technical Reasoning
Crucially, training the underlying technical competence is distinct from training the fluent verbal articulation of that competence under pressure. To counter articulation degradation, empirical research points to specific interventions.
