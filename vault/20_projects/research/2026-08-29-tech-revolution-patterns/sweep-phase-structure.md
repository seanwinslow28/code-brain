## SOURCES

### 1. Carlota Perez — "The Double Bubble at the Turn of the Century" (CERF WP No. 31, May 2009; published CJE 33(4))
- **URL:** https://carlotaperez.org/wp-content/downloads/publications/theoretical-framework/C.PEREZ_CJE_DoubleBubble_WP%20CERF%20No%2031.pdf (read pp. 1–14)
- **Classification:** scholarly/primary — the best fetchable primary statement of the 2002 book's framework, with the canonical Table 1.
- A "major technology bubble" regularly occurs *midway* through assimilation of each revolution, culminating twenty–thirty years of experimentation (p. 5). The collapse "signals the end of a period when financial capital is in control" — control passes to production capital (p. 5).
- Sequence: irruption → 2–3-decade **Installation** ending in bubble collapse → institutional recomposition → **Deployment** ("more organic growth") until maturity; each surge 50–60 years (pp. 5–6).
- **Table 1 canonical dates:** (i) 1771, canal mania, 1793 collapse; (ii) 1829, railway mania, **1847**; (iii) 1875, global-infra bubbles, 1890–93; (iv) 1908, Roaring Twenties, **1929**; (v) 1971 (Intel), double bubble, **2000 and 2007–08** → fifth Deployment marked only **"?"**.
- Phase-timing evidence: 1847 UK railway investment ≈ **7% of national income, ~55% of gross fixed capital formation** (p. 8); 1990s: VC ~1% of US GDP, 85–90% to ICT; tech 35% of US market cap; NASDAQ avg P/E >200× (pp. 8–12); 1996–2000 non-financial profits flat while market cap soared (p. 13).
- On app-vs-rails: infrastructure is overinstalled *by* the bubble during Installation; broad application-driven prosperity is a Deployment phenomenon after crash + recomposition — ~20–30 years from big bang to deployment start in every surge.

### 2. Carlota Perez — "The advance of technology and major bubble collapses" (Engelsberg essay, 2011)
- **URL:** https://carlotaperez.org/wp-content/downloads/media/articles-and-blogs/PEREZTechnologyandbubblesforEngelsbergseminar.pdf
- **Classification:** primary essay (not peer-reviewed).
- Bubbles paradoxically finance the infrastructure that enables subsequent growth (canals, railways, dot-com fiber); golden ages follow collapse as technologies become utilities. As of 2011 she placed ICT deployment *still not achieved*.
- **NOTE:** no AI-specific Perez paper exists as of this sweep — her publications page (fetched) lists nothing on AI 2023–2026; most recent is Lema & Perez 2024 on the *green* transformation. **Anyone quoting "Perez says AI is in the frenzy phase" is quoting an interpreter, not her.**

### 3. Bresnahan & Trajtenberg — "General purpose technologies: 'Engines of growth'?" (J. Econometrics 65, 1995)
- **URL (PDF read pp. 83–90):** https://tbres.su.domains/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Bresnahan-and-Trajtenberg-1995-General-purpose-technologies-%E2%80%98Engines-of-growth.pdf
- **Classification:** scholarly (founding GPT paper).
- GPTs: pervasiveness, inherent improvement potential, *innovational complementarities* — downstream R&D productivity rises as the GPT improves. GPTs enable; electricity's gains were the factory redesign it permitted, not cheaper energy (p. 84).
- Core market failure: decentralized economy delivers *too little, too late* innovation; **vertical externality** (neither GPT nor application sector captures the surplus its innovation creates for the other — "dual appropriability") and **horizontal** (each adopter raises returns to GPT improvement benefiting all others) (pp. 85–88).
- Translation: the application layer is where surplus is created and where the economy chronically underinvests — the standing arbitrage the theory names.

### 4. Paul A. David — "The Dynamo and the Computer" (AER 80(2), May 1990)
- **URL (read in full):** https://www.almendron.com/tribuna/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/the-dynamo-and-the-computer-an-historical-perspective-on-the-modern-productivity-paradox.pdf
- **Classification:** scholarly (canonical diffusion-lag paper).
- 1899: electric lighting in 3% of US residences; electric motors <5% of factory drive; ~two more decades to 50% diffusion (p. 356). Factory electrification's productivity impact arrived early 1920s — "four decades after the first central power station opened for business" (p. 357).
- **Why the lag:** unprofitable to scrap serviceable steam plants; 1910s retrofits ("group drive") *raised* capital-output ratio; the "unit drive" unlocked gains but required *new* factories, new locations, experienced factory architects, the 1920s fixed-capital boom (pp. 357–358).
- Quantified payoff: ~**half of the 5-point acceleration in US manufacturing TFP growth 1919–29** statistically accounted for by secondary electric-motor capacity growth (p. 359). Both leading economies saw productivity *slowdown* 1890–1913 — mid-installation, productivity got worse (p. 356).
- His own caution: computers are not dynamos — information structures don't physically depreciate, so nothing forces periodic redesign (p. 360).

### 5. Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson — "The Productivity J-Curve" (NBER WP 25148, 2018; AEJ:Macro 2021)
- **URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w25148 · **Classification:** scholarly.
- GPTs require large complementary *intangible* investments measured poorly by national accounts; measured productivity first dips, then surges. Adjusted TFP 11.3% above official by end-2004, 15.9% by end-2017 for the computer wave; AI intangible effects "small but growing" at publication. Converts David's physical-rebuild story into an intangibles story — the version that survives the "AI needs no factories" objection.

### 6. Andrew Odlyzko — "Collective Hallucinations and Inefficient Markets: The British Railway Mania of the 1840s" (UMN, 2010)
- **URL (read pp. 1–10 of 187):** https://www-users.cse.umn.edu/~odlyzko/doc/hallucinations.pdf
- **Classification:** scholarly working paper.
- The greatest technology mania in history: peak private railway investment more than twice Britain's military spending; 1847 investment share ≈ >$1T scaled to today's US (pp. 4, 9).
- Miles sanctioned: 91 (1843) → 805 → 2,700 → **4,538 (1846)** → 1,354 (1847) → 8 (1850); capital peaked 1847 at ~£44M vs ~£50M national budget (pp. 6–8).
- Investors crushed: index roughly halved 1845→late 1849; Brontë's shares £50→120→20. The 1855 *Economist*: railways are "an honour to our age and country: commercially, they are great failures." (public domain, quoted p. 5).
- **The data to see it coming existed** — trustworthy demand measures available in advance showed revenue expectations couldn't be met; a "collective hallucination" made investors ignore them. Same for long-haul fiber and European 3G (~$100B/€100B each) (p. 8).
- "Railway speed" in the 1840s = "Internet time" in the 1990s (p. 9).

### 7. Doug O'Laughlin — "Lessons from History: The Rise and Fall of the Telecom Bubble" (Fabricated Knowledge, Oct 16, 2023)
- **URL:** https://www.fabricatedknowledge.com/p/lessons-from-history-the-rise-and
- **Classification:** analyst (trade history, not peer-reviewed).
- Peak telecom capex ~$120B/yr; cumulative >$500B; capacity up ~186,000× in seven years vs. demand orders of magnitude smaller; "traffic doubles every 100 days" was false (reality ~annual); by 2002 only ~2.7% of installed fiber lit; WorldCom/Global Crossing/60+ carriers failed 2001–03. Survivors inherited drastically cheaper bandwidth → 2000s internet growth.
- AI parallel: "training demand doubles every ~3.5 months" rhymes with the 100-days myth; flagged disanalogy — today's builders (Microsoft, Google, Meta, Nvidia) fund capex from cash flows with minimal net debt.
- Corroborating (not deeply read): TechFinitive on post-crash dark fiber enabling YouTube (bandwidth ~90% cheaper): https://www.techfinitive.com/features/the-infrastructure-nobody-wanted-is-the-infrastructure-everyone-uses/

### 8. David Cahn — "AI's $600B Question" (Sequoia, June 20, 2024)
- **URL:** https://sequoiacap.com/article/ais-600b-question · **Classification:** analyst (VC, market participant; transparent arithmetic).
- Implied annual AI revenue needed to justify buildout grew $200B (Sept 2023) → $600B (mid-2024) while OpenAI revenue doubled to $3.4B. Three breaks in the railroad analogy: GPU compute lacks rails' physical-monopoly pricing; speculative waves incinerate capital; semiconductors depreciate on a Moore's-Law clock unlike century-life rails. Winners/losers: investors bear destruction; founders and consumers get subsidized-then-cheap compute.

### 9. Wang & Chen — "Boom, Bubble, or Buildout?" (arXiv:2606.01575, June 2026)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01575 · **Classification:** scholarly preprint (abstract-level read).
- Five-pillar diagnostic; verdict: a real technological revolution with *localized* bubble dynamics — neither pure mania nor bubble-free. Fragilities: capex accelerating faster than observed monetization in some layers; valuation concentration.

## SYNTHESIS

### (1) Evidenced vs. asserted phase-structure claims

**Evidenced (multiple quantified episodes):**
- **Infrastructure equity as a class loses money in the buildout, even when the technology succeeds.** Railways 1840s + fiber 1990s; canals arguably a third.
- **Overbuilt infrastructure survives the crash at written-down cost and becomes the cheap input for the next layer.** Post-1849 rails; post-2002 dark fiber → YouTube/Web 2.0.
- **A multi-decade complementary-innovation lag between GPT availability and measured payoff.** Electrification quantified (David); computing formalized (Brynjolfsson et al.).
- **Manias concentrate on the new infrastructural networks at extraordinary investment shares.** 1847 ≈ 55% of UK gross fixed capital formation; 1990s VC/market-cap concentration (Perez).
- **The overshoot is foreseeable from demand data at the time.** Odlyzko's central documented finding, ×3 episodes (railways, fiber, 3G).

**Asserted / weakly evidenced:**
- The clockwork itself (five 50–60-year surges): n=5, theorist-chosen periodization; her Table 1 marks the fifth deployment "?"; the framework needed a "double bubble" patch to fit 2000–2008. The *sequence* rhymes; the *schedule* is not a forecasting tool.
- "The crash is functionally necessary" — a reading, not her claim; B&T's model implies coordination could avoid the incineration.
- The hype cycle's shape — empirically falsified as a general law.
- Amara's law — proverb, unknown author.
- "Deployment golden age reliably follows" — survivorship; ICT's golden age still pending on Perez's own account.

### (2) What each episode says about app-vs-rails value timing

- **Railways:** rails capital earned sub-par returns permanently; durable value went to *users* of cheap transport and later consolidators buying below replacement cost. Application capture began ~5–15 years after peak buildout, post-crash.
- **Electrification:** utilities regulated into modest returns; surplus went to factory owners who performed the *unit-drive redesign* — application-layer actors who rebuilt operations around the input, 30–40 years after the tech existed. **Retrofitters got worse capital productivity. Adoption ≠ redesign; the value was in redesign.**
- **Fiber/dot-com:** rails equity annihilated; application layer captured durable value 3–8 years post-crash, on 90%-cheaper surviving infrastructure.
- **GPT theory:** the persistent, theory-named gap is *under-supplied application-layer complements* — precisely the complement-the-rails position.
- **Cross-episode pattern for the founder's constraint:** complementing rails is the historically favored seat, **but its timing trigger is rails-input commoditization, not rails announcement.** The application golden age began when the rails input got cheap. The AI-era analogue to watch: inference/token prices and payment-rail take-rates, not model releases. Wrinkle in the founder's favor: AI's input price is collapsing *during* the boom — may pull the application window earlier than the historical post-crash lag.

### (3) Honest disanalogies for AI

1. **The rails rot.** GPUs depreciate in ~3–5 years vs. fiber's 25+ and rail beds' century. A post-crash "dark compute" inheritance may largely not exist. (Counterpoint: power, shells, interconnect, and trained weights persist longer than silicon.)
2. **No leverage, different crash.** AI capex funded from hyperscaler operating cash flow; a demand shortfall produces write-downs, not a bankruptcy wave dumping assets at pennies — the "buy the rails cheap" play may never open.
3. **The physical rebuild is (mostly) gone — but the organizational rebuild isn't.** David's lag was factory architecture; AI distributes instantly. But the modern lag lives in intangibles — process, workflow, data, trust, institutional redesign (Brynjolfsson et al.). Expect a compressed but real J-curve. (And agentic AI *does* have a physical layer — power, grid queues, datacenters.)
4. **This time the rails vendors are not commodities.** 1840s rails and 1990s fiber competed to marginal cost. Stripe and Cloudflare are platform businesses with network effects, pricing power, and a habit of expanding up-stack. The complement-builder can't count on the input being driven to marginal cost — and must watch platform-envelopment risk the historical rails never posed.
5. **Contested position on the curve.** Genuinely unresolved whether AI is (a) a sixth surge in irruption/frenzy (→ application golden age 10–20 years out) or (b) the *deployment* engine of the unfinished ICT surge (→ application value capturable now). Opposite build-timing advice; Perez has published nothing settling it.
6. **Revenue exists this time.** Unlike 1845 railways and 1999 telecom, current AI shows real fast-growing usage and revenue; the gap is *scale of capex*, not existence of demand ("localized bubble dynamics").

### (4) Unsettled — candidates for a paid deep-research run

1. Systematic return decomposition by layer across episodes (infrastructure vs equipment vs application equity, long-run realized returns) — Odlyzko has rails; nobody quantifies the application side rigorously. Research-shaped, should pull tier-A.
2. Perez applied to AI by serious scholars: any peer-reviewed 2024–26 adjudication of "sixth surge vs ICT deployment."
3. GPU/datacenter depreciation economics vs fiber: does a "cheap inheritance" layer survive an AI capex correction? Biggest analogy break, least scholarly coverage.
4. Measured complementary-lag length for AI so far: micro-productivity RCTs aggregated against the J-curve prediction.
5. **Platform-envelopment base rates:** how often complements to *non-commodity* platform rails retained durable value vs got absorbed — the right reference class for the Stripe/Cloudflare-complement strategy is platform economics (Gawer/Cusumano lineage), not railway history; this sweep did not cover it.
6. Falsification of "agentic-web rails are underbuilt vs overbuilt": reuse Odlyzko's method — check whether demand data available *today* (agent transaction volumes, x402/ACP adoption) supports the buildout. Nobody appears to have done it.
