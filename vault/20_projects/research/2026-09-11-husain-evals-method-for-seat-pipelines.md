# Husain's evals method, read against a seven-seat artifact pipeline

Resolves [#289](https://github.com/seanwinslow28/code-brain/issues/289) (child of #264). Facts and sources only; the design ticket (#272) decides. Every claim below was read from the primary page on 2026-09-11 and the numeric quotes were re-checked against the raw HTML of the three long posts. Quotes are kept under 25 words.

**Headline.** Across "Your AI Product Needs Evals" (2024), "A Field Guide to Rapidly Improving AI Products" (2025), "Creating a LLM-as-a-Judge That Drives Business Results" (2024, since revised), and the 2025–2026 Evals FAQ co-written with Shreya Shankar, Husain prescribes one invariant order: log full traces, have a single domain expert read them in a purpose-built viewer, write binary pass/fail plus a free-text critique per trace (open coding), group the critiques into a failure taxonomy and count (axial coding), fix the obvious failures directly, and only then build a judge, per persistent failure mode, validated against held-out human labels by true positive rate and true negative rate rather than raw agreement. For agents and multi-step chains the FAQ adds a specific rule: annotate only the first upstream failure per trace and tabulate a transition failure matrix (last good step by first failing step). The EvalGen paper supplies the theory for why criteria cannot be fixed ahead of reading outputs (criteria drift). Almost everything transfers to a one-expert, no-user, local-only pipeline; the parts that do not are production sampling, user-feedback signals, A/B tests, and the cadences that assume live traffic.

## 1. Prescribed order of operations, first trace to first judge

The three sources agree on the sequence; the FAQ states it most compactly.

1. **Log traces.** "Level 2: Model & Human Eval" begins with "Logging Traces" then "Looking At Your Traces" in the 2024 post; the FAQ's minimum viable setup says to "Start with error analysis, not infrastructure" and review "20-50 LLM outputs" in "30 minutes" per cycle. ([evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/), [MVE FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/whats-a-minimum-viable-evaluation-setup.html))
2. **Error analysis first, by the expert.** "Error analysis helps you decide what evals to write in the first place." The error-analysis FAQ orders it: create a dataset (real or synthetic traces) → open coding → axial coding → iterate to saturation. ([error analysis FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/why-is-error-analysis-so-important-in-llm-evals-and-how-is-it-performed.html))
3. **Fix obvious errors before judging.** Step 4 of the judge post: "Instead of plowing ahead and building an LLM judge, you want to fix any obvious errors." The FAQ: "Focus automated evaluators on failures that persist after fixing your prompts." ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/), [automate FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/should-i-build-automated-evaluators-for-every-failure-mode-i-find.html))
4. **Cheap checks before expensive judges.** "Start with cheap code-based checks where possible: regex patterns, structural validation, or execution tests." The 2024 post's Level 1 unit tests come before Level 2 model evals: "conquer a good portion of your Level 1 tests before you move into model-based tests." ([automate FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/should-i-build-automated-evaluators-for-every-failure-mode-i-find.html), [evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/))
5. **Build the judge iteratively, one failure mode at a time.** Judge post step 5: "Start with Expert Examples... Keep Iterating on the Prompt Until Convergence With Domain Expert." Step 7 warns: "Don't jump directly to using specialized LLM judges until you have gone through this critique shadowing process." ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/))
6. **Only for problems you will revisit.** "Only build expensive evaluators for problems you'll iterate on repeatedly." A judge "require[s] 100+ labeled examples, ongoing weekly maintenance." ([automate FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/should-i-build-automated-evaluators-for-every-failure-mode-i-find.html))
7. **Keep looking at data after the judge exists.** "You can never stop looking at data—no free lunch exists." and "you must continue doing periodic exercises to monitor the model and human agreement." ([evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/))

The Field Guide's NurtureBoss case is the worked example of the order: read logs, "Categorized the types of date-handling failures", "Built specific tests to catch these issues", "Measured improvement on these metrics", date-handling success "improved from 33% to 95%." ([field-guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/))

## 2. What a trace contains and how he reads it

**Definition.** A trace is "the complete record of all actions, messages, tool calls, and data retrievals from a single initial user query" to the final response. It includes "every step across all agents, tools, and system components in a session." For multi-step workflows: "Log the entire workflow from initial trigger to final business outcome. Include LLM calls, tool usage, human approvals, and database writes." ([what is a trace](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/what-is-a-trace.html), [multi-step FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-do-i-evaluate-complex-multi-step-workflows.html))

**The custom-viewer argument.** "You must remove all friction from the process of looking at data. This means rendering your traces in domain-specific ways." ([evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/)) "Build a custom annotation tool. This is the single most impactful investment you can make for your AI evaluation workflow." Teams with custom tools "iterate ~10x faster"; "you can build a tailored interface in hours." ([custom tool FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/should-i-build-a-custom-annotation-tool-or-use-something-off-the-shelf.html)) The 2024 post gives the build cost as "Gradio, Streamlit, Panel, or Shiny in less than a day"; Rechat's viewer was Shiny for Python. ([evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/))

**What the viewer must show** (Field Guide list, verbatim heads): "Show all context in one place." "Make feedback trivial to capture. One-click correct/incorrect buttons beat lengthy forms." "Capture open-ended feedback." "Enable quick filtering and sorting." "Have hotkeys that allow users to navigate between data examples and annotate without clicking." ([field-guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/)) The FAQ adds: "Present the trace in a way that's intuitive for the domain" (emails rendered as emails, code with syntax highlighting, less critical detail collapsed); "Include progress indicators (e.g., 'Trace 45 of 100')"; "Enable hotkeys for navigating between traces (e.g., N for next)"; "Allow reviewers to filter traces by metadata or search by keywords"; "Surface traces flagged by guardrails, CI failures, or automated evaluators for review"; and the governing constraint, "Keep your annotation interface minimal." ([good interface FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/what-makes-a-good-custom-interface-for-reviewing-llm-outputs.html)) The Rechat viewer recorded per trace "What tool (feature) & scenario was being evaluated" and "Whether the trace resulted from a synthetic input or a real user input." ([evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/))

**Fallback.** "if you're just beginning, a spreadsheet is better than nothing." He aligned the Honeycomb judge by emailing "the following spreadsheet every few days" with "25-50 examples at a time." ([field-guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/), [evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/))

## 3. Rules for labels

**Binary, not scales.** "I've found that assigning scores or more granular ratings is more onerous to manage than binary ratings." ([evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/)) "If your evaluations consist of a bunch of metrics that LLMs score on a 1-5 scale... you're doing it wrong." "People don't know what to do with a 3 or 4." ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)) The FAQ's three reasons against Likert: "the difference between adjacent points (like 3 vs 4) is subjective and inconsistent across annotators"; "detecting statistical differences requires larger sample sizes"; "annotators often default to middle values to avoid making hard decisions." When something feels like a 3: "consider measuring specific sub-components with their own binary checks." "Numeric labels are advanced and usually not necessary." ([binary FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/why-do-you-recommend-binary-passfail-evaluations-instead-of-1-5-ratings-likert-scales.html)) Field Guide: "Binary decisions work best when paired with detailed critiques that capture the nuance of why something passed or failed." ([field-guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/))

**The written critique.** "The critique should be detailed enough so that you can use it in a few-shot prompt for a LLM judge." "detailed enough that a new employee could understand it. Being too terse is a common mistake." ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)) The 2024 post: "by collecting critiques from labelers explaining why they are making a decision, you can iterate on the evaluator model." ([evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/))

**One expert: the benevolent dictator.** "In most organizations there is usually one (maybe two) key individuals whose judgment is crucial." "Focusing on the principal expert ensures that evaluations are consistent and aligned with the most critical standards." "If you are an independent developer, you should be the domain expert (but be honest with yourself about your expertise)." ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)) FAQ: "Appointing a single domain expert as a 'benevolent dictator' is the most effective approach"; it "eliminates annotation conflicts and prevents the paralysis that comes from 'too many cooks in the kitchen.'" Multiple annotators are reserved for organisations "operating across multiple domains", measured with "Cohen's Kappa, which accounts for agreement beyond chance." ([annotators FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-many-people-should-annotate-my-llm-outputs.html))

**How many traces.** Judge post: "I start with around 30 examples and keep going until I do not see any new failure modes." FAQ: "Start with 100 diverse traces and annotate at least the first 30 yourself"; "We recommend reviewing at least 100 traces. Continue past 100 while you are still learning" until "theoretical saturation, meaning new reviews stop revealing failure modes." "A working pool of roughly 100 diverse traces is a useful guardrail." ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/), [how many FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-many-examples-do-i-need-for-an-eval.html), [error analysis FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/why-is-error-analysis-so-important-in-llm-evals-and-how-is-it-performed.html)) The Lenny's guest post says "Arm this expert with a representative set of around 100 user interactions." ([Lenny's](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/building-eval-systems-that-improve))

**Open coding then axial coding.** Open coding: annotators "write open-ended notes about issues"; "This process is akin to 'journaling' and is adapted from qualitative research." Axial coding: "Categorize the open-ended notes into a 'failure taxonomy'", "Group similar failures into distinct categories", "Count the number of failures in each category", "You can use an LLM to help with this step." ([error analysis FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/why-is-error-analysis-so-important-in-llm-evals-and-how-is-it-performed.html)) What an LLM may do: "After you've open coded 30–50 traces yourself, use an LLM to organize your raw failure notes." What stays human: "Always read through the raw traces yourself at the start"; "For any data used for testing/validating LLM-as-Judge evaluators, hand-validate each label." "Use LLMs to scale what you've learned, not to avoid looking at data." ([automation FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/what-parts-of-evals-can-be-automated-with-llms.html)) Field Guide's bottom-up framing: "open-ended notes on any undesired behavior. Then we used an LLM to build a taxonomy of common failure modes." ([field-guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/))

## 4. Validating a judge against human labels

**Metric.** "Raw agreement can be misleading when classes are imbalanced." "Treat the human labels as ground truth and report the judge's True Positive Rate and True Negative Rate separately." ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)) The 2024 post says the same with precision/recall: "using raw agreement is generally not recommended and can be misleading when classes are imbalanced." ([evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/)) "Focus on achieving high True Positive Rate (TPR) and True Negative Rate (TNR) with your judge on a held out labeled test set." ([same-model FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/can-i-use-the-same-model-for-both-the-main-task-and-evaluation.html))

**Split and sample sizes** (identical in the judge post and the FAQ): "A typical split uses 10 to 20 percent of the labeled examples for training, with 40 to 45 percent each for dev and test." "Aim for 30 to 50 examples of each label in each set when possible. Never place dev or test examples in the judge prompt." "Test set: Keep these examples hidden until you finish revising the prompt. Freeze the prompt, run the judge on the test set once." "Aim for about 100 examples per failure mode... Below 60 examples, the confidence intervals are often too wide to support a useful conclusion." FAQ: "Plan to label 100 to 200 examples for each failure mode." ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/), [how many FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-many-examples-do-i-need-for-an-eval.html))

**The loop.** Judge on dev set → "Inspect the errors, revise the prompt, and repeat. Dev set examples must stay out of the prompt." Honeycomb result: "It took us only three iterations to achieve > 90% agreement between the LLM and Phillip." The 2024 mechanism: a spreadsheet of model critique + model outcome beside "phillip_" columns, which "allowed me to iterate on the prompt of the critique model." Ongoing: "After bringing the model-based evaluator in line with the human, you must continue doing periodic exercises." ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/), [evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/)) Judge model choice: "start with the most capable models available to establish strong alignment with human judgments. You can optimize for cost later"; same model for task and judge "is usually fine." ([same-model FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/can-i-use-the-same-model-for-both-the-main-task-and-evaluation.html)) Failure conditions named: "The AI is overscoped... The process is not followed correctly... The expectations of alignment are unrealistic"; fallback is to "lean heavier on human annotations." ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/))

**Criteria drift (EvalGen).** Shankar, Zamfirescu-Pereira, Hartmann, Parameswaran, Arawjo, arXiv 2404.12272 (v1 2024-04-18, cs.HC): "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria." Some criteria are "dependent on the specific LLM outputs observed (rather than independent criteria that can be defined a priori)." Evidence (§7.3.1): "Participants wanted to add new criteria when they observed new 'types' of bad LLM outputs"; participants "reinterpret existing criteria to better fit the LLM's behavior" (P2/P8 relaxed "all entities are proper nouns" to "most"); participants revised earlier grades. Alignment procedure (§4.1): GPT-4 proposes binary criteria → candidate implementations as Python or grader prompts → user grades a sample with Good/Bad buttons → select "candidate assertion with highest alignment with user's grades," where alignment combines coverage ("ability to fail outputs that the user thinks are bad") and false failure rate ("ability to not fail outputs that the user thinks are good"), with a user-set FFR threshold (10% in the study, 40% on one pipeline). Study used "16 graded outputs per pipeline"; n = 9 practitioners; P2: "You should enforce that we all look at at least 20 examples first." Design implications (§8): "adjust criteria dynamically as user grades and gives feedback"; "As criteria changes over time, ask users to grade new LLM outputs... and adapt assertion sets"; pipeline changes cause drift, which "presume[s] settled, expert labels" against. ([arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272), [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12272v1)) The Field Guide restates it as Husain's own field observation: "it is impossible to completely determine evaluation criteria prior to human judging of LLM outputs," seen "firsthand when working with Phillip Carter at Honeycomb." ([field-guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/))

## 5. Multi-step and agentic pipelines

The three long posts say almost nothing here (the 2024 post: "Evaluating RAG is beyond the scope of this post"; the judge post shows one trace with function calls but judges the final response). The specific guidance lives in four FAQ entries.

- **Annotate the first upstream failure only.** "Focus on noting the first failure observed in a trace, as upstream errors can cause downstream issues." "Look at the entire trace and focus on the first upstream failure." "Annotate only the first failure in the trace at first. Downstream failures often cascade from the first issue." "Fixing the upstream failure can resolve the dependent ones." ([error analysis FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/why-is-error-analysis-so-important-in-llm-evals-and-how-is-it-performed.html), [multi-turn FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-do-i-debug-multi-turn-conversation-traces.html))
- **End-to-end first, then step-level.** "Treat the agent as a black box and decide whether it met the user's goal," with "a precise success rule per task" judged by humans or aligned judges; then step-level diagnostics on "tool selection, parameter extraction, error handling, context retention, efficiency metrics, and goal checkpoints." ([agentic FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-do-i-evaluate-agentic-workflows.html))
- **Transition failure matrix.** Rows are "the last successful state and columns represent where the first failure occurred." Worked example from Bryan Bischof's text-to-SQL agent: "GenSQL → ExecSQL transitions cause 12 failures while DecideTool → PlanCal causes only 2." "This reveals failure hotspots and guides where to invest debugging effort." ([agentic FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-do-i-evaluate-agentic-workflows.html), [multi-step FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-do-i-evaluate-complex-multi-step-workflows.html)) The Lenny's post repeats it: "Rows represent the last successful step, columns represent the step where failure occurred." ([Lenny's](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/building-eval-systems-that-improve))
- **Segment by stage; process before outcome; early stages first.** "Segment your error analysis by workflow stages. Early stage failures (understanding user input) differ from middle stage failures." "Process failures are often easier to debug since they're more deterministic, so tackle them first." Early-stage fixes matter most because "errors cascade in LLM chains." ([multi-step FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-do-i-evaluate-complex-multi-step-workflows.html))
- **Reproduce at the smallest step.** "When you find a failure, reproduce it with the simplest possible test case" (a 4-turn conversation reduced to a single turn); multi-turn tests are reserved for failures that need the context, and the matrix is invoked "when the conversation includes tools or several agents." ([multi-turn FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-do-i-debug-multi-turn-conversation-traces.html))
- **Tool calls.** Evaluate tool names, arguments, results and resulting state separately; "Use code assertions when the expected behavior is objective"; check authorization and preconditions. ([agentic FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-do-i-evaluate-agentic-workflows.html))
- **RAG as the model of component-wise evaluation.** "The retrieval component is a search problem," measured with "Recall@k", "Precision@k", "MRR"; generation is judged separately by error analysis. The retrieval set is built by "taking documents from your corpus, extracting key facts, then generating questions those facts would answer." ([RAG FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-should-i-approach-evaluating-my-rag-system.html))

## 6. Hosted and off-the-shelf tools versus hand-built

- "Keep it simple. Don't buy fancy LLM tools. Use what you have first." "Don't rely on generic evaluation frameworks to measure the quality of your AI." ([evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/))
- "Generic metrics are worse than useless – they actively impede progress in two ways" (false sense of progress; fragmented attention). "Every domain has unique needs that off-the-shelf tools rarely address." ([field-guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/))
- Ready-made metrics: "No. Generic evaluations waste time and create false confidence when you use them as quality measures." Permitted exception: "Experienced practitioners may use generic metrics as exploration signals." ([ready-to-use FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/should-i-use-ready-to-use-evaluation-metrics.html)) "Generic metrics like BERTScore, ROUGE are not useful for evaluating LLM outputs in most AI applications." ([similarity FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/are-similarity-metrics-bertscore-rouge-etc-useful-for-evaluating-llm-outputs.html))
- Off-the-shelf judges: "Nothing is strictly wrong with them... many people are led astray by them." "This is why I hate off the shelf metrics that come with many evaluation frameworks." ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/))
- Vendors: "I have no favorite vendor. Their features are very similar—I often build custom tools on top of them." Names "Langsmith, Arize and Braintrust"; choose by "who can offer the best support, as opposed to purely features." No mention of local-only or self-hosted data constraints on that page. ([vendor FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/whats-your-favorite-eval-vendor.html))
- Annotation tooling: build custom; off-the-shelf "may be justified when you need to coordinate dozens of distributed annotators with enterprise access controls," and even then "many teams find the configuration overhead and limitations aren't worth it." ([custom tool FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/should-i-build-a-custom-annotation-tool-or-use-something-off-the-shelf.html))
- Notebook as the minimum: "a notebook to review traces and analyze data, or build your own custom annotation interface," so you can "write arbitrary code, visualize data, and iterate quickly." ([MVE FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/whats-a-minimum-viable-evaluation-setup.html)) The 2024 post's Rechat stack used LangSmith for logging plus a hand-built Shiny viewer on top; the Field Guide shows Arize Phoenix, LangSmith and Braintrust playgrounds while preferring integrated in-house prompt environments. ([evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/), [field-guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/))

## 7. What would not transfer to one expert, no users, long local artifacts

Stated plainly, the following pieces of the method presuppose things the studios do not have.

- **Level 3 A/B testing and user-behavior outcomes.** "it is always good to perform A/B tests to ensure your AI product is driving user behaviors or outcomes you desire." Husain himself defers it until "convinced that your AI product is suitable for showing to real users." There are no users to show. ([evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/))
- **User-feedback as a sampling signal.** The sampling FAQ lists "Feedback: Selects traces with negative user feedback" (with its own caveat that it "misses problems that users do not report"); the Lenny's post samples "Traces that have negative user feedback." The surfacing FAQ is titled "beyond user feedback" and its remaining signals (random sample, stress-test queries, existing evals, outlier detection) do not need users. ([sampling FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-can-i-efficiently-sample-production-traces-for-review.html), [surfacing FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-do-i-surface-problematic-traces-for-review-beyond-user-feedback.html))
- **Production sampling and asynchronous production judges.** "you can sample live traces and run evaluators against them asynchronously"; "track confidence intervals for production metrics." The CI/CD half of that entry (small purpose-built sets, "Favor assertions or other deterministic checks over LLM-as-judge evaluators") assumes no traffic. ([CI/CD FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-are-evaluations-used-differently-in-cicd-vs-monitoring-production.html))
- **Cadences sized to traffic.** "at least 100+ fresh traces each review cycle," cycles of "2-4 weeks," "Review 10-20 traces weekly," triggers "after incidents, user complaint spikes, or metric drift," and "Scaling usage introduces new edge cases." An engagement yields ten to thirty invocations, so 100 fresh traces per cycle is several engagements' worth. ([re-run FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-often-should-i-re-run-error-analysis-on-my-production-system.html))
- **Judge validation sample sizes.** "30 to 50 examples of each label in each set," "100 to 200 examples for each failure mode," "Below 60 examples, the confidence intervals are often too wide." At one long artifact per seat invocation, these counts are per failure mode, not per engagement, and Husain gives no smaller-N variant. ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/), [how many FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-many-examples-do-i-need-for-an-eval.html))
- **Multi-annotator machinery.** Cohen's Kappa, independent labeling before discussion, rubric arbitration: all scoped to "Larger organizations or those operating across multiple domains." The single-expert case is his default, not an exception. ([annotators FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/how-many-people-should-annotate-my-llm-outputs.html))
- **Trace shape.** His trace definition starts "from a single initial user query" and every viewer example (emails, CRM queries, SQL, chat turns) renders short conversational units; he gives no guidance on reading a multi-thousand-word artifact as one trace, nor on seats that hand full prior artifacts forward rather than tool results. ([what is a trace](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/what-is-a-trace.html))
- **The independent-developer clause.** "If you are an independent developer, you should be the domain expert (but be honest with yourself about your expertise)," and such teams should "regularly validate their assumptions against real user feedback." The second half has no counterpart here. ([llm-judge](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/))
- **Synthetic-data bootstrap.** The no-data case he addresses ("What if we don't have any data yet?") is solved by generating synthetic *inputs* across features, scenarios and personas; the studios' scarcity is in outputs and engagements, not inputs. ([field-guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/field-guide/), [evals](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals/))
