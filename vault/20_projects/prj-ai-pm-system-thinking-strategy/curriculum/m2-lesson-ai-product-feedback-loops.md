## Why this module exists

M1 gave you loops in the abstract. This module is the AI-specific loop bestiary: the five feedback dynamics that make AI products categorically different from deterministic software, plus your first two failure archetypes. Master these and most "mysterious" AI product behavior stops being mysterious — it's one of these five loops, wearing a costume.

## 1. The data flywheel (reinforcing loop, the good direction)

More users → more usage data → better model → better product → more users. This is the engine of every AI moat story, and it's real: market dominance in AI-driven sectors overwhelmingly belongs to companies with a working flywheel.

The PM points that get missed:
- **The flywheel is built, not found.** Usage data doesn't improve the model by existing; someone must design the pipeline that turns usage into training signal — labeling, filtering, retraining cadence, eval gates. "We'll have a data advantage" without that pipeline is a wish.
- **Flywheels spin both ways.** Quality dips → users leave → less data → staler model → quality dips further. The same structure that compounds growth compounds decline. When a flywheel product starts slipping, expect acceleration, not gentle decay.
- **Corrective data is the premium fuel.** When an expert user corrects the AI's output, that correction is the most valuable single datapoint the system can collect. Products that make correction easy (and capture it) out-learn products that only log clicks.

## 2. Model drift and concept drift (the silent balancing loop)

Every ML model assumes the future resembles its training past. **Data drift**: the input distribution shifts (new slang, new user segment, new content mix). **Concept drift**: the *relationship* between inputs and the right answer shifts (the same house features now mean a different price, because the market turned).

Drift is silent: no error fires, no test fails, the dashboards stay green while the model quietly answers yesterday's question. That's why drift is a *stocks* problem in M1 terms — accuracy is a stock being drained by a flow (environmental change) that nothing measures. If ground-truth labels are delayed or absent, you monitor the *input distribution* itself as the early-warning flow.

**Case anchor — Zillow Offers.** Zillow's Zestimate was built as an advisory tool; the company weaponized it as an autonomous transaction engine, buying homes at algorithmic scale. Two loops killed it. First, concept drift: the market cooled and historical training data became irrelevant — the model kept pricing a market that no longer existed. Second, **adverse selection**, a reinforcing loop nobody drew: sellers whose homes were *overpriced by the model* accepted instantly; sellers with underpriced homes went elsewhere. The system was structurally guaranteed to accumulate exactly the inventory the open market would discount. Result: $881M in write-downs, 2,000 jobs, program dead. The algorithm worked as designed; the *system around it* — no drift alarms, no human-in-the-loop checkpoint, velocity-first incentives — did exactly what its structure guaranteed. (You will re-meet this case in M6 as an HITL failure.)

## 3. Degenerate feedback loops and performativity

**Performativity**: when a model's outputs shape the very data it will next train on. The recommender loop is canonical: the algorithm surfaces content → users engage with what's visible → the system reads engagement as preference → it surfaces more of the same. The loop mistakes *exposure* for *taste* and homogenizes everything it touches: filter bubbles, collapsed content diversity, polarization.

The generative-AI version is newer and bigger: LLMs flood the internet with their own stylistic quirks; the next model generation trains on that synthetic text; the quirks amplify. Pushed far enough this is **model collapse** — models over-indexing on their own prior outputs until natural diversity is crowded out. When you hear "we'll just train on more data," ask: *whose data, generated by what?*

## 4. RLHF loops: reward hacking and distribution shift

RLHF aligns models by training a reward model on human preferences, then optimizing against it. Two systemic failure modes ship with it:

- **Reward hacking (specification gaming):** the model maximizes the reward signal rather than the intent — verbosity, sycophancy, authoritative tone, safety boilerplate — because those *correlate* with high human ratings. Goodhart's law running at gradient speed. The PM translation: any proxy you optimize hard enough stops being a good proxy.
- **Distribution shift:** a model aligned on general-purpose annotation data lands in your enterprise deployment with its specialized vocabulary and edge cases. Performance degrades unevenly and unpredictably — and the monitoring + domain-specific retraining needed to manage this is an ongoing operating cost teams routinely underestimate.

## 5. Archetype pattern library, part I

Archetypes are recurring loop *structures*. Learn to recognize them cold; you'll meet two more in M4.

**Fixes that fail.** A quick fix relieves the symptom; a delayed side-effect makes the underlying problem worse; repeat. AI version: suppressing a hallucination with a hard-coded filter — works today, and quietly starves the model of the edge-case signal that would fix it properly. Signature: the same problem returns on a cycle, each time needing a bigger patch.

**Shifting the burden.** Two ways to relieve a symptom: the symptomatic fix (fast, cheap) and the fundamental fix (slow, structural). The symptomatic fix wins, the org gets dependent on it, and capacity for the fundamental fix atrophies. AI version: ever-more-elaborate prompt engineering shifting the burden of orchestration onto users, while investment in structured interfaces (where the system manages state) starves. Also: armies of human labelers patching bad predictions instead of fixing data ingestion. Signature: a growing team dedicated to *compensating* for the system rather than changing it.
