# The Confident Stranger Wearing My Face

"Every rep is a vote for the person you're becoming."

I read that twice and typed one word back at the machine that wrote it. GROSS.

It was supposed to be me. I'd asked a model to draft a few lines in my voice, and that is what came back: smooth, certain, swollen with belief. A LinkedIn cathedral built in my name while I wasn't looking. Every word spelled right. The grammar immaculate. And the guy inside it was a total stranger, the kind who treats a bad week like inspirational stationery and means it. It nailed every word and got me completely wrong, which is somehow more insulting than if it had just been bad.

Why was I letting a machine audition for my voice at all? Because I'd finally run out of excuses.

I'd been promising myself I'd start the Substack for years, the way you promise you'll learn guitar, or call your grandmother more, or stop buying the family-size Chips Ahoy and eating it by the sleeve. Then, a week ago, I got laid off.

I won't dress it up. Getting laid off is a quiet, specific kind of bad. You keep the same body and the same apartment and the same girlfriend, Mary, who handles it better than I do, and the only thing that changes is that the calendar that used to own you suddenly belongs to you. Every empty hour of it, just sitting there, asking what you're going to do now. For a few days I didn't have an answer. Then I did: I'd finally write the thing I'd been dodging. I sat down with more feeling in my chest than I'd had in months, ready to put my actual voice on a page. And the machine handed me a stranger with a laminated dream journal.

I could have shipped him. People do it every day. The whole internet is wallpapered with confident strangers wearing somebody else's face.

My first instinct was to give the machine more rules. If it didn't know me, I'd just tell it. Be self-deprecating. Use pop culture. Keep the closers short and punchy. I wrote it a little instruction manual for being Sean, the way you'd brief a stand-in before a stunt.

It got worse. The next draft was more confident and more wrong, a stranger who'd now read my notes and was doing a full impression. Tell a machine to "be self-deprecating" and it performs humility, which is the least humble thing on earth. Describing your own voice, it turns out, is like describing a smell. You can pile up adjectives all afternoon and the person on the other end still can't tell if you mean a bakery or a fire. Every rule I added sharpened the impression and blurred the man. You cannot spec your way to a self.

So I stopped describing.

Here's what I'd had backwards the whole time. I kept trying to hand the machine a tidy summary it could distill me from, and you cannot distill a person out of a description of the person. You have to give it the raw material, the actual evidence, the stuff it could never guess on its own. Stop telling it what you sound like. Start showing it what you can't stand.

That GROSS at the top? That was the entire method. I just didn't know it yet.

So I built the machine a gauntlet. Ten sentences, all deliberately rancid, the exact LinkedIn poetry it kept reaching for, and I reacted to each one like a judge on a cooking show I never agreed to host. Not analyzing. Reacting, fast and mean, before politeness could get involved. "Getting laid off wasn't an ending. It was an invitation to begin." No. "New city, new chapter, same dream." Nope: different city, different chapter, same shit. The negative space did what a thousand positive rules couldn't. Every single "no" drew a sharper outline around the real guy than any description of him ever could. I have no clue how to describe my own voice. I can smell what isn't it from across the room.

But a gauntlet only tells you what you're not, and knowing what disgusts you is a start, not a personality. So I gave the machine two more things, and this is where it finally quit guessing.

First, I inventoried what I actually love. Not the impressive version. The real one. Left to guess, it had me pegged as a man of prestige drama and refined taste. The honest inventory is Guy Fieri, that beautiful toolbag, driving through Flavortown handing out donkey sauce to the masses. It's watching Chopped mainly for the sob stories and clocking how many absolute maniacs are cooking my dinner at nice restaurants. It's a CPU-only round of Super Smash Bros with a few bucks riding on which computer-controlled character wins, all of us drinking while they beat the hell out of each other. The machine guessed Sunday-night prestige. I had to tell it I think the Ace of Cakes guy is genuinely funny.

Second, I went looking for evidence that predated every machine. I wrote eleven screenplays in my twenties, long before any of this, and I fed them in whole. Buried in there is a kids'-party mascot named Petey Possum getting stabbed on the way to a birthday gig, and a woman "covered in vomit stains and regret," and a few hundred other lines that prove I had a voice years before a model offered to lend me one. It was never going to learn me from rules. It was going to learn me from the receipts.

And then it worked.

Same prompt as day one, the one that coughed up the confident stranger. This time it stopped inventing a guy and started quoting the one in the evidence. The references came from my actual life instead of a stock photo of one. The jokes broke where I break them. It wasn't doing an impression anymore. It was handing me sentences I recognized, like finding your own handwriting in a notebook you forgot you kept. It got me wrong the first time because I'd asked it to guess instead of giving it a single real thing to go on. The draft you're reading came out of that fixed machine, receipts and all. It didn't learn my voice. I just stopped describing the smell and handed it the bakery.

You don't need my stack for any of this. The method is free and it's yours and you can run the whole thing this weekend. Write your own gauntlet: ten sentences in the cheese you hate, reacted to fast, before your brain turns diplomatic. Inventory what you actually love, not the bio version. Then dig up something you made before AI existed, an old notebook, a zine, the texts you sent your friends in 2014, and feed it the real you instead of a description of the real you. The machine will stop guessing. It'll start quoting you back to yourself, which is a deeply strange thing to read the first time. Try not to find it as comforting as I did.
