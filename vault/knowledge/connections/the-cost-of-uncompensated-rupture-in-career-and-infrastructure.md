## Synthesis

Both Gary’s career rupture and Sean’s potential agent/job-hunt failures share a common mechanism: the assumption that removing a problematic element (a job, a buggy agent) solves the underlying tension without addressing the dependency it served. Gary quits to reclaim family time, but because he has no 'Work-as-Done' plan for income or status, the rupture creates immediate poverty and domestic violence. Similarly, Sean’s tendency to view agent failures as mere technical glitches ignores the 'Work-as-Imagined' narrative that keeps his job hunt moving; if he 'quits' an agent without a robust fallback, he inherits the same structural debt Gary did. The consequence is that binary ruptures are only sustainable when the underlying value chain has already been decoupled from the failed element.

## Implications

- Sean must audit his 'rupture points' (agent failures, job rejections) to ensure he has a viable 'Work-as-Done' alternative before executing binary actions like firing an agent or quitting a role.
- The 'Fredrickson case' pattern in Sean’s job hunt—constantly deferring the real work of application strategy behind the narrative of 'preparation'—is a direct precursor to the same kind of catastrophic rupture Gary experienced.
