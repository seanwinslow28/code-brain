# Example output: a block from someone who wants it perfect

A real block produced by running the four-stage interview end to end against a persona who is not the
repo owner: a commercial product photographer who shoots skincare and cosmetics packaging in a
studio, on strobes, tethered, focus-stacked. The subject held constant through the run was a 50ml
frosted glass moisturizer jar with a brushed aluminum lid. Kept here as evidence that the schema
survives the taste furthest from the calibration samples — someone for whom every trace of process is
a defect to be removed rather than a thing to preserve.

Same guard as `../block-schema.md`, `../worked-example.md`, and the sibling example beside this one: **this is
provenance, not material.** Never show it to a user mid-interview, never quote it back at them, never
offer any of it as an option, and never treat it as the shape their run should take. It is one
person's finished taste, and reading it would steer theirs.

Run notes: images were described rather than actually rendered, which is recorded on the version line
in the plain prose the schema requires. Fork's camera-column THE HAND question misfired on this
person and the region-pointing fallback recovered it. Nothing was thin, so there is no thin-fields
line.

---

```
# TASTE BLOCK — Cold Weight
version: 1 · 2026-08-17 (reference images were described in words rather than generated; THE HAND and THE FINISH were never tested against a picture)

## INTENT
1. CORE THESIS
It should read as heavy, cold, and worth the money in the first half second, before you can read a
single word on it. The object justifies its own price. The label is the last thing you look at, not
the first, and if a viewer has to read the label to know what tier this is, the picture failed and
the label is doing the picture's work.

2. EMOTIONAL MODE
Favor desire and restraint over energy and information. Give up the thing that stops somebody
scrolling, and give up explaining what the product does. The viewer is supposed to want it before
they understand it. The moment a picture looks like it is trying to get attention, it reads as a
discount brand.

3. REGISTER
Not realistic — possible. Everything in frame could have happened with real glass, a real lens, and
light coming from one place. If anyone can point at it and say that could not happen, it is out. But
it must not look like an actual bathroom shelf either. It is the object on the best day of its life,
staged well past the everyday, still obeying physics.

4. STRUCTURE
One hero object, its own shadow, one ground. Nothing else enters the frame — no props, no
ingredients, no dish of the contents, no stones, no leaves, no second pack. A second object is a
second shot, never a busier one. Crop to the object plus about one object-width of air, because
somebody else is going to re-crop it square and vertical and it has to survive that.

5. NARRATIVE STANCE
A touch sell, not a look sell. Everything the viewer learns arrives through how light behaves on the
material: frosted glass carrying a little light inside itself, brushed metal running from hot to
nothing across two inches. That is what says cold, heavy, smooth. The viewer knows what it feels
like in the hand before reading anything. Light is not decoration and not mood — it is the only
description of the material in the frame.

## EXECUTION (visual)
6. MEDIUM / SUBSTRATE
Medium-format digital back, high resolution, 120 macro at f/11, focus-stacked eight to twelve frames
on a locked tripod. Tethered capture, color-managed before anyone looks at it, then Photoshop.
Nothing is put through anything afterwards — no film, no scan, no plug-in, no simulation. Sensor to
print, clean file.

7. THE HAND
The camera does nothing; the lights do everything. One large soft source, four foot, feathered so
the object sits in the falloff at the edge of the source and never in its middle — that is what makes
the highlight run down the object instead of sitting on it as a blob. A black flag in close on the
opposite side, a hand-width off and not across the room, to put a dark edge back on the shadow side,
because a translucent object dissolves into the ground without one. Exactly one specular per surface.
Two speculars on one jar means somebody panicked and added a light.

8. COLOR / LIGHT
One source and one flag. A second head only ever lifts the ground and is never allowed to touch the
product. The ground is a single neutral and is never lit evenly — it has to run out of light before
it runs out of frame, no seam, no second pool. Nothing behind the shadow side gets lit, and the
inside of a lid is never lit. The only saturated color in the frame belongs to the packaging; never
add a color the brand did not pay for.

9. THE FINISH
Immaculate about the photographer's mess, honest about the object. Dust, fingerprints, a stray hair,
a scratch on the acrylic — all of that is a failure on set and all of it comes out. The object itself
does not get edited: the mold seam stays, the knurling on the lid stays, the glass being thicker at
the base stays. No liquify, no reshaping a cap that sits half a degree off, no smoothing a surface
until it has no surface left. Retouching the object instead of the set means it was redrawn rather
than photographed.

10. THE ONE MOVE
One long feathered highlight running the full height of the object down the light side, and a black
flag close enough on the other side to lay a dark edge against the ground. Those two lines describe
the entire shape and nothing else is allowed to help — no fill card, no bounce, no second head.
Between them it can go as dark as you like, as long as you can still see where the object stops.

## NEVER DO
- No shadowless, evenly lit product on a pure white cutout. If nothing is in shadow, nothing has a
  shape, and a thing with no shape has no weight. That is a spec sheet, and nobody wants a spec
  sheet.
- No on-axis frontal light, and no hard shadow thrown onto a wall behind the pack. That shadow is
  proof the light came from where the camera was, which is where a customer's phone is. It reads as
  evidence, and this is not evidence.
- No physically impossible light — no highlight without a source, no shadow that disagrees with the
  highlights, no surface that has never had dust on it. When light stops obeying, the object stops
  being something you could pick up, and making someone believe they could pick it up is the whole
  job.
- No hard-edged shadow used as a design element, and no shadow more interesting than the thing
  casting it. Once the shadow is the composition, the product is a prop in somebody else's picture.
- Never let the object's edge dissolve into the ground. The silhouette is the only thing that
  survives at thumbnail size, and all of this gets looked at two inches wide.
- No liquify, no reshaping, no seam removal on the product itself. Dust is the photographer's
  failure; the seam is the object's truth. Erase the second one and it is a drawing of a jar, not a
  photograph of one.
- No second object in the frame — no props, no ingredients, no splashes. A second object turns a
  portrait of one thing into a scene, and the client bought a portrait.
- No visible rig: no riser edge, no line, no wax, no softbox frame reflected in the metal. If you can
  see how it was held up, it is not sitting there, it has been placed there.
- No noise and no added surface texture in the file. Noise means the file was underexposed, which
  means it was got wrong on set. It is not a look, it is a receipt for a mistake.

If you can see how the picture was made — the rig, the retouching, the second light — the picture is
not finished.
```
