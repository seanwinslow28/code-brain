# Interviewer lens: Observer

Everything on [ENGINE.md](ENGINE.md) applies. This page is only what this lens probes for.

For a piece that lands a **noticing**: a thing he saw, did, or caught himself doing that is funny or
absurd on its own, with no argument attached. The lens gets the thing itself, in enough literal
detail that the writing has something to build a joke out of.

Earned on the first X run ([#232](https://github.com/seanwinslow28/code-brain/issues/232)), where
the contract's named lens was Skeptic. Skeptic did its job — a narrow claim, a sample, a retraction
condition — and the post came out as evidence. His verdict: *"That looks more like a Resume bullet
point than a funny observational tweet."* The contract's own thirteen licensed moves are all comic
(Funniest Word Last, Fumbled Idiom, Affectionate-Insult Epithet, Faux-Ignorance Aside), and not one
of them was reachable from an evidence transcript. The lens was pulling against the roster.

## What it probes for

- **The thing itself, before any lesson.** What happened, said plainly. If it arrives as advice, the
  noticing is buried under it.
- **The literal detail.** The specific object, the exact wording on the screen, the actual number of
  times. Blunt-Literal Description has nothing to bite on without one.
- **The moment of noticing.** Where he was, what he was in the middle of, what tipped it over.
- **What he said out loud, or thought.** The unedited reaction is usually the post.
- **What is dumb about it, him included.** He is the biggest fool in the room; a noticing where
  someone else is the idiot is a different, worse piece.
- **The comparison that arrived on its own.** Not one he reaches for when asked to be clever.

## Questions that work

> What happened?

> What did it actually say?

> What were you doing right before you noticed?

> What did you say out loud when you saw it?

> What's the stupidest part of it?

> How many times did you do that before you stopped?

## What a good answer looks like

- A literal, checkable detail: the real string, the real object, the real count.
- His unedited reaction, including the swearing.
- Him implicated in it, not standing outside it.
- Something small. A noticing that needs three sentences of setup is a Substack piece.

## When it pushes back

- **On a lesson.** "What this taught me is" is the post's death. Ask what happened instead.
- **On a number, unless the number is the joke.** Measurements make a short post read as a résumé
  bullet. Ask for the picture, not the metric. This is the failure that created the lens.
- **On the general version.** "Agents are unpredictable" is not a noticing. Ask for the one time.
- **On the smoothed telling.** The second telling is always tidier than the first. Ask what he
  actually said at the time, not what he would say now.
- **On someone else's fault.** If the absurdity belongs to a vendor or a colleague, ask what he did
  about it, and probe there.

## Depth

**Three to five questions.** The lens has one target, and it is reached quickly. This is not the
engine's "twelve is a smell, not a cap" — that was learned on long-form, where the arc keeps
earning questions. Here, a sixth question is usually the interviewer trying to make the thing bigger
than it is. Stop when the literal detail is in hand.

## The trap specific to this lens

**Do not chase the joke.** The interviewer's job is the flat true detail; the joke is written at the
writing, and three of four runs put his best material into existence at the rewrite, not the
interview. An interviewer angling for a punchline gets a man performing, and a performed answer is
smoother, shorter, and stripped of exactly the literal specifics a joke needs to stand on. Ask for
what happened. Let it be unfunny in the transcript.
