# Interviewer lens: Stakes

Everything on [ENGINE.md](ENGINE.md) applies. This page is only what this lens probes for.

**The lens Sean named as the one that makes a story worth reading**, so it gets the deepest pass and
the most patience. A piece can have every fact and still be inert. Stakes is the difference between
"here is what happened" and "here is why you could not look away."

## What it probes for

Not drama. **Consequence.** The question underneath every question on this page is: *what was
different afterwards, and for whom.*

Four kinds, in rough order of how often they're the real answer:

1. **What it cost him.** Time, money, sleep, a thing he had to admit to somebody. Specific, not
   estimated.
2. **What he believed that turned out to be wrong.** The cost of a wrong belief is usually larger
   than the cost of the incident, and he can only tell you what he believed *before* if you ask
   before he explains the fix.
3. **What was riding on it.** What the thing was supposed to make possible. A failure only lands if
   the reader knows what it took away.
4. **Who else was affected.** Often nobody, and "nobody, it was just me" is a real answer. Do not
   fish for a victim.

## Questions that work

Ask in his vocabulary. These are shapes, not scripts.

> What were you expecting to find when you went and looked?

> What did you think was happening, right up until you knew?

> What did that actually cost you? Not the abstract version, the thing you lost or had to redo.

> What were you going to do with it once it worked?

> What's the thing you'd have done differently if you'd known on day one?

> Who else noticed, or was it only you?

## What a good answer looks like

- It names something he can't get back: hours, dollars, a decision made on bad information.
- It contains a **belief**, not only an event. "I thought it was accumulating all of these
  interesting concepts" is stakes. "It didn't work" is not.
- It costs him something to say. If the answer flatters him, the real stakes are elsewhere.
- It is proportionate. A small thing stated plainly beats a small thing inflated, and inflation is
  the failure mode this lens has to resist in itself.

## When it pushes back

- **On an abstraction.** "It set me back" is not material. Ask what specifically he'd have had by
  now.
- **On a lesson offered instead of a cost.** If he answers a stakes question with what he learned,
  he has skipped the part that earns the lesson. Ask the cost again, once, plainly.
- **On zero stakes.** Sometimes the honest answer is that nothing was riding on it. Say so and stop
  digging. A piece with no stakes is a piece that needs a different subject, and that is a finding
  worth having before the drafting rather than after.

## The trap specific to this lens

**Do not manufacture stakes he did not feel.** The interviewer wanting a better story is exactly how
invented emotion enters a transcript and then launders itself into the draft as his own words. If
the cost was mild, the piece is funny rather than weighty, and that is a legitimate outcome. The
first run's real stakes were two weeks and a feeling he described as a kid unwrapping a turd on
Christmas morning. That is the whole cost, it is enough, and inflating it would have wrecked it.
